1 General Equilibrium

Walrasian competitive equilibrium in exchange and production economies. The core machinery: budget constraints, market clearing, Walras' Law, excess demand, existence and uniqueness of equilibrium.

Source: FHSMicroWk1 Lectures 1 to 3 (Lecture_1_GE_Intro, Lecture_2_Trade, Lecture_3_Finding_GE). Textbook: Varian Intermediate Ch 31 to 33.

Question Types: General Equilibrium and Welfare

All Core Microeconomics finals questions on general equilibrium, welfare economics and social choice from 2014 to 2025, organised by Part A short problem, Part B essay, and Part B long-answer problem. Maps to Topics 1 and 2 in the 2025 reading list.
Exam rubric: Part A is ~4 compulsory short problems worth 40 percent total. Part B has ~6 long questions of which candidates pick 2, worth 60 percent. One Part B question is mainly technical; others are essay-style or structured.
Excluded sub-topics

None identified, but verify against current Micro2025 syllabus. The 2014 to 2020 sample is biased towards Q1 (Part A) and Q10/Q11 (one Part B). Older syllabi may have included formal Arrow's impossibility proofs or core convergence in larger replica economies, which do not appear in the recent set.

Part A: short compulsory problems

The typical Part A GE question is an exchange or production economy where you compute the competitive equilibrium, check Pareto efficiency, and discuss policy. Almost every year features one. The questions split between (i) production economies with firms and households (2014, 2017, 2018, 2019) and (ii) pure exchange economies in an Edgeworth box (2015, 2020, 2022, 2024, 2025).

Sub-question Type A.1: Compute competitive equilibrium in a pure exchange economy
  • What it asks: Given endowments $\omega_A, \omega_B$ and utilities (usually Cobb-Douglas or quasi-linear), find the price ratio $p_x / p_y$ and the equilibrium allocation $(x_A^, y_A^, x_B^, y_B^)$.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Normalise: set $p_y = 1$ and write $p = p_x$. With two goods Walras' Law makes one market redundant, so only one price needs solving.
  2. Write each agent's wealth: $m_A = p \omega_{Ax} + \omega_{Ay}$. Same for B.
  3. Get Marshallian demands. For Cobb-Douglas $u = x^\alpha y^{1-\alpha}$ the demands are $x^ = \alpha m / p_x$ and $y^ = (1-\alpha) m / p_y$. Memorise this, it appears every year.
  4. Impose market clearing for good x: $x_A^(p) + x_B^(p) = \omega_{Ax} + \omega_{Bx}$.
  5. Solve for p, then plug back to get allocations. Check the y-market clears as a sanity check on Walras.
  6. For quasi-linear $u = v(x) + y$ (2025 Q1), demand for x is determined by $v'(x) = p$ alone (income effect on x is zero), so $x^ = (v')^{-1}(p)$. The numeraire good y absorbs all income variation. Equilibrium x comes from $\sum x_i^(p) = \sum \omega_{ix}$.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q1, 2020 Q1, 2022 Q1, 2024 Q1, 2025 Q1.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium, Concepts/Walras Law, Concepts/Cobb-Douglas Demand, Concepts/Quasi-linear Utility, Concepts/Excess Demand.
Sub-question Type A.2: Compute competitive equilibrium in a production economy
  • What it asks: Firms use labour $L$ to produce output $Y$ via a technology like $Y = 2AL^{1/2}$ or Cobb-Douglas $Y = A K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$; households choose consumption and labour supply with utility over $c$ and leisure. Find the equilibrium real wage $w/p$, employment, and output.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Firm side. Profit max gives $p \cdot MPL = w$, so $MPL = w/p$. For $Y = 2AL^{1/2}$, $MPL = A L^{-1/2}$, giving labour demand $L^d = (A p / w)^2$ per firm.
  2. Aggregate firm demand and supply. With $N$ firms, total $L^d = N (Ap/w)^2$ and total $Y^s = N \cdot 2A L^{1/2} = 2 N A^2 p / w$.
  3. Household side. With log utility over $c$ and leisure $\ell$, $u = \alpha \log c + (1-\alpha) \log \ell$ and time endowment $T = 1$. Budget is $p c = w (1 - \ell) + \pi$ where $\pi$ is profit income. Cobb-Douglas gives $c^ = \alpha (w + \pi) / p$, leisure $\ell^ = (1-\alpha)(w + \pi)/w$.
  4. Aggregate household demand. With $M$ households, total $c^d = M c^$, total labour supply $L^s = M (1 - \ell^)$.
  5. Impose market clearing. Either labour ($L^d = L^s$) or goods ($Y^s = c^d$); Walras gives the other for free. Solve for $w/p$.
  6. Compare with planner's problem. By the first welfare theorem the competitive allocation maximises a representative household's utility subject to feasibility, so it should equal the direct planner's choice in 2017 Q1.
  • Appeared in: 2014 Q1, 2017 Q1, 2018 Q1, 2019 Q1.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium, Concepts/First Welfare Theorem, Concepts/Cobb-Douglas Demand, Concepts/PPF and MRT.
Sub-question Type A.3: Verify Walras' Law and use it to skip a market
  • What it asks: Show $\sum_g p_g z_g(p) = 0$ for all $p$, where $z_g$ is aggregate excess demand for good $g$. Then conclude that if $K-1$ markets clear, the $K$th does too.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Start from individual budget constraints: $\sum_g p_g x_{ig}(p) = \sum_g p_g \omega_{ig}$ for each agent $i$. This is an identity from budget exhaustion (Walras' Law on the individual).
  2. Subtract endowment from both sides: $\sum_g p_g (x_{ig} - \omega_{ig}) = 0$, so $\sum_g p_g z_{ig} = 0$ for each $i$.
  3. Sum over agents: $\sum_i \sum_g p_g z_{ig} = \sum_g p_g z_g(p) = 0$. Done.
  4. Application: in 2014 Q1 the question gives you goods and labour markets and asks you to verify the labour market clears using Walras given the goods market clears. Just compute aggregate value of excess demand in the cleared market and use Walras to back out the other.
  • Appeared in: 2014 Q1(e), 2022 Q1(a), 2025 Q1(a).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Walras Law, Concepts/Excess Demand.
Sub-question Type A.4: Verify Pareto efficiency of an allocation
  • What it asks: Given an allocation, prove it is (or is not) Pareto efficient. Either invoke MRS equalisation or construct a Pareto-improving reallocation.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Recall the interior PE condition for a two-good two-agent exchange economy: $MRS_A = MRS_B$ together with feasibility. With Cobb-Douglas $u_A = x_A^\alpha y_A^{1-\alpha}$, $MRS_A = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \cdot \frac{y_A}{x_A}$.
  2. Plug in the candidate allocation and check equality. For 2024 Q1 with symmetric $\alpha = 1/2$ at $(12, 12; 12, 12)$, both MRSs equal 1, so PE holds.
  3. If MRSs are unequal, propose a small trade: the agent with the higher MRS for $x$ relative to $y$ buys $x$ from the other. Show both utilities rise.
  4. For corner cases (one agent has zero of a good), check Kuhn-Tucker style: PE only requires that no Pareto-improving feasible deviation exists. Leontief preferences (2022 Q1) create kinks where MRS is a set, not a number, and the PE locus thickens; check feasibility along the kink.
  5. For multi-good economies (2019 Q10), the condition generalises to $MRS_{xy}^A = MRS_{xy}^B$ for every pair of goods $(x, y)$. Equivalently, at the allocation, find prices $p$ such that each agent's bundle solves their own utility max at those prices (this is the second welfare theorem route).
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q1(b), 2019 Q1(b), 2019 Q10(c), 2020 Q1(a), 2022 Q1(b), 2024 Q1(a), 2025 Q1(b).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Pareto Efficiency, Concepts/Edgeworth Box, Concepts/Second Welfare Theorem.
Sub-question Type A.5: First welfare theorem (statement and use)
  • What it asks: State the first welfare theorem and link a computed competitive equilibrium to Pareto efficiency.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. State the theorem: in an economy with locally non-satiated preferences and complete markets without externalities, every competitive equilibrium allocation is Pareto efficient.
  2. Prove sketch: suppose not, then there is a feasible $(x_i')$ Pareto-dominating equilibrium $(x_i^)$. By LNS, any bundle preferred to $x_i^$ must cost at least as much as $x_i^*$ at equilibrium prices, and any strictly preferred bundle must cost strictly more. Summing over agents the dominating allocation costs more than the endowment in value, contradicting feasibility.
  3. Use in the question: after computing CE in part (c), state that PE follows immediately by FWT, so we do not need to verify MRS equality separately.
  4. Watch the assumptions: 2017 Q1(d) asks about the effect of a wage-income tax, which breaks the link because the tax wedge introduces a distortion that violates the LNS-CE setup.
  • Appeared in: 2017 Q1(d), 2019 Q1, 2024 Q1, plus implicit in 2022 Q5 and 2023 Q5 essays.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/First Welfare Theorem, Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium, Concepts/Pareto Efficiency.
Sub-question Type A.6: Second welfare theorem and lump-sum redistribution
  • What it asks: Given a Pareto efficient target allocation $K$, find lump-sum transfers of endowments that decentralise $K$ as a competitive equilibrium, and find the supporting price.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. State the theorem: under convexity of preferences and production sets, every Pareto efficient allocation is a competitive equilibrium for some redistribution of initial endowments.
  2. Find the supporting price: at $K$, compute $MRS_A(K) = MRS_B(K)$ (true because $K$ is PE). This common value is the equilibrium price ratio $p_x / p_y$.
  3. Construct the transfer: find new endowments $\tilde \omega_A, \tilde \omega_B$ such that (i) $\tilde \omega_A + \tilde \omega_B = \omega_A + \omega_B$ (aggregate preserved), (ii) at price $p_x / p_y$ from step 2, agent A's Cobb-Douglas demand from wealth $p \tilde\omega_{Ax} + \tilde\omega_{Ay}$ equals $K_A$, same for B.
  4. In practice for 2015 Q1(e): solve $\alpha (p \tilde\omega_{Ax} + \tilde\omega_{Ay}) / p = K_{Ax}$ for the wealth, then split this wealth between $\tilde\omega_{Ax}$ and $\tilde\omega_{Ay}$ along the budget line.
  5. Easy case: transfers in one good only. 2020 Q1(b) asks for a Pareto improving transfer of $x$ only, exploiting that at the endowment $MRS_A > MRS_B$, so moving $x$ from B to A while letting them retrade lifts both.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q1(e), 2020 Q1(b), 2024 Q1(b), 2025 Q1(c).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Second Welfare Theorem, Concepts/Edgeworth Box, Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium.
Sub-question Type A.7: Social planner choices (utilitarian, Rawlsian)
  • What it asks: Pick the allocation that maximises a given social welfare function over the set of feasible allocations or over the set of allocations reachable by a specific instrument (e.g. transfers of one good).
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Utilitarian planner maximises $W = u_A + u_B$ subject to feasibility. With Cobb-Douglas $u_i = \log x_i + \log y_i$ and a transfer of good $y$ only at fixed equilibrium prices (2024 Q1(c)), the planner picks the transfer to equate weighted marginal utilities of income across agents.
  2. Algorithm for utilitarian transfer: write each agent's indirect utility $v_i(p, m_i)$, then maximise $v_A(p, m_A) + v_B(p, m_B)$ subject to $m_A + m_B$ constant. FOC equates $\partial v_A / \partial m = \partial v_B / \partial m$. For Cobb-Douglas $u = \log x + \log y$ this gives equal wealth across agents, so the recipient is the poorer one.
  3. Rawlsian planner maximises $\min(u_A, u_B)$. Algorithm: equalise utilities subject to feasibility. For 2025 Q1(c) with quasi-linear $u = v(x) + y$, transfers of $y$ shift utility one-for-one. Solve $v_A(x_A^) + y_A = v_B(x_B^) + y_B$ together with $y_A + y_B = \omega_{Ay} + \omega_{By}$.
  4. For 2020 Q1(d) with log utility: utilitarian maximises $\log x_A + \log y_A + \log x_B + \log y_B$ subject to feasibility. FOC give $x_A = x_B$ and $y_A = y_B$ (equal split). Rawlsian also equalises utilities given symmetric utilities, so it coincides here when the planner can choose freely. Distinguish carefully which instruments are available.
  5. Note: utilitarian and Rawlsian can coincide when utility functions are symmetric and the planner has full instrument freedom. They differ when utilities differ across agents or when only some transfers are allowed.
  • Appeared in: 2020 Q1(d), 2024 Q1(c), 2025 Q1(c).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Utilitarian Planner, Concepts/Rawlsian Planner, Concepts/Social Welfare Functions.
Sub-question Type A.8: Edgeworth box construction and identification of regions
  • What it asks: Draw the Edgeworth box, mark endowment $\omega$, the contract curve, the lens of Pareto-improving allocations, and identify whether labelled points are PE, Pareto-superior to $\omega$, or Pareto-inferior.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Set up the box. Width equals $\omega_{Ax} + \omega_{Bx}$, height equals $\omega_{Ay} + \omega_{By}$. Origin $O_A$ at bottom-left for A, origin $O_B$ at top-right for B (so B's quantities increase down and left).
  2. Mark the endowment $\omega$. Draw the indifference curves of A and B through $\omega$. The lens between them is the set of allocations that Pareto-dominate $\omega$.
  3. Contract curve: locus of $MRS_A = MRS_B$ from $O_A$ to $O_B$. For symmetric Cobb-Douglas with identical $\alpha$, this is the diagonal. For asymmetric $\alpha_A \neq \alpha_B$ it bows.
  4. Classify labelled points: a point is PE iff on the contract curve. Pareto-superior to $\omega$ iff in the closure of the lens. Pareto-inferior iff $\omega$ is in the lens of the point.
  5. 2015 Q1(c) twist: point $G$ is on the contract curve (PE) but not in the lens of $A$. State that PE does not imply unanimous improvement, then propose a social welfare function such as utilitarian or Rawlsian to break the tie.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q1(a)(b)(c), 2020 Q1(a), 2022 Q1(b).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Edgeworth Box, Concepts/Pareto Efficiency, Concepts/Pareto Criterion.
Sub-question Type A.9: MRS equals MRT, the production efficiency condition
  • What it asks: For a one-consumer one-firm production economy, derive and verify the condition $MRS = MRT$ at the competitive equilibrium, and draw the PPF.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Compute MRT from the PPF. For $Y = 2 L^{1/2}$ with time endowment $T = 1$, leisure $\ell = 1 - L$ and so $Y^2 / 4 + \ell = 1$, giving a PPF in $(\ell, Y)$ space. $|dY/d\ell| = 2/Y$ along the PPF.
  2. Compute MRS for the consumer between leisure and output. For $u = \alpha \log Y + (1-\alpha) \log \ell$, $MRS = \frac{\alpha \ell}{(1-\alpha) Y}$.
  3. Set MRS = MRT: $\frac{\alpha \ell}{(1-\alpha) Y} = 2/Y$, solve for $\ell$ and then $Y, L$.
  4. Compare to CE. In CE, the firm's FOC gives $MPL = w/p$, the consumer's FOC gives $MRS = w/p$, and the firm's technology gives $MRT = 1/MPL$. So the consumer's FOC times the firm's FOC gives $MRS = MRT$, confirming FWT.
  5. Draw the PPF with the equilibrium tangency point. Mark the budget line through the endowment (here, $(1, 0)$ since the household owns time and the firm) with slope equal to the negative price ratio.
  • Appeared in: 2017 Q1, 2019 Q1(a)(d).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/PPF and MRT, Concepts/First Welfare Theorem, Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium.
Sub-question Type A.10: Comparative statics on competitive equilibrium
  • What it asks: How does the equilibrium price or allocation move when an exogenous variable changes (number of firms $N$, productivity $A$, number of households $M$, endowment $\omega$, transfer $T$)?
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Write the equilibrium equation implicitly, e.g. $L^d(w/p; N, A) = L^s(w/p; M)$ for the production economy.
  2. Totally differentiate: $\frac{\partial L^d}{\partial (w/p)} d(w/p) + \frac{\partial L^d}{\partial A} dA = \frac{\partial L^s}{\partial (w/p)} d(w/p)$, solve for $d(w/p)/dA$.
  3. For 2014 Q1(d) with $N$ firms and $M$ households, the equilibrium $w/p$ depends on $N/M$ (ratio matters under constant returns at the aggregate). Doubling both leaves $w/p$ unchanged.
  4. For 2018 Q1(d) a rise in productivity $A$ in Cobb-Douglas tech raises factor shares of revenue proportionally but the price effect distributes differently across capitalists and workers. Decompose: $d \pi / dA$ for capitalists has a direct production effect and an indirect price effect.
  5. For 2024 Q1(b) a transfer of good $y$: under Cobb-Douglas with both agents consuming both goods in interior, transfers of $y$ shift wealth but the relative demand for $x$ versus $y$ within each agent is unchanged in the income ratio, so the relative price stays put. Show this by computing aggregate $x$-demand as a function of $p$ after the transfer and checking it does not move.
  • Appeared in: 2014 Q1(d), 2018 Q1(d), 2024 Q1(b).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium, Concepts/Cobb-Douglas Demand.
Sub-question Type A.11: Identify CE versus non-CE via excess demand
  • What it asks: At a candidate price $p$, compute aggregate excess demand $z(p)$. The price is a CE iff $z(p) = 0$. Identify why a non-CE price fails (excess demand or excess supply).
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Compute individual demands at $p$ from Marshallian formulas.
  2. Sum to aggregate demand $D(p)$, subtract aggregate endowment to get excess demand $z(p) = D(p) - \Omega$.
  3. Check sign. If $z(p) > 0$ for good $x$, there is excess demand and the auctioneer should raise $p_x$. If $z(p) < 0$, lower it.
  4. For 2015 Q1(d): at $p_0$, $z = 0$; at $p_1$, $z \neq 0$ in at least one market. Identify the excess in words, e.g. "at $p_1$ A wants to sell more $x$ than B wishes to buy at this price".
  5. For 2022 Q1(a) with Leontief-Cobb-Douglas: verify the monotonicity claim $\partial z / \partial p < 0$ in one good. This is a property of gross substitutes, which ensures uniqueness of CE.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q1(d), 2022 Q1(a)(b).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Excess Demand, Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium.
Sub-question Type A.12: Pareto criterion definition and critique
  • What it asks: Define the Pareto criterion as a partial ordering on allocations; discuss its strengths (minimal, uncontroversial) and weaknesses (incompleteness, silence on distribution).
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Define: allocation $A$ Pareto-dominates $B$ iff $u_i(A) \geq u_i(B)$ for all $i$ and strict for some $i$. $A$ is Pareto efficient iff nothing dominates it.
  2. Strengths: requires no interpersonal utility comparisons, respects unanimity, is the weakest defensible welfare criterion.
  3. Weaknesses: incomplete (cannot rank most allocation pairs), silent on distribution (any point on the contract curve is PE, including extremely unequal ones), insensitive to inequality.
  4. Propose an extension like utilitarian $W = \sum u_i$, Rawlsian $W = \min u_i$, or Kaldor-Hicks (potential compensation). State the corresponding axiomatic cost: utilitarian needs cardinal utility, Rawlsian privileges the worst-off, Kaldor-Hicks ignores actual compensation.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q1(a)(c), 2020 Q1(c), plus essays 2022 Q5 and 2024 Q5.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Pareto Criterion, Concepts/Social Welfare Functions, Concepts/Kaldor-Hicks Compensation.
Part B: essay-style questions
Sub-question Type B.E.1: Two fundamental welfare theorems and policy implications
  • What it asks: State and prove (or sketch) FWT and SWT. Discuss what each one means for the design of microeconomic policy: should the state redistribute endowments and let markets clear, or intervene in markets directly?
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis (~150 words): the FWT establishes the efficiency case for markets; the SWT establishes that any desired efficient distribution can be reached through lump-sum transfers, in principle separating efficiency from distribution. The practical bite is much smaller than the formal result suggests, because lump-sum transfers do not exist for endowments that are partly skill or effort, and because the assumptions fail in many real markets.
  2. Define carefully: a competitive equilibrium $(p^, x^)$, Pareto efficiency, the assumptions (LNS preferences, complete markets, no externalities, convex preferences and production for SWT).
  3. Prove FWT: contradiction argument from LNS, as in A.5 step 2.
  4. Sketch SWT: separating hyperplane theorem on the set of preferred bundles and the aggregate production set, both convex. The separating hyperplane is the price.
  5. Policy use: FWT supports laissez-faire under its assumptions. SWT supports the "tax endowments then let markets work" view. Together they suggest distribution is a political problem solvable by lump-sum transfers; the second-best Mirrleesian view comes in when transfers must depend on observed income, generating distortions.
  6. Limitations: externalities (Topic 3), public goods, asymmetric information (Topics 7 and 8), monopoly power, incomplete markets, non-convexities from fixed costs or indivisibilities, behavioural failures. Each kills FWT, SWT, or both.
  7. Conclusion: SWT is more politically interesting, FWT more theoretically central. Together they frame why microeconomists separate efficiency analysis from distributional analysis.

Sub-question Type B.E.2: Models of trade and distributional implications
  • What it asks: Compare two or three models of international trade (Ricardian, Heckscher-Ohlin, monopolistic competition / new trade) and what each predicts about who gains and who loses.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis (~150 words): each model isolates a different gain from trade. Ricardian: comparative advantage from technology. HO: factor endowment differences. New trade: scale economies and variety. The distributional consequences differ sharply across models, which is why the political economy of trade does not fit one box.
  2. Ricardian setup: two goods, one factor (labour), constant returns. Country with comparative advantage in good $x$ exports $x$. All workers gain in real terms if specialisation is complete and trade is voluntary. Diagram: world PPF as the upper envelope of country PPFs.
  3. HO setup: two goods, two factors, identical technology across countries. Stolper-Samuelson: the factor used intensively in the exported good gains in real terms, the other loses. So skilled labour in skill-abundant countries gains; unskilled labour loses. Direct distributional conflict within country.
  4. New trade: monopolistic competition with love of variety, fixed costs. Gains come from larger market, lower price-cost margin, and variety. Distributional effects more diffuse but matter at firm level (productive firms expand, unproductive exit, workers reallocate).
  5. Synthesis: trade raises aggregate income, but the distributional question depends on which margin dominates empirically. Autor-Dorn-Hanson on the China shock shows large concentrated losses in import-competing regions that the standard models predict only weakly.
  6. Conclusion: the compensation logic from Kaldor-Hicks (essay B.E.4) connects directly here, since the question of whether the gains can compensate the losers, and whether they actually do, is central.

Sub-question Type B.E.3: Social choice rule design
  • What it asks: A senior tutor must aggregate tutors' rankings of students into a single ranking. Design or evaluate aggregation rules in light of Arrow-style impossibility results.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis (~150 words): no rule can satisfy unrestricted domain, Pareto, IIA, and non-dictatorship simultaneously. Practical rules trade off these properties; the question becomes which trade-off the senior tutor finds least bad.
  2. State Arrow's theorem: with at least three alternatives and at least two voters, no aggregation rule satisfies (i) unrestricted domain, (ii) Pareto, (iii) IIA, (iv) non-dictatorship.
  3. Survey candidate rules: simple majority (fails transitivity on three or more alternatives, Condorcet cycles), Borda count (fails IIA, can be manipulated by adding alternatives), plurality (ignores intensity), approval voting (fails ordinal completeness), score voting (uses cardinal info, fails IIA).
  4. Apply to tutors: how many alternatives? how many voters? if only a small number of clearly best students, plurality with runoff is reasonable. If many students of similar quality, Borda or score voting captures intensity better but is more manipulable.
  5. Strategy-proofness: Gibbard-Satterthwaite says any non-dictatorial rule with three or more alternatives is manipulable. Senior tutor should expect tutors to vote strategically; pick a rule whose manipulability is empirically modest.
  6. Conclusion: recommend a specific rule, justify on the senior tutor's likely preferences over (Pareto, IIA, simplicity, strategy-proofness, transparency). A common pragmatic answer is Borda count for ranking with a sanity check by simple majority on the top candidate.

Sub-question Type B.E.4: Kaldor-Hicks compensation principle as a policy guide
  • What it asks: Should microeconomic policy follow the Kaldor-Hicks criterion (a change is desirable if gainers could in principle compensate losers, even if they do not), and if not, what should guide policy?
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis (~150 words): KH coincides with cost-benefit analysis and is a workhorse of applied welfare economics; but its silence on whether compensation actually happens makes it ethically and politically unstable. Policy should use KH as a screening tool for efficiency and a separate distributional weight for politics.
  2. Define KH: change from $A$ to $B$ is a KH improvement iff the winners in $B$ could compensate the losers and still be better off than at $A$. Equivalent to $\sum \Delta CV \geq 0$ when CVs are well-defined.
  3. Strengths: completes the Pareto ordering, supports CBA, transparent in money terms, separates efficiency from distribution analytically.
  4. Weaknesses: (i) Scitovsky paradox: KH can be cyclic, $A$ over $B$ and $B$ over $A$. (ii) Hypothetical compensation does not help actual losers. (iii) Implicit weighting: a dollar to a millionaire counts the same as a dollar to a poor person. (iv) Path-dependent measure (CV vs EV).
  5. Alternatives: actual compensation (rare and politically costly), weighted CBA (distributional weights, used by HMT Green Book), Sen's capability approach, Rawlsian maximin, utilitarian SWF, or even procedural rules ignoring outcome welfare.
  6. Conclusion: KH is a useful first cut; serious policy needs explicit distributional weights or transfer instruments alongside it. The free-trade case shows the limitation, because the China shock losers were not in practice compensated.

  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Kaldor-Hicks Compensation, Concepts/Pareto Criterion, Concepts/Social Welfare Functions.
Sub-question Type B.E.5: Free trade under unanimous consent
  • What it asks: Under what conditions does free trade make everyone better off in a country that requires consensus? Is such adoption likely?
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis (~150 words): free trade raises aggregate real income (Ricardian and HO both predict this) but creates losers as well as gainers within country. Unanimous consent requires either no losers or credible compensation. The first is rare except in special cases; the second is hard to commit to.
  2. No-loser cases: small open economy with one factor (pure Ricardian), so the wage rises uniformly. Or autarky already at world prices, so trade has no effect. Or trade in goods that no domestic factor produced (variety gains only).
  3. General case requires compensation: SWT and FWT together imply that any Pareto-efficient distribution can be reached by lump-sum transfers, so if the post-trade aggregate is larger than autarky and transfers are feasible, unanimous improvement is reachable.
  4. Practical adoption likely?: lump-sum transfers are not available; redistribution comes through taxes that distort behaviour. Losers also discount future transfer promises if the political process is uncertain. Hence consensus is hard to achieve in practice, even when the KH gains are large.
  5. Sketch a model: Ricardian world with two goods, two countries, symmetric L. Show that with quasi-linear preferences over an outside numeraire good plus a tax-financed lump-sum transfer, free trade Pareto dominates autarky. Then show that with realistic distortionary taxes the result can fail.
  6. Conclusion: free trade with redistribution is theoretically a unanimous improvement; without redistribution it usually creates losers. The empirical record (China shock) suggests we should be sceptical that the redistribution actually arrives.

Sub-question Type B.L.1: Two-sector Ricardian trade with PPF and gains from trade
  • What it asks: Two countries (Home and Foreign) produce two goods using a single factor (labour) under linear technology. Derive PPFs, find autarky and trade equilibria, compute gains from trade, and do comparative statics on the foreign endowment.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Derive PPF and MRT. In each country with labour $L$ and unit labour requirements $a_x, a_y$, the PPF is the line $a_x X + a_y Y = L$. $MRT = a_x / a_y$, constant.
  2. Derive MRS for Cobb-Douglas preferences $u = X^\alpha Y^{1-\alpha}$. $MRS = \frac{\alpha Y}{(1-\alpha) X}$.
  3. Autarky CE: with one representative consumer-producer per country, set $MRS = MRT$ to find autarky allocation $(X^a, Y^a)$ and price $p^a = a_x / a_y$.
  4. World price under trade: with symmetric labour endowments and symmetric productivity, $p^w = 1$ by symmetry. With asymmetry, $p^w$ lies between the two autarky prices.
  5. Pattern of trade: each country exports the good in which it has comparative advantage (lower autarky price relative to world price).
  6. Gains from trade diagram: draw the country's PPF, the autarky indifference curve tangent at $(X^a, Y^a)$, and the trade budget line through the production point at world price $p^w$. The trade indifference curve lies strictly outside the autarky one, with the area between representing gains from trade.
  7. Comparative statics: if Foreign $L^*$ rises, Foreign supply of its export good rises, pushing the world relative price away from autarky-Foreign towards Home. Home's terms of trade improve, Home real income rises. Use the trade triangle to verify.

Sub-question Type B.L.2: Multi-good exchange economy with N traders
  • What it asks: Three goods, three types of traders with Cobb-Douglas preferences over the goods, asymmetric endowments. Compute individual demands, aggregate excess demands, CE prices, check Walras, then check PE of a candidate allocation.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Individual demands. For Cobb-Douglas $u_i = \sum_g \alpha_{ig} \log x_{ig}$ with $\sum_g \alpha_{ig} = 1$, the demand is $x_{ig}^* = \alpha_{ig} m_i / p_g$ where $m_i = \sum_g p_g \omega_{ig}$.
  2. Will all types trade? Compare each agent's endowment-MRS to the others'. If $MRS_{xy}^i \neq MRS_{xy}^j$ at the endowment, there is a Pareto-improving trade between $i$ and $j$. Confirm gains from trade exist.
  3. Excess demand for each good: $z_g(p) = \sum_i (x_{ig}^*(p) - \omega_{ig}) = \sum_i (\alpha_{ig} m_i / p_g - \omega_{ig})$.
  4. Normalise $p_3 = 1$. Two market-clearing equations $z_1(p_1, p_2) = 0$ and $z_2(p_1, p_2) = 0$ give two equations in two unknowns. Solve. With Cobb-Douglas, prices come out cleanly: $p_g = \sum_i \alpha_{ig} m_i / \sum_i \omega_{ig}$ which becomes a fixed-point in $m_i$.
  5. Walras check: verify $\sum_g p_g z_g(p) = 0$ identically. If you solved two markets to zero, the third must clear automatically.
  6. PE check of a new allocation $K$. Compute $MRS_{xy}^i$ for each agent at $K_i$. PE iff all three MRSs equal across agents for every pair of goods. If yes, find the supporting prices (the common MRS values). If no, identify a Pareto-improving trade between two specific agents.
  • Appeared in: 2019 Q10.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium, Concepts/Cobb-Douglas Demand, Concepts/Walras Law, Concepts/Excess Demand, Concepts/Pareto Efficiency, Concepts/Second Welfare Theorem.
How to recognise the format on the day

A Part A short problem in GE/Welfare almost always opens with a small economy described in 4 to 6 lines, then asks sub-parts (a) to (e) with verbs like "compute", "show", "find", "verify", "draw". Each sub-part is worth a few marks, the total is around 10 marks, and a competent answer is two to three pages of working. Expect numeric answers and clearly stated economic conditions (MRS = MRT, price ratio, market clearing).

A Part B essay uses verbs like "discuss", "evaluate", "assess", "should". One short paragraph sets up a claim or policy question; the rest of the prompt is a single open invitation. No diagrams are demanded but one or two well-chosen diagrams strengthen the answer. Expect 4 to 6 pages of writing with a few formal results inserted as scaffolding rather than as the main content.

A Part B long-answer problem looks like a stretched Part A: a longer setup with more agents, more goods, more parameters, and sub-parts (a) to (f) or (g) where later parts build on earlier ones. The verbs are still "compute", "derive", "find", but the algebra is heavier and the diagram requirements often explicit ("sketch the PPF", "draw the trade triangle"). Expect 5 to 8 pages and worry less about narrative, more about correctness and clear labelling.

1 Competitive Equilibrium Tier 1

1. Intuition

A competitive equilibrium is a price vector together with an allocation such that, taking prices as given, every consumer maximises utility within their budget and every firm maximises profits, and all markets clear (supply equals demand for every good).

The key idea is that prices coordinate decisions made independently by many agents. No one is in charge of matching demand to supply: the price simply adjusts until the two coincide. This is the workhorse benchmark of microeconomics, and almost every Part A question on GE asks you to compute one.

2. Formal mathematics

A competitive equilibrium in a pure exchange economy with $I$ consumers and $L$ goods is a price vector $p^* \in \mathbb{R}^L_{++}$ and an allocation $(x_i^*)_{i=1}^I$ such that (i) each $x_i^*$ solves $\max u_i(x_i)$ subject to $p^* \cdot x_i \leq p^* \cdot \omega_i$, and (ii) markets clear: $\sum_i x_i^* = \sum_i \omega_i$.

The key feature is that agents take prices as given and choose only their own bundle. No agent coordinates with another. Equilibrium emerges because the price vector reconciles all individual plans simultaneously.

To derive existence in the 2-good, 2-person Edgeworth box, we look for $p^* = (p_1^*, p_2^*)$ such that excess demand $z(p) = \sum_i (x_i(p, p\cdot \omega_i) - \omega_i)$ is zero.

  1. Each consumer solves $\max u_i(x_{i1}, x_{i2})$ subject to $p_1 x_{i1} + p_2 x_{i2} = p_1 \omega_{i1} + p_2 \omega_{i2}$. The interior FOC gives $MRS_i = p_1/p_2$.
  2. By Walras' law, $p \cdot z(p) \equiv 0$, so if good 2 clears then good 1 clears too. We only need one market equation.
  3. Continuity of $z$ plus boundary behaviour (excess demand explodes as a price tends to zero) gives a fixed point via Brouwer, hence $p^*$ exists.
  4. At $p^*$, $MRS_1 = MRS_2 = p_1^*/p_2^*$, so the allocation sits on the contract curve, confirming the First Welfare Theorem.

The First Welfare Theorem (Micro2025 Lecture 4, Varian Ch. 17) states: any competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient, provided preferences are locally non-satiated and there are no externalities. The proof is one paragraph. Suppose $(x_i^*)$ is not Pareto efficient. Then there exists $(\hat x_i)$ with $u_i(\hat x_i) \geq u_i(x_i^*)$ for all $i$ and strict for some. Local non-satiation plus utility maximisation imply $p^* \cdot \hat x_i \geq p^* \cdot \omega_i$ for all $i$ and strict for some, hence $p^* \cdot \sum_i \hat x_i > p^* \cdot \sum_i \omega_i$, contradicting feasibility.

The Second Welfare Theorem provides the converse: any Pareto efficient allocation can be supported as a competitive equilibrium after suitable lump-sum transfers, provided preferences are convex. This is the formal basis for the equity-efficiency separation that motivates much of Topic 1.

References: Micro2025.pdf Topic 1 Lectures 3-5; Varian, Microeconomic Analysis, Ch. 17-18; Mas-Colell, Whinston, Green Ch. 15-16.

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • Stock and commodity exchanges approximate the price-taking ideal: thousands of small participants, public prices, near-instant clearing. The price reconciles plans without any central planner observing preferences.
  • Online ad auctions (Google, Meta) are a real-time competitive market for impressions. Walrasian-style equilibrium logic predicts the marginal value of an impression equals the marginal cost across advertisers.
  • Electricity wholesale markets (Nord Pool, ERCOT) implement a Walrasian auctioneer literally: the system operator solves for a clearing price that equates supply and demand at each node.
  • Evaluation move: Kate Doornik rewards essays that distinguish the positive claim (markets clear, FWT holds) from the normative claim (the resulting allocation is desirable). The FWT says nothing about distribution. Pair it with the Second Welfare Theorem to make the equity-efficiency separation explicit.
  • Evaluation move: the convexity assumption in SWT is load-bearing. Without convex preferences, supporting prices need not exist, and the planner cannot decentralise the chosen Pareto point.
  • Standard limitation: FWT fails under externalities, public goods, asymmetric information, or non-convexities. These failures motivate Topic 3 on market failure.

1 Quasi-linear Utility Tier 1

1. Intuition

Utility takes the form $u(x, y) = v(x) + y$, where x is the good we care about and y is a numeraire (often interpreted as money). The key property is no income effect on x: demand for x depends only on price, not income.

Quasi-linear utility makes welfare analysis trivial because compensating variation, equivalent variation, and change in consumer surplus all coincide. It is also the assumption behind summing utilities for Pareto efficiency in externality problems.

2. Formal mathematics

Quasi-linear utility takes the form $u(x_1, \dots, x_{L-1}, m) = v(x_1, \dots, x_{L-1}) + m$, where $m$ is a numeraire good (often interpreted as money or composite consumption) and $v$ is concave in the other goods. The defining feature is that utility is linear in $m$ and separable from the other arguments.

The behavioural consequence is that demand for the non-numeraire goods is independent of income, provided the consumer is not at a corner. To see this, set $p_m = 1$ and consider $\max v(x) + m$ subject to $p \cdot x + m = w$.

  1. Substitute the constraint: $\max v(x) + w - p \cdot x$.
  2. FOC in each $x_l$: $\partial v / \partial x_l = p_l$. Wealth $w$ does not appear.
  3. Hence Marshallian demand $x^*(p)$ depends only on prices. All income effects load onto $m$.
  4. The indirect utility is $V(p, w) = v(x^*(p)) - p \cdot x^*(p) + w$, additively separable in $w$.

Two implications matter for welfare analysis. First, Marshallian and Hicksian demand coincide for the non-numeraire goods, so consumer surplus, compensating variation, and equivalent variation are all equal: $CV = EV = \Delta CS$. This is why quasi-linear preferences are the default in partial equilibrium welfare economics (Varian Ch. 10, Hindriks-Myles Ch. 5).

Second, in a quasi-linear economy, utility is transferable via $m$. The Pareto frontier is a straight line with slope $-1$ in the utility space (between any two agents), and the efficient quantity of any non-numeraire good is independent of the distribution of wealth. This is the technical condition that makes the Kaldor-Hicks criterion coincide with Pareto efficiency, and it underpins the cost-benefit logic used throughout Topic 3.

For a public good $G$ financed by quasi-linear agents with $u_i = v_i(G) + m_i$, the efficient level solves $\sum_i v_i'(G) = c'(G)$, the Samuelson condition, with no distributional caveat because money utility is constant.

Limitation: quasi-linearity rules out income effects on the goods of interest. For goods that are a non-trivial share of the budget (housing, food in developing countries), the assumption is poor and welfare measures diverge. Hausman (1981) shows how to recover exact welfare measures from estimated Marshallian demand when quasi-linearity fails.

References: Micro2025.pdf Topic 1 Lecture 2 and Topic 3 Lecture 1; Varian Microeconomic Analysis Ch. 7 and 10; Mas-Colell Ch. 3 Appendix.

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • Auction theory assumes quasi-linear bidders ($u_i = v_i - p$) so the seller can compare bids directly in money. Without quasi-linearity, the Vickrey-Clarke-Groves mechanisms lose their efficiency guarantee.
  • Cost-benefit analysis of infrastructure (HS2, Crossrail) reports sums of willingness to pay across households. This aggregation is only welfare-meaningful under quasi-linearity, otherwise winners and losers cannot be netted by adding pounds.
  • Insurance and risk-sharing models sometimes use $u = v(c) + m$ where $c$ is a contingent consumption profile, allowing closed-form premium calculations.
  • Evaluation move: Kate Doornik often rewards essays that flag when quasi-linearity is invoked silently. For example, claiming a Pigouvian tax raises welfare typically uses $\Delta CS + \Delta PS - \text{externality}$, which presumes transferable utility.
  • Evaluation move: distinguish quasi-linear in money from quasi-linear in leisure. The labour supply literature often uses $u = v(c) - \phi(\ell)$, which is quasi-linear in consumption and gives a wage elasticity of labour supply that is purely substitution-driven.
  • Standard limitation: Hausman (1981) and Vartia (1983) show that for goods with material income elasticity (housing, transport in poor households), CS overstates true welfare loss by 5-15%. The default partial-equilibrium toolkit is mildly biased.

1 Walras Law Tier 3

1. Intuition

Walras' Law says that the value of total excess demand across all markets is identically zero, regardless of prices. 2 Arrow's Impossibility Theorem Tier 2

1. Intuition

With three or more alternatives, no preference aggregation rule can simultaneously satisfy unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, non-dictatorship, and independence of irrelevant alternatives while producing a transitive social ordering. Some axiom has to give.

Arrow's theorem is not a counsel of despair; it tells you that any actual rule (majority, Borda, etc.) is making implicit trade-offs between the axioms. Knowing which trade-off your rule is making is the substantive content.

2 Pareto Efficiency Tier 2

1. Intuition

An allocation is Pareto efficient if no reallocation can make someone better off without making someone else worse off. This is a minimal benchmark: it does not say anything about fairness or distribution, only about avoiding waste.

Pareto efficiency in an exchange economy requires equalised MRS across consumers. With production, you also need MRS equal to MRT. The set of all Pareto efficient allocations forms the contract curve (in exchange) or the utility possibility frontier.

2 Second Welfare Theorem Tier 2

1. Intuition

Any Pareto efficient allocation can be supported as a competitive equilibrium after a suitable lump-sum redistribution of endowments. So efficiency and distribution can be separated: pick the distribution you want and let the market find the prices that decentralise it.

The theorem requires convex preferences and technologies. The bigger practical caveat is that lump-sum transfers are hard to implement in reality (because they depend on unobservable types), so policymakers fall back on distortionary taxation.

2 First Welfare Theorem Tier 3

1. Intuition

Every competitive equilibrium allocation is Pareto efficient, provided preferences are locally non-satiated. This is the formal statement of Adam Smith's invisible hand: self-interested decentralised behaviour produces a no-waste outcome.

The theorem requires no externalities, no public goods, no asymmetric information, no market power. Any of those breaks the result, which is why most of the rest of micro is about what goes wrong when an assumption fails.

Part A: short compulsory problems
Sub-question Type A.1: Define public good vs private vs club, identify the free-rider problem
  • What it asks: State the two defining characteristics of a pure public good, contrast with private goods and club goods, then explain why decentralised provision is inefficient.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Define the two properties using Concepts/Rivalry and Excludability. Non-rival: one person's consumption does not reduce what is available to another, formally $u_i$ depends on the same $Q$ for all $i$. Non-excludable: the provider cannot prevent any agent from consuming once $Q$ is supplied.
  2. Build the 2x2 table. Pure public good: non-rival, non-excludable (national defence, flood defence). Private good: rival and excludable (apple). Club good: non-rival but excludable (Netflix). Common pool: rival but non-excludable (open-access fishery, congested road), see Concepts/Tragedy of the Commons.
  3. Explain the Concepts/Free Rider Problem. Each agent's private optimum sets their own marginal benefit equal to marginal cost, ignoring the benefit conferred on the other $n-1$ agents. So private contributions $q_i$ aggregate to $Q < Q^*$.
  4. Note the link: non-excludability removes the ability to charge, non-rivalry means the efficient price is zero anyway (the marginal cost of an extra user is zero). Combined, the market fails on both supply and pricing.
  5. If asked for one sentence of policy lead-in: government provision financed by taxation, subsidies, or mechanisms like Concepts/Lindahl Prices and the Concepts/Clarke-Groves Mechanism are the textbook responses.
  • Appeared in: 2014 Q5(a), 2024 Q2(a) prelude, 2025 Q6 (essay backbone).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Public Goods, Concepts/Rivalry and Excludability, Concepts/Free Rider Problem, Concepts/Tragedy of the Commons.
Sub-question Type A.2: Samuelson condition, derive optimal $Q^*$ and discuss comparative statics in $n$
  • What it asks: Derive the Concepts/Samuelson Condition for efficient public good provision, solve for $Q^*$ given specific utilities, and sign the effect of more residents $n$.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Write the social planner's problem. With Concepts/Quasi-linear Utility $u_i(Q, x_i) = b_i(Q) + x_i$ and aggregate resource $\sum x_i + c(Q) = W$, the planner maximises $\sum_i b_i(Q) - c(Q)$.
  2. Take FOC in $Q$. This gives $\sum_i b_i'(Q) = c'(Q)$, the Samuelson condition: sum of marginal benefits equals marginal cost.
  3. Plug in the functional form from the question. For 2014 Q5 with $b_i(Q) = a\ln(Q)$ and $c(Q) = Q^2/2$ (or whatever the paper supplies), get $\sum_i a / Q^ = c'(Q^)$, so $Q^* = (na/c')^{1/k}$ for some $k$.
  4. Sign $\partial Q^/\partial n$. With identical agents, the sum scales linearly in $n$, so $Q^$ rises with $n$. The intuition: more beneficiaries means the same physical $Q$ generates more total marginal benefit.
  5. Contrast with the symmetric voluntary equilibrium $Q^{VC}$ (Type A.3) where $Q^{VC}$ does not rise with $n$ under standard quasi-linear forms. The wedge $Q^* - Q^{VC}$ grows in $n$: more agents means more free-riding.
  6. Diagram: draw the vertical sum of individual MB curves (because non-rival) versus MC, the intersection is $Q^*$. Contrast with horizontal sum for private goods.
  • Appeared in: 2014 Q5(b), 2014 Q5(e) diagram, 2022 Q10(b), 2024 Q2(b), 2025 Q6 (formal core).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Samuelson Condition, Concepts/Public Goods, Concepts/Quasi-linear Utility, Concepts/Marginal Private vs Social Benefit.
Sub-question Type A.3: Voluntary contribution Nash equilibrium and why it is independent of $n$
  • What it asks: Write the reaction function $q_i(q_{-i})$, find the symmetric Concepts/Voluntary Contribution Nash Equilibrium, and explain why $Q^{VC}$ does not depend on the number of contributors under quasi-linear preferences.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Set up agent $i$'s problem taking $q_{-i} = \sum_{j \ne i} q_j$ as given. With private numeraire $x_i$ and budget $x_i + p q_i = w_i$, the agent chooses $q_i \ge 0$ to maximise $b_i(q_i + q_{-i}) + w_i - p q_i$.
  2. Take FOC. Interior FOC: $b_i'(Q) = p$ where $Q = q_i + q_{-i}$. So each contributor sets their own marginal benefit equal to the private marginal cost, ignoring all $n-1$ others.
  3. Solve the reaction function. Invert: $Q = (b_i')^{-1}(p)$, i.e. each agent wants total provision to equal their private optimum $\bar{Q}_i$, contributing $q_i = \bar{Q}_i - q_{-i}$ if positive, else zero (corner).
  4. Identify the symmetric equilibrium. With identical agents, only one $\bar{Q}$ exists. In equilibrium $Q^{VC} = \bar{Q}$, and each contributes $q_i = \bar{Q}/n$ (or only some contribute, others corner-solve to zero).
  5. State the $n$-independence punchline. The total $Q^{VC} = \bar{Q}$ does not depend on $n$ because adding a contributor with the same $\bar{Q}$ just shrinks individual contributions, leaving aggregate fixed. This is the formal statement of Concepts/Free Rider Problem: as $n$ grows, the gap $Q^* - Q^{VC}$ explodes.
  6. Verify with the question's numbers. Compute the wedge $Q^ - Q^{VC}$ and comment on the welfare loss, often quantifiable in the quasi-linear setup as the integral of the gap between $\sum b_i'$ and $c'$ between $Q^{VC}$ and $Q^$.
  • Appeared in: 2014 Q5(c) and (d), 2022 Q10(a), 2024 Q2(a), 2025 Q6 (formal core).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Voluntary Contribution Nash Equilibrium, Concepts/Free Rider Problem, Concepts/Samuelson Condition, Concepts/Quasi-linear Utility.
Sub-question Type A.4: Tragedy of the commons, derive equilibrium $N^e$ and the optimum toll
  • What it asks: For a congested road, open-access fishery, or shared resource, derive private equilibrium entry $N^e$, compare to the social optimum $N^*$, and design the Concepts/Pigouvian Tax / congestion toll.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Set up the Concepts/Tragedy of the Commons framing. The good is rival but non-excludable, so any user can enter without paying for the externality they impose. Specify per-user benefit $b(N)$ that falls with congestion $N$, and outside option $\bar{u}$.
  2. Free-entry condition. Users enter until the marginal user is indifferent: $b(N^e) = \bar{u}$. This pins down $N^e$.
  3. Social planner problem. Maximise total surplus $N \cdot b(N) - N \bar{u}$. FOC: $b(N^) + N^ b'(N^) = \bar{u}$. The extra term $N^ b'(N^*) < 0$ is the negative externality each new user imposes on infra-marginal users.
  4. The wedge. Since $b'(N) < 0$, $N^* < N^e$: free entry over-uses the resource.
  5. Pigouvian toll. Set $\tau = -N^ b'(N^) > 0$, which is the marginal external cost evaluated at the optimum. With the toll, the entry condition becomes $b(N) - \tau = \bar{u}$, which is satisfied at $N^*$ by construction.
  6. Revenue and welfare. Toll revenue is $\tau \cdot N^$. If returned as a lump-sum transfer, it does not affect entry (because users compare margins). Deadweight loss saved is the triangle between $b(N) + N b'(N)$ and $\bar{u}$ over $[N^, N^e]$.
  7. Road improvement subtlety (2015 Q5(e)): under free entry the long-run user welfare is pinned at $\bar{u}$, so widening a road raises $N^e$ without raising per-user benefit. Surplus accrues only if entry is taxed.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q5 (full question), 2024 Q6 (congestion essay variant).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Tragedy of the Commons, Concepts/Pigouvian Tax, Concepts/Marginal Private vs Social Benefit, Concepts/Externality Internalisation.
Sub-question Type A.5: Derive Pigouvian tax with quasi-linear utilities, compute tax revenue
  • What it asks: Given an agent generating an externality on another, find the private optimum, the Pareto efficient level, and the Concepts/Pigouvian Tax that decentralises efficiency. Often a follow-up asks for revenue.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Write the two utility functions. Typically $u_A(x) = f_A(x) - g_A(x)$ and $u_B(x) = -h(x)$ where $x$ is the externality-generating activity. Both linear in money via Concepts/Quasi-linear Utility.
  2. Solve A's private problem. FOC: $f_A'(x^p) - g_A'(x^p) = 0$, i.e. $x^p$ ignores Bob's loss $h(x)$.
  3. Solve the social problem. Maximise $u_A + u_B$ (quasi-linear aggregation is valid). FOC: $f_A'(x^) - g_A'(x^) - h'(x^) = 0$. The extra term $-h'(x^)$ is the marginal external cost.
  4. Construct the Concepts/Pigouvian Tax. Tax A at rate $t$ per unit of $x$. A's new FOC: $f_A'(x) - g_A'(x) - t = 0$. Match to the social FOC: set $t = h'(x^*)$. The tax equals the marginal external damage at the optimum.
  5. Compute revenue. Revenue is $R = t \cdot x^ = h'(x^) \cdot x^$. Note this is generally not equal to total damage $h(x^)$, so the tax does not by itself fully compensate Bob.
  6. Lump-sum compensation (Coase angle). To make Bob whole, transfer $T = h(x^)$ from A to B. With quasi-linear preferences this is a pure utility transfer with no efficiency loss, illustrating the second part of the Concepts/Coase Theorem: efficient $x^$ plus a lump-sum split achieves any point on the contract curve.
  • Appeared in: 2023 Q1 (full question), 2025 Q2(b).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Pigouvian Tax, Concepts/Coase Theorem, Concepts/Externality Internalisation, Concepts/Marginal Private vs Social Benefit, Concepts/Quasi-linear Utility.
Sub-question Type A.6: Coase theorem and lump-sum transfer for compensation
  • What it asks: Given the efficient activity level $x^*$, find the lump-sum transfer that compensates the harmed party fully, or that leaves both parties at least as well off as the no-externality benchmark.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. State the Concepts/Coase Theorem in one line. With well-defined property rights, zero transaction costs, and quasi-linear preferences, parties bargain to the Pareto efficient $x^*$ regardless of initial rights. The rights assignment determines only distribution.
  2. Compute utilities at the efficient point. $u_A(x^) = f_A(x^) - g_A(x^)$, $u_B(x^) = -h(x^*)$.
  3. Compute baseline. Either the no-externality status quo (A produces nothing, $x=0$) or A's private optimum without intervention.
  4. Compensation transfer. If A pays B a transfer $T$:
  • To make B indifferent to status quo: $T = h(x^*)$.
  • To split surplus equally: $T = h(x^) - \tfrac{1}{2}[f_A(x^) - g_A(x^) - h(x^) - (f_A(0) - g_A(0))]$.
  • To fully compensate A for moving from private optimum to $x^$: $T = [f_A(x^p) - g_A(x^p)] - [f_A(x^) - g_A(x^*)]$.
  1. Verify rights neutrality. If B has rights to clean air, A pays B for $x^$. If A has rights to produce, B pays A to reduce $x$ to $x^$. In both cases the same efficient $x^*$ emerges, only the direction of $T$ flips.
  2. Limitations to flag. Many parties means transaction costs; non-quasi-linear preferences mean wealth effects change $x^*$; asymmetric information breaks the bargaining.

  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Coase Theorem, Concepts/Externality Internalisation, Concepts/Quasi-linear Utility.
Sub-question Type A.7: Externality under uncertainty, quadratic profit/loss, Pigouvian tax robustness
  • What it asks: Pollution $x$ generates benefits to a firm and losses to society, both quadratic. Compute efficient $x^$, derive the formula for $t^$, and discuss when a tax remains optimal under uncertainty about parameters.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Write profit and loss. Standard setup: $\pi(x) = (A + a\varepsilon)x - \tfrac{1}{2}x^2$ (linear marginal benefit declining in $x$), $L(x) = Bx + \tfrac{1}{2}bx^2$ (marginal damage increasing in $x$). Random shock $\varepsilon$ may be observed by the firm but not the regulator.
  2. Efficient $x^$. Maximise $\pi - L$: FOC $A + a\varepsilon - x = B + bx$, so $x^ = (A + a\varepsilon - B)/(1 + b)$.
  3. Tax rate. Regulator sets tax $t$, firm's FOC: $A + a\varepsilon - x - t = 0$, so $x_t = A + a\varepsilon - t$. Setting $x_t = x^$ gives $t^ = (B + bA + b a\varepsilon)/(1 + b)$.
  4. Special case from 2025 Q2. When the regulator knows $b = 0$ (marginal damage is flat at $B$), $t^* = B$ regardless of $A$ or $\varepsilon$. This is because a flat MD curve means the optimal price (Pigouvian tax) is identical for every realisation, while a flat marginal benefit means a quantity instrument would be wrong if $A$ is unknown.
  5. State the Concepts/Weitzman Prices vs Quantities intuition. When marginal damage is flatter than marginal benefit (slope of MD $<$ slope of MB), prices (taxes) dominate quantities (permits) under uncertainty. The 2025 Q2 setup with $b = 0$ is the corner case where this is strict.
  6. Why some pollution is Pareto superior to zero (2025 Q2(a)). At $x = 0$ marginal profit $A > 0$ exceeds marginal damage $B$ (assuming $A > B$), so a small increase in $x$ creates a gain that can be redistributed via a transfer to make everyone better off.
  • Appeared in: 2025 Q2 (full question).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Pigouvian Tax, Concepts/Weitzman Prices vs Quantities, Concepts/Externality Internalisation, Concepts/Tradeable Permits, Concepts/Marginal Private vs Social Benefit.
Sub-question Type A.8: Lindahl pricing in a two-agent public good problem
  • What it asks: Describe a Lindahl scheme, compute the personalised prices, and evaluate practical limitations.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Definition. Each agent $i$ faces a personalised price $p_i$ per unit of $Q$ with $\sum_i p_i = c'(Q)$ (prices add up to marginal cost), and each agent demands the same quantity $Q$ at their own price.
  2. Compute demands. Agent $i$ solves $\max_Q b_i(Q) - p_i Q$, FOC $b_i'(Q) = p_i$. Invert: $Q_i^D(p_i) = (b_i')^{-1}(p_i)$.
  3. Equilibrium condition. Find $(p_1, ..., p_n, Q^L)$ such that $Q_i^D(p_i) = Q^L$ for all $i$ and $\sum_i p_i = c'(Q^L)$.
  4. Use the demand equality to get $b_i'(Q^L) = p_i$, sum over $i$: $\sum_i b_i'(Q^L) = \sum_i p_i = c'(Q^L)$. This is the Concepts/Samuelson Condition, so $Q^L = Q^*$.
  5. So Concepts/Lindahl Prices decentralise efficiency: personalised prices generate the Pareto efficient $Q^*$ as a competitive equilibrium.
  6. Practical limitations (the killer point). Truthful revelation: each agent has incentive to understate $b_i'(Q)$ to lower their personalised price. So Lindahl is not strategy-proof and the regulator cannot directly observe preferences. This motivates the Clarke-Groves mechanism, which is strategy-proof but typically runs a budget deficit.
  • Appeared in: 2022 Q10(c), 2024 Q2(c).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Lindahl Prices, Concepts/Samuelson Condition, Concepts/Clarke-Groves Mechanism, Concepts/Free Rider Problem.

Sub-question Type B.E.1: Positive externalities essay, are they safe to ignore
  • What it asks: Critically assess the claim that policymakers can disregard positive externalities, contrasting with negative externalities and pointing to specific examples (R and D, vaccination, education).
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis: The claim is wrong as a blanket rule. Both positive and negative externalities create a wedge between marginal private and marginal social benefit / cost, so both cause under- or over-provision relative to the social optimum. The case for ignoring positives rests on second-best considerations (administrative cost, distributional concerns, infra-marginality), not on the symmetry of the welfare analysis.
  2. Formal core. Set up Concepts/Quasi-linear Utility with two agents, one of whom benefits from another's activity. Show that the social FOC is $f_A'(x) + h'(x) = 0$ where $h'(x) > 0$ is the marginal external benefit. The private FOC ignores this term, so $x^p < x^*$. Symmetric to the negative externality case in sign.
  3. Pigouvian subsidy. The dual of a Concepts/Pigouvian Tax is a subsidy $s = h'(x^*)$ paid to the generator. This is funded from general taxation, which itself has deadweight costs (the $\lambda > 1$ shadow value of public funds). This is one substantive reason a regulator may rationally underweight small positive externalities.
  4. When ignoring is defensible. (a) Inframarginal benefits: the externality may be large in total but zero at the relevant margin, e.g. an iconic building enjoyed by passers-by where the marginal benefit of an additional building of the same kind is zero. (b) Existing institutions already internalise: patents on R and D, schools subsidies for education, public health vaccination subsidies. (c) Identification problems: positive spillovers are hard to measure, so subsidy levels are guesses.
  5. When ignoring is dangerous. R and D: the social return is estimated at roughly 2 to 4 times the private return, so under-investment is large. Vaccination: herd immunity gives a discontinuous social benefit at high coverage that warrants mandates or aggressive subsidies. Education: rising tertiary fees in the UK and US plausibly under-supply human capital below the social optimum.
  6. Comparison with negatives. Negative externalities have stronger political constituencies (victims demand action), positives often do not (beneficiaries are diffuse and unaware). So policy may be biased toward addressing negatives even when the welfare-relevant size is similar.
  7. Conclusion. Disregarding positive externalities is defensible only when the marginal benefit is small, fiscal cost is high, or existing institutions already correct the wedge. As a general principle, the symmetry of the welfare analysis means positives deserve the same scrutiny as negatives.

  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Marginal Private vs Social Benefit, Concepts/Pigouvian Tax, Concepts/Externality Internalisation, Concepts/Quasi-linear Utility.
Sub-question Type B.E.2: Public goods canonical essay, private provision to Lindahl to Clarke-Groves
  • What it asks: A canonical Topic 3 essay walking through the full sequence: definition of public goods, private provision and free-riding, Samuelson condition, Lindahl pricing, and the Clarke-Groves mechanism, with discussion of practical limits at each stage.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis: Pure public goods (non-rival, non-excludable) generate two layers of market failure. Voluntary provision is inefficient because of free-riding; correcting this by simple subsidies cannot work because the regulator does not know preferences. The literature offers a ladder of solutions of increasing sophistication and decreasing practicality: Concepts/Lindahl Prices decentralise efficiency but are not strategy-proof; the Concepts/Clarke-Groves Mechanism is strategy-proof but generally not budget-balanced. Real-world institutions rely on cruder tools (taxation, regulation) combined with cost-benefit analysis.
  2. Definition and free-rider problem. Use the Concepts/Rivalry and Excludability 2x2. Define Concepts/Voluntary Contribution Nash Equilibrium: each agent's FOC $b_i'(Q) = p$ ignores the $n-1$ external benefits, so $Q^{VC} < Q^*$. Under Concepts/Quasi-linear Utility $Q^{VC}$ does not rise with $n$, so the wedge grows linearly in $n$.
  3. Samuelson condition. State and derive $\sum_i b_i'(Q^) = c'(Q^)$ from the planner's problem. Contrast with the private good condition $b_i'(q) = c'(q)$ for all $i$: vertical sum versus horizontal sum reflects non-rivalry.
  4. Lindahl prices. Each agent faces personalised $p_i$ with $\sum p_i = c'(Q)$. Show that demand equality $Q_i^D(p_i) = Q^L$ for all $i$ plus the budget constraint reproduces the Samuelson condition, so $Q^L = Q^*$. Critique: not strategy-proof, since agents shade $b_i'$ downwards to lower $p_i$.
  5. Clarke-Groves mechanism. Each agent reports their valuation $\tilde{b}_i(Q)$. The regulator chooses $Q$ to maximise reported social surplus. Each agent pays a tax equal to the externality their report imposes on others: $t_i = \max_Q \sum_{j \ne i} \tilde{b}_j(Q) - c(Q) - [\sum_{j \ne i} \tilde{b}_j(Q^) - c(Q^) + b_i\text{'s own term}]$ (Clarke pivot rule). Each agent's net payoff equals reported social surplus up to a constant, so truth-telling is a dominant strategy. Result: efficient $Q$ achieved without knowing preferences.
  6. Clarke-Groves limitations. Generally not budget-balanced (sum of $t_i$ does not equal $c(Q^*)$); typically runs a surplus, so requires a residual claimant outside the mechanism. Also fails if preferences are not quasi-linear or if collusion among agents is possible. Practically used in some auction designs (VCG), not in routine public good provision.
  7. What policy actually uses. (a) Tax-financed provision with cost-benefit analysis based on willingness-to-pay surveys; (b) Concepts/Tradeable Permits for partially excludable cases; (c) clubs and matching grants for local public goods; (d) Tiebout sorting across jurisdictions.
  8. Conclusion. The theory traces a clean trade-off: Samuelson gives the target, Lindahl gives the price system, Clarke-Groves gives strategy-proofness, but no mechanism delivers all three of efficiency, strategy-proofness, and budget balance simultaneously (a corollary of Myerson-Satterthwaite). Real institutions accept some inefficiency in exchange for administrative tractability.
  • Appeared in: 2023 Q6, 2025 Q6.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Public Goods, Concepts/Rivalry and Excludability, Concepts/Free Rider Problem, Concepts/Voluntary Contribution Nash Equilibrium, Concepts/Samuelson Condition, Concepts/Lindahl Prices, Concepts/Clarke-Groves Mechanism, Concepts/Quasi-linear Utility.
Sub-question Type B.E.3: Policy instruments comparison for GHG or congestion
  • What it asks: Compare property rights / Coasean bargaining, Pigouvian taxes, tradeable permits, and command-and-control regulation for either greenhouse gas emissions or traffic congestion.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis: All four instruments can in principle achieve the socially efficient level of pollution / congestion, but they differ on three margins: (i) information requirements, (ii) distributional effects, (iii) robustness to uncertainty and incomplete enforcement. For greenhouse gases the most relevant trade-off is between price instruments (taxes) and quantity instruments (permits) under uncertainty. For congestion, simple Pigouvian tolling works best but distributional concerns dominate political feasibility.
  2. Frame the externality. Marginal abatement cost curve $MAC(q)$ (slopes up as abatement rises) and marginal damage curve $MD(q)$ (slopes up as emissions rise). Efficient emissions $q^$ where $MAC(q^) = MD(q^*)$. The four instruments differ in how they make agents internalise $MD$.
  3. Property rights and the Concepts/Coase Theorem. Assign emission rights to either polluter or victim, allow bargaining. Achieves $q^*$ with zero transaction costs. For GHG this fails because of the very large number of parties (every human on earth) and the resulting transaction costs. For congestion it works locally (HOA agreements, road clubs) but not at city scale.
  4. Pigouvian tax. Tax $t = MD(q^*)$ per unit emission. Polluters internalise: optimal abatement when $MAC(q) = t$. With heterogeneous polluters the tax automatically equalises marginal abatement costs across firms, so total abatement is achieved at minimum cost. Revenue can be recycled (carbon dividend, lower labour taxes). Uncertainty over $MAC$ leaves $q$ random but expected damages low.
  5. Tradeable permits. Issue $\bar{Q} = q^*$ permits, allow trade. In equilibrium permit price $p$ equals MAC for all firms, $p = MAC(\bar{Q}/N)$ for each firm. Equivalent to tax under certainty. Under uncertainty over MAC, permits fix $q$ but allow price to vary; tax fixes $p$ but allows $q$ to vary. The Concepts/Weitzman Prices vs Quantities result: prefer permits when MD slope $>$ MAC slope (worry about catastrophic damages), prefer tax when MAC slope $>$ MD slope (worry about cost spikes). GHG has roughly flat short-run MD curve (climate damage is in stock, not flow), so taxes look better in theory. Permits dominate politically because emitters keep the rents from grandfathered allocations.
  6. Command-and-control regulation. Uniform emission standards or technology mandates. Equalises emissions, not marginal costs, so generically more costly than tax or permits. Justified when monitoring of quantities is cheap (e.g. catalytic converters) but emissions monitoring expensive, or when corner solutions (zero emissions of a toxin) are wanted.
  7. Congestion specifics (if Q chooses traffic). Pigouvian toll $\tau = N b'(N)$ where $b' < 0$ is the marginal congestion externality. Implemented as London / Stockholm / Singapore congestion charges. Property rights work for tolls private roads but not public roads. Permits (rationing license plates by day) used in Mexico City and Beijing but distort towards multi-car households. Regulation (low-emission zones) is technology-targeted, complementary to tolls.
  8. Distributional caveat. Carbon taxes and congestion charges are regressive in incidence but progressive when revenues are recycled per capita. Permit grandfathering transfers rents to incumbents.
  9. Conclusion. Under standard assumptions and certainty all four instruments achieve $q^*$. Under uncertainty the price-quantity distinction is the live margin: GHG favours taxes on Weitzman grounds, congestion favours tolls. Regulation is a second-best when monitoring is asymmetric. Coasean bargaining is theoretically clean but practically null for global externalities.
  • Appeared in: 2024 Q6.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Pigouvian Tax, Concepts/Tradeable Permits, Concepts/Coase Theorem, Concepts/Weitzman Prices vs Quantities, Concepts/Externality Internalisation, Concepts/Marginal Private vs Social Benefit, Concepts/Tragedy of the Commons.

Sub-question Type B.L.1: Two colleges sharing a library, full Nash + Samuelson + Lindahl
  • What it asks: Two colleges have Cobb-Douglas log utility over private good $x_i$ and shared books $B$. Find (a) the Concepts/Voluntary Contribution Nash Equilibrium, (b) the Pareto efficient joint provision using the Concepts/Samuelson Condition, (c) the Lindahl prices supporting efficiency and comment on practical implementation (strategy-proofness, subsidy interpretation).
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Set up. Two colleges $i = 1, 2$ with endowment $w_i$, utility $u_i(x_i, B) = \alpha_i \ln x_i + \beta_i \ln B$ where $B = b_1 + b_2$ is total books, $b_i$ is college $i$'s contribution at unit price (so $x_i + b_i = w_i$). Often $\alpha_i, \beta_i$ are symmetric.
  2. (a) Nash best response. College $i$ takes $b_j$ as given, maximises $\alpha_i \ln(w_i - b_i) + \beta_i \ln(b_i + b_j)$ in $b_i \ge 0$. FOC: $-\alpha_i/(w_i - b_i) + \beta_i/(b_i + b_j) = 0$. Rearrange: $\beta_i(w_i - b_i) = \alpha_i(b_i + b_j)$, so $b_i = [\beta_i w_i - \alpha_i b_j]/(\alpha_i + \beta_i)$.
  3. Symmetric NE. With $\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \alpha$, $\beta_1 = \beta_2 = \beta$, $w_1 = w_2 = w$: substitute $b_1 = b_2 = b$: $b = [\beta w - \alpha b]/(\alpha + \beta)$, so $b(\alpha + \beta) + \alpha b = \beta w$, giving $b = \beta w/(2\alpha + \beta)$. Total $B^{VC} = 2\beta w/(2\alpha + \beta)$.
  4. (b) Samuelson and the Pareto efficient $B^$. Planner maximises $\sum_i u_i$ subject to $\sum_i x_i + B = 2w$. With log utility the Samuelson condition is $\sum_i \partial u_i/\partial B \div \partial u_i/\partial x_i = 1$, i.e. $\sum_i \beta_i x_i / (\alpha_i B) = 1$. With symmetry: $2 \beta x / (\alpha B) = 1$, so $\alpha B = 2 \beta x$, combined with $2x + B = 2w$ gives $B^ = 2 \beta w / (\alpha + \beta)$.
  5. Verify the wedge. $B^{VC}/B^* = (2\beta w/(2\alpha + \beta))/(2 \beta w/(\alpha + \beta)) = (\alpha + \beta)/(2\alpha + \beta) < 1$. Free-riding confirmed.
  6. (c) Lindahl prices. Personalised prices $p_1, p_2$ with $p_1 + p_2 = 1$ (the marginal cost of a book). College $i$'s demand at price $p_i$: $b_i^D(p_i)$ such that the agent demands the same total $B$. From log utility, $i$'s demand is $\beta_i (w_i)/(\alpha_i p_i + \beta_i)$... Carefully: $i$ buys $B$ units at $p_i$ each, so budget $x_i + p_i B = w_i$, utility $\alpha_i \ln(w_i - p_i B) + \beta_i \ln B$. FOC: $\beta_i/B = \alpha_i p_i /(w_i - p_i B)$, so $\beta_i(w_i - p_i B) = \alpha_i p_i B$, giving $B = \beta_i w_i / [p_i (\alpha_i + \beta_i)]$.
  7. Demand equality plus budget. Equating $B_1^D = B_2^D = B^L$ with $\alpha_i = \alpha$, $\beta_i = \beta$ symmetric: $B^L = \beta w/[p_i(\alpha + \beta)]$ for both, so $p_1 = p_2 = 1/2$. Each college pays half. Substitute: $B^L = \beta w/[(1/2)(\alpha + \beta)] = 2\beta w/(\alpha + \beta) = B^*$. So Lindahl decentralises the Samuelson optimum.
  8. Subsidy interpretation. If the colleges face market price 1 for books but the regulator subsidises so each pays $p_i = 1/2$, financed by a lump-sum tax $T_i$ equal to the subsidy bill, the outcome is efficient. With identical colleges this collapses to the equal-share rule.
  9. Strategy-proofness critique. Each college has incentive to understate $\beta_i$ to push their Lindahl price down. Truthful revelation requires a Clarke-Groves variant. In a closed-form essay closer, mention that this is why real consortium libraries use formula-based contributions (FTE-weighted) rather than direct preference elicitation.
  • Appeared in: 2022 Q10.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Voluntary Contribution Nash Equilibrium, Concepts/Samuelson Condition, Concepts/Lindahl Prices, Concepts/Free Rider Problem, Concepts/Public Goods, Concepts/Clarke-Groves Mechanism.
How to recognise the format on the day

Part A externality / public good questions in Core Micro almost always lead with one of three set-ups: a discrete public good (flood defence, library, vaccine), a common pool resource (road, fishery), or a bilateral negative externality (Alesha and Bob, polluter and victim). Spotting the set-up tells you which model to deploy in the first 30 seconds:

  • If the prompt mentions "n residents" or "voluntary contributions" and the good is non-rival, expect Concepts/Voluntary Contribution Nash Equilibrium in one part and Concepts/Samuelson Condition in another (2014 Q5, 2024 Q2). The voluntary-contribution equilibrium will be independent of $n$ under quasi-linear preferences, the social optimum scales linearly in $n$. Show the wedge widening.
  • If the prompt mentions "free entry," "congestion," or a rival but non-excludable good, expect Concepts/Tragedy of the Commons with $b(N^e) = \bar{u}$ for the equilibrium and $b(N^) + N^ b'(N^) = \bar{u}$ for the optimum (2015 Q5). The Pigouvian toll is $\tau = -N^ b'(N^*)$.
  • If the prompt is bilateral ("Alesha generates an externality on Bob") with Concepts/Quasi-linear Utility, expect to compute private optimum, social optimum, Concepts/Pigouvian Tax (= marginal damage at optimum), and a compensating lump-sum transfer (2023 Q1).
  • If the prompt has quadratic profit and quadratic loss with random shocks, expect a Concepts/Weitzman Prices vs Quantities comparison and a robustness claim about the tax (2025 Q2). Watch for the regulator knowing only the slope, not the level, of one curve.

Part B essays follow three patterns:

  • A canonical "explain the theory of public goods" essay (2023 Q6, 2025 Q6). Walk through definition, free-rider problem, Samuelson, Lindahl, Clarke-Groves with one practical sentence on each. Aim for the ladder of decreasing practicality at the end.
  • A policy instrument comparison (2024 Q6). Use the four-way comparison: property rights, taxes, permits, regulation. The marginal point is usually Weitzman under uncertainty.
  • A contrarian-claim essay (2022 Q6 on positive externalities). Engage with the claim, present the formal symmetry argument, then defend the second-best caveats. Top scripts always concede where the claim has merit before rejecting it overall.

Part B long-answer problems in this topic are always a structured public-good calculation with three parts: Nash, Samuelson, Lindahl, sometimes a fourth on Clarke-Groves or a subsidy. The 2022 Q10 college library is the canonical template. If you see Cobb-Douglas log preferences over a private good and a shared good, the algebra is almost mechanical: Nash gives a smaller $B$ than Samuelson by a known ratio, Lindahl prices split as the ratio of weights $\beta_i / (\alpha_i + \beta_i)$ (or in the symmetric case, $1/2$ each).

Three minute-savers on the day:

  1. In voluntary contribution problems with identical agents and quasi-linear preferences, write down "Q^{VC} is independent of n" before doing any algebra; this is worth a free sentence in part (d) of most A questions.
  2. In Samuelson derivations, draw the vertical-sum diagram (MB_1 stacked on MB_2 against MC). The diagram is worth marks and forces you to write the condition correctly.
  3. In Pigouvian tax problems, always check that the tax revenue $t^ \cdot x^$ is not the same as total external damage $h(x^*)$. The two coincide only when $h$ is linear, and confusing them is the most common error.

3 Pigouvian Tax Tier 1

1. Intuition

A per-unit tax on an externality-producing activity set equal to the marginal external cost at the efficient quantity. Private agents then face the social marginal cost and choose the socially optimal activity level.

Pigouvian taxes implement first-best efficiency under certainty. Their main practical challenge is that the regulator needs to know the marginal external cost function, and they may need to vary with output. Cap-and-trade is the dual quantity instrument.

2. Formal mathematics

A Pigouvian tax is a per-unit levy on an activity that generates a negative externality, set equal to the marginal external damage at the social optimum. It internalises the externality by aligning private with social marginal cost.

Setup: a competitive industry produces $q$ at private marginal cost $c'(q)$, facing inverse demand $p(q)$. Production imposes external damage $d(q)$ with $d'(q) > 0$. Social welfare is $W(q) = \int_0^q p(s)\,ds - c(q) - d(q)$.

  1. Private equilibrium without intervention: firms set $p = c'(q)$, giving $q^M$ where $p(q^M) = c'(q^M)$.
  2. Social optimum: $W'(q^*) = 0$ gives $p(q^*) = c'(q^*) + d'(q^*)$.
  3. Since $d' > 0$ and $p$ is downward-sloping, $q^* < q^M$. Unregulated output is too high.
  4. Impose a per-unit tax $t$ on producers. The firm now sets $p = c'(q) + t$. To replicate $q^*$, choose $t^* = d'(q^*)$.
  5. At $t^*$, private FOC becomes $p(q^*) = c'(q^*) + d'(q^*)$, matching the social FOC. The market decentralises the optimum.

The deadweight loss eliminated is the area between the social marginal cost curve and the demand curve, between $q^*$ and $q^M$: $DWL = \int_{q^*}^{q^M} [c'(q) + d'(q) - p(q)]\,dq$. Tax revenue $t^* q^*$ flows to the government and can be rebated lump-sum without distorting the corrected allocation, because labour supply effects are zero in the partial-equilibrium frame.

Equivalence with quantity instruments (Weitzman 1974): under certainty, a Pigouvian tax $t^*$ and a tradeable permit cap $\bar Q = q^*$ produce the same allocation. Under uncertainty about marginal abatement cost, the welfare ranking depends on the relative slopes of marginal damage and marginal cost: taxes are preferred when MD is flatter than MC, permits when steeper.

Key qualifications. (i) The tax must equal $d'(q^*)$, not $d'(q^M)$. Setting the tax at the marginal damage observed at the distorted equilibrium over-corrects. (ii) If the externality varies by source (a power plant near a city versus a remote one), a uniform tax is second-best; first-best requires differentiated rates. (iii) Coase (1960) noted that with low transaction costs and well-defined property rights, parties can bargain to the efficient outcome without government intervention, making the Pigouvian instrument unnecessary.

References: Micro2025.pdf Topic 3 Lecture 2; Hindriks and Myles, Intermediate Public Economics, Ch. 7; Varian Ch. 24.

3. Graphical illustration

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • London Congestion Charge (2003 onward): a flat daily fee for driving in central London, justified as a Pigouvian instrument against road congestion and local air pollution. Leape (2006) estimated traffic fell 18% and welfare rose, though the flat structure misses the time-varying marginal damage that a first-best charge would track.
  • EU Emissions Trading System (ETS): a cap-and-trade scheme for $\mathrm{CO}_2$. By Weitzman logic, permits dominate when marginal damage is steep (climate tipping points), which is the standard defence for quantity instruments in climate policy.
  • US federal gasoline tax (18.4 cents per gallon since 1993): far below the Parry-Small (2005) estimate of optimal external cost (about $1 per gallon), so it is Pigouvian in form but under-set in level.
  • Evaluation move: Kate Doornik rewards essays that distinguish setting $t = d'(q^*)$ from $t = d'(q^M)$. The latter is the common student error and leads to over-correction. Pinning the tax to the optimal quantity, not the distorted one, is the substantive point.
  • Evaluation move: raise the double-dividend hypothesis (Bovenberg and de Mooij 1994). Whether revenue recycling can eliminate other distortionary taxes depends on labour-market interactions ignored in the partial-equilibrium diagram.
  • Standard limitation: the regulator must observe $d'(q^*)$. For diffuse externalities (greenhouse gases) the marginal damage estimates span an order of magnitude. Coase argued that for small-numbers externalities, bargaining outperforms taxation.
  • Cross-reference: see [[Concepts/Externality]] for the underlying market failure and [[Concepts/Coase Theorem]] for the property-rights alternative.
3 Samuelson Condition Tier 1

1. Intuition

The Pareto-efficient quantity of a public good satisfies $\sum_i MRS_i = MRT$. In words: the sum of marginal willingness-to-pay across all consumers equals the marginal cost of provision. This is the public-good analogue of the private-good condition MRS = MRT.

Why sum and not equate? With a public good, every consumer enjoys every unit, so the social marginal benefit at any quantity is the vertical sum of individual marginal benefits, not the horizontal sum that you would use for a private good.

2. Formal mathematics

The Samuelson condition (Samuelson 1954) characterises the Pareto-efficient quantity of a pure public good. With $I$ consumers, one private good $x$ and one public good $G$, produced from a single resource with marginal rate of transformation $MRT_{Gx}$, efficiency requires:

$$\sum_{i=1}^I MRS^i_{Gx} = MRT_{Gx}.$$

The right-hand side is the social marginal cost of one more unit of $G$ (in private-good units). The left-hand side is the sum of marginal willingness to pay across consumers, summed because every consumer enjoys the same $G$ simultaneously: public goods are non-rival.

Contrast with a private good $x$, where efficiency requires $MRS^i_{xy} = MRT_{xy}$ for each $i$ separately. Private goods get horizontally summed demand; public goods get vertically summed willingness to pay.

  1. Each consumer has $u_i(x_i, G)$, with feasibility $\sum_i x_i + c(G) = \Omega$ where $\Omega$ is the aggregate resource endowment.
  2. Pareto problem: $\max u_1(x_1, G)$ subject to $u_i(x_i, G) \geq \bar u_i$ for $i \geq 2$ and the resource constraint, with multipliers $\lambda_i$ and $\mu$.
  3. FOC in $x_i$: $\partial u_i / \partial x_i = \mu$ for all $i$. So $\mu$ is the common shadow value of the private good.
  4. FOC in $G$: $\sum_i \lambda_i (\partial u_i / \partial G) = \mu c'(G)$, with $\lambda_1 = 1$.
  5. Divide the $G$-FOC by $\mu$ and use $\lambda_i = \mu / (\partial u_i / \partial x_i)$ implied by the $x_i$-FOCs: $\sum_i (\partial u_i/\partial G)/(\partial u_i / \partial x_i) = c'(G)$, which is $\sum_i MRS^i_{Gx} = MRT_{Gx}$.

Comparison with private provision. If each consumer chooses contribution $g_i$ taking others' contributions as given, the Nash FOC is $MRS^i = c'(G)$ for each contributor. Adding these across contributors gives a sum on the left, but each contributor sets her own MRS equal to MC, not the sum. Result: $G^{Nash} < G^*$, the standard free-rider underprovision.

With quasi-linear preferences $u_i = v_i(G) + x_i$, the Samuelson condition simplifies to $\sum_i v_i'(G^*) = c'(G^*)$, which is the FOC used in essay computations. The efficient $G^*$ is independent of the distribution of wealth, a transferable-utility feature inherited from quasi-linearity.

References: Micro2025.pdf Topic 3 Lecture 3; Hindriks and Myles Ch. 6; Varian Ch. 23.

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • National defence is the textbook pure public good: non-rival (my protection does not diminish yours) and non-excludable. Defence budgets are set by political process, an attempt at implementing the Samuelson condition through voting rather than markets.
  • Open-source software (Linux kernel, Wikipedia) is non-rival in use. Empirical work by Lerner and Tirole estimates that voluntary contributions implement a fraction of the Samuelson optimum, with the gap explained by reputational and intrinsic motives, not pure free-riding.
  • Scientific research basic findings: a proof or a measured constant is non-rival once published. Government R&D subsidies (UKRI, NSF) are a partial Samuelson-style correction.
  • Evaluation move: the practical critique is that the planner cannot observe each $MRS_i$ directly. This motivates the Clarke-Groves mechanism, which makes truth-telling a dominant strategy at the cost of failing budget balance (Green and Laffont 1979).
  • Evaluation move: distinguish pure public goods (national defence) from impure ones (congestible roads, club goods). The Samuelson condition needs modification when there is rivalry at the margin; see Buchanan (1965) on clubs.
  • Standard limitation: Bergstrom, Blume, Varian (1986) show that the underprovision result depends on linear contribution technology. With increasing returns to private contribution, Nash equilibria can support corner solutions where a single agent provides everything.

3 Lindahl Prices Tier 2

1. Intuition

Each consumer faces a personalised price for the public good equal to their marginal willingness-to-pay at the optimum. With Lindahl prices, everyone agrees on the same quantity (the Samuelson optimum), and each pays their share of marginal cost.

Lindahl pricing decentralises the public-good problem the way ordinary prices decentralise private-good provision. The practical problem is that the planner does not know preferences, and consumers have no incentive to reveal them truthfully.

2. Formal mathematics

Lindahl prices (Lindahl 1919) are personalised prices for a public good that decentralise the Samuelson optimum. Each consumer $i$ faces her own price per unit of $G$, denoted $\tau_i$, with $\sum_i \tau_i = c'(G^*)$. Given $\tau_i$, each consumer chooses her preferred $G$, and at a Lindahl equilibrium all consumers demand the same quantity, which equals the efficient $G^*$.

Formally, a Lindahl equilibrium is $(\tau_1^*, \dots, \tau_I^*, G^*)$ such that:

  1. $G^* \in \arg\max_G \{u_i(\omega_i - \tau_i^* G, G)\}$ for every $i$. Each consumer, taking her own personalised price as given, prefers exactly $G^*$.
  2. $\sum_i \tau_i^* = c'(G^*)$. The personalised prices sum to marginal cost, so the production side breaks even.

Derivation in the quasi-linear case $u_i = v_i(G) + x_i$ with $x_i = \omega_i - \tau_i G$:

  1. Consumer $i$'s problem: $\max_G v_i(G) - \tau_i G$. FOC: $v_i'(G) = \tau_i$.
  2. For all consumers to demand the same $G$, set $\tau_i^* = v_i'(G^*)$. The price equals the marginal willingness to pay.
  3. Producer side: $\sum_i \tau_i^* = \sum_i v_i'(G^*) = c'(G^*)$ by the Samuelson condition.
  4. Solving these jointly gives the efficient $G^*$ and a personalised price for each consumer equal to her marginal benefit at the optimum.

So Lindahl pricing is the analogue of Walrasian pricing for public goods. Just as a competitive equilibrium decentralises Pareto efficiency for private goods via a common price, a Lindahl equilibrium decentralises Samuelson efficiency via personalised prices.

Existence and welfare. Foley (1970) showed that under standard assumptions (convex preferences, continuous utilities) a Lindahl equilibrium exists. Both Welfare Theorems extend: every Lindahl equilibrium is Pareto efficient, and any efficient public-good allocation can be supported as a Lindahl equilibrium after lump-sum redistribution of the private good.

The implementation problem. The mechanism requires the planner to know each $v_i'$ to set $\tau_i^*$. Consumers have an incentive to misreport: if I announce a low marginal benefit, my price is reduced while $G$ is barely affected. The Lindahl equilibrium is not strategy-proof. This is the central practical objection and motivates the demand-revelation mechanisms (Clarke-Groves) discussed in Topic 3 Lecture 4.

References: Micro2025.pdf Topic 3 Lecture 3; Hindriks and Myles Ch. 6.4; Mas-Colell Ch. 16.

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • Property tax differentials: Tiebout (1956) sorting across local jurisdictions is sometimes interpreted as approximate Lindahl pricing for local public goods (schools, parks). Households with stronger preference for schools self-select into higher-tax districts.
  • Voluntary club fees: members of professional bodies (RSA, BMA) pay different rates based on income or grade, an admittedly crude Lindahl analogue when membership generates partial public-good benefits within the profession.
  • Carbon contribution funds: some firms offer customers a per-tonne carbon offset price calibrated to individual willingness to pay, a private experiment in personalised pricing of a public bad.
  • Evaluation move: stress that Lindahl prices are a conceptual not implementable solution. Kate Doornik tends to reward essays that note the strategic misreporting problem: announcing $v_i' = 0$ gets the consumer $G^*$ for free if everyone else reports truthfully.
  • Evaluation move: Lindahl equilibrium pairs Pareto efficiency with proportional cost sharing by marginal benefit, which has a fairness appeal absent from Samuelson. This is the link to the Lindahl-as-equity argument in Foley (1970).
  • Standard limitation: existence relies on convex preferences and continuous utilities; with non-convexities (lumpy public projects like a bridge), Lindahl equilibrium may not exist and integer programming approaches are needed.
  • Cross-reference: see [[Concepts/Samuelson Condition]] for the efficiency condition Lindahl pricing implements, and [[Concepts/Mechanism Design]] for the strategy-proof alternatives that respond to the implementation critique.

Sub-question Type A.1: Find all pure-strategy Nash equilibria of a 2x2 game (and identify dominance relations)
  • What it asks: Given a 2x2 (or occasionally 2x3) bimatrix, list every pure-strategy NE. Often paired with a request to check for dominant strategies, iteratively eliminate dominated strategies, or comment on weak vs strict dominance.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Write the bimatrix in standard form, Row payoff first then Column payoff.
  2. Check dominance first. For each player, ask: is there a strategy whose payoff is strictly higher than another strategy's payoff for every choice by the opponent? If yes, eliminate the strictly dominated strategy. Repeat. Iterated strict dominance preserves the NE set.
  3. Best-response method for any leftover game. For each Column strategy, underline Row's highest payoff. For each Row strategy, underline Column's highest payoff. A cell with both payoffs underlined is a pure-strategy NE.
  4. State each pure NE as a strategy profile and quote both players' equilibrium payoffs.
  5. If asked about weak dominance: note that weak dominance can eliminate Nash equilibria, so iterated weak dominance is order-dependent and is a less safe solution concept.

Sub-question Type A.2: Find the mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium of a 2x2 game
  • What it asks: Compute the mixed NE when no pure NE exists, or when the question asks for "all" NE. Often paired with computing expected equilibrium payoffs.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Let Row play Top with probability $p$, Bottom with probability $1-p$. Let Column play Left with probability $q$, Right with probability $1-q$.
  2. Indifference principle: in any totally mixed NE, each player must be indifferent between the strategies in their support. So I set $u_{\text{Row}}(\text{Top} \mid q) = u_{\text{Row}}(\text{Bottom} \mid q)$ and solve for $q$. Then $u_{\text{Col}}(\text{Left} \mid p) = u_{\text{Col}}(\text{Right} \mid p)$ and solve for $p$.
  3. Key intuition to write down: player 1's mixing probability is determined by making player 2 indifferent, not by making player 1 indifferent. This is the most common mistake.
  4. Substitute the equilibrium probabilities back into either pure-strategy payoff to get each player's expected equilibrium payoff. Both expressions must give the same number (a sanity check).
  5. If the question asks "find all NE", list pure NEs from A.1 first and then add the mixed NE.

Sub-question Type A.3: Define Nash equilibrium formally and discuss its predictive content
  • What it asks: A bookwork prompt embedded in a longer problem. Sometimes paired with: when does NE coincide with dominance solvability, when does dominance solvability fail to pick a NE, why is NE useful for prediction.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. State the formal definition: a strategy profile $(\sigma_1^, \ldots, \sigma_n^)$ is a Nash equilibrium if for every player $i$ and every alternative strategy $\sigma_i$, $u_i(\sigma_i^, \sigma_{-i}^) \ge u_i(\sigma_i, \sigma_{-i}^*)$.
  2. Restate in words: no player can strictly gain by unilaterally deviating, holding others' strategies fixed.
  3. Note that strictly dominant-strategy equilibrium implies NE, but not the converse. Iterated strict dominance preserves NE; weak dominance does not.
  4. For "useful for prediction" discussion: NE is necessary for any self-enforcing rational play, but coordination games show NE need not be unique, so it is not always sufficient for sharp prediction. Refinements (SPE, trembling-hand, risk-dominance) help in specific contexts.
  5. If asked when dominance suffices: dominance-solvable games (like Prisoner's Dilemma) yield a unique prediction without invoking NE refinements.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2018 Q2 (preamble asks for the NE definition)
  • 2019 Q2 (dominance vs NE relationships and predictive usefulness)

Sub-question Type A.4: Solve homogeneous-product Bertrand duopoly (price equals marginal cost)
  • What it asks: Two firms set prices simultaneously for an identical good, marginal cost $c$. Show the unique pure-strategy NE has $p_1 = p_2 = c$. Often a stepping stone to a discussion of why this "Bertrand paradox" is unrealistic and what extensions restore positive margins.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. State the demand-allocation rule: all consumers buy from the lower-priced firm, demand is split equally if $p_1 = p_2$.
  2. Argue $p_i < c$ is dominated (negative profit if any positive sales).
  3. Argue $p_i > c$ with $p_j \ge p_i$ is not best response: firm $j$ can undercut by $\epsilon$ and capture the entire market at a still-positive margin.
  4. The only profile with no profitable deviation is $p_1 = p_2 = c$, both earning zero profit.
  5. State the policy implication: two firms suffice to mimic a perfectly competitive price.
  6. Note the assumptions that drive the result: identical product, unlimited capacity, perfect information, no search costs, one-shot interaction. Relaxing any one restores positive margins.

Sub-question Type A.5: Solve differentiated Bertrand for the symmetric equilibrium
  • What it asks: Linear demand of the form $q_i = \alpha - \beta p_i + \gamma p_j$, marginal cost $c$. Derive best-response functions and find the symmetric NE $(p^, p^)$. Often paired with: comparative statics in $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $c$, and a merger-to-monopoly comparison.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Write firm $i$'s profit $\pi_i = (p_i - c)(\alpha - \beta p_i + \gamma p_j)$.
  2. FOC with respect to $p_i$: $\alpha - 2\beta p_i + \gamma p_j + \beta c = 0$, giving the best response $p_i(p_j) = \frac{\alpha + \beta c + \gamma p_j}{2\beta}$.
  3. Impose symmetry $p_i = p_j = p^$ and solve: $p^ = \frac{\alpha + \beta c}{2\beta - \gamma}$.
  4. Check that $\gamma < 2\beta$ for an interior solution and for stability of the best-response system.
  5. Comparative statics: $p^$ rises in $\alpha$ (demand size), in $c$ (cost passthrough), and in $\gamma$ (less substitutability via cross-price effect). $p^$ falls in $\beta$ (own-price elasticity dominates).
  6. Merger to monopoly: after merger the firm internalises the cross-price effect. Set common $p$ to maximise total profit $2(p-c)(\alpha - (\beta - \gamma) p)$. Monopoly price $p^M = \frac{\alpha + (\beta - \gamma) c}{2(\beta - \gamma)}$. Compare with $p^$: $p^M > p^$ unless costs fall.

Sub-question Type A.6: Cournot duopoly best-response and symmetric equilibrium
  • What it asks: Linear inverse demand $p = a - b(q_1 + q_2)$, constant marginal cost $c$. Derive each firm's best-response and the symmetric NE quantities, price, and profits.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Profit $\pi_i = (a - b(q_i + q_j))q_i - c q_i$.
  2. FOC: $a - 2 b q_i - b q_j - c = 0$, best response $q_i(q_j) = \frac{a - c}{2b} - \frac{q_j}{2}$.
  3. Symmetric NE $q_1 = q_2 = q^$: $q^ = \frac{a - c}{3 b}$.
  4. Total output $Q^ = \frac{2(a-c)}{3b}$, equilibrium price $p^ = \frac{a + 2 c}{3}$, per-firm profit $\pi^* = \frac{(a-c)^2}{9 b}$.
  5. Compare with monopoly ($q^M = \frac{a-c}{2b}$, $p^M = \frac{a+c}{2}$, $\pi^M = \frac{(a-c)^2}{4 b}$) and perfect competition ($p = c$). Cournot price sits strictly between the two.
  6. If costs are asymmetric ($c_1 \ne c_2$), redo the FOCs separately. The lower-cost firm produces more.

Sub-question Type A.7: Characterise the SPE of a finite sequential game by backward induction
  • What it asks: Either an extensive-form tree or a simultaneous game played sequentially. Find the SPE strategy profile and the equilibrium path.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Identify the proper subgames, starting from the smallest (latest-move) subgames at the leaves.
  2. In each smallest subgame, solve for the optimal action by the player who moves. Record this as that player's SPE behaviour at the corresponding information set.
  3. Replace the subgame with the resulting payoff vector and move one stage up. Repeat.
  4. After folding the tree all the way back to the root, the equilibrium path is the sequence of optimal actions traced from the root.
  5. The SPE specifies actions at every node, including off-path nodes. This is the difference from a NE of the reduced strategic form, where some off-path threats may not be credible.
  6. Compare with the NE of the simultaneous-move version: a sequential game's SPE is a NE of the strategic form, but not every NE of the strategic form is an SPE (some rely on incredible threats).

Sub-question Type A.8: Define SPE of a repeated game and sustain cooperation via grim trigger
  • What it asks: For an infinitely repeated stage game, find the critical discount factor $\delta^*$ such that the grim-trigger strategy supports the cooperative outcome as an SPE. Often phrased as "show that (Coop, Coop) is sustainable for $\delta$ large enough".
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. State the grim-trigger strategy: play the cooperative action in every period unless any deviation has occurred in any previous period; if any deviation has occurred, play the static-NE (punishment) action forever.
  2. Cooperation payoff stream: $V^C = \frac{u^C}{1 - \delta}$ where $u^C$ is the per-period cooperative payoff.
  3. Deviation payoff stream: deviate optimally today and earn $u^D$, then punished at static-NE payoff $u^N$ forever: $V^D = u^D + \frac{\delta u^N}{1 - \delta}$.
  4. No-deviation condition: $V^C \ge V^D$. Rearrange to $\delta \ge \frac{u^D - u^C}{u^D - u^N} \equiv \delta^*$.
  5. State $\delta^*$ explicitly and interpret: cooperation is sustainable when the per-period gain from deviation is small relative to the long-run loss of punishment.
  6. SPE check: the punishment phase prescribes the static NE, which is a NE of the stage game, so playing it forever is itself an SPE of the continuation. This makes the grim-trigger strategy itself an SPE, not merely a NE.

  • 2014 Q3(c)(d) (SPE of repeated arms race, sustaining (No, No) using MSE as punishment)
  • 2020 Q2(b)(c) (define SPE of repeated game; sustain (M, M) for large $\delta$)
  • 2025 Q3(c) (SPE strategies sustaining the monopoly-price collusive outcome in repeated Bertrand, find critical $\delta$)

Sub-question Type A.9: Williamson trade-off, merger from duopoly to monopoly
  • What it asks: A merger between two firms creates market power (deadweight loss) but may also create cost synergies (productive efficiency gain). Decide whether the merger raises social welfare, often with a diagram.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Draw the demand curve. Mark pre-merger price (duopoly $p^D$) and quantity $Q^D$, and pre-merger marginal cost $c^D$.
  2. Mark post-merger price $p^M$ (monopoly), quantity $Q^M$, and post-merger marginal cost $c^M$ (lower because of synergies).
  3. Welfare-reducing area: the deadweight loss triangle from raising price from $p^D$ to $p^M$, evaluated at the pre-merger cost.
  4. Welfare-enhancing area: the rectangle of cost savings $(c^D - c^M) \cdot Q^M$.
  5. Williamson trade-off: the merger raises total surplus iff the cost-saving rectangle exceeds the DWL triangle. The rectangle is first-order in the cost reduction while the triangle is second-order in the price increase, so even modest synergies often dominate.
  6. Consumer surplus check: consumers gain only if the price actually falls, which requires the cost reduction to be large enough that monopoly $p^M(c^M) < p^D$. The standard Williamson logic favours producers, so the regulator must care about total surplus rather than only consumer surplus for the merger to be approved.

  • 2015 Q2(d) (monopoly price after merger)
  • 2017 Q2 (full Williamson trade-off prompt with diagram)
  • 2023 Q2(c) (post-merger monopoly with cost reduction, whether to allow)

Part B: essay-style questions
Sub-question Type B.E.1: Oligopoly theory plus merger essay (when should a duopoly merger be allowed?)
  • What it asks: Use oligopoly theory (Cournot, Bertrand, differentiated Bertrand, Williamson) to analyse whether a proposed two-to-one merger should be permitted, and explain how the conclusion changes if there are more than two initial firms.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis: a duopoly merger generates market power that raises price and reduces consumer surplus, but it can also generate cost synergies. The regulator's decision depends on (i) the magnitude of synergies relative to deadweight loss, (ii) the standard of welfare (consumer vs total surplus), (iii) the post-merger market structure, and (iv) the threat of entry. With more than two initial firms, residual competition softens the price effect, making mergers more often welfare-improving.
  2. Static benchmark in homogeneous Bertrand: duopoly already yields $p = c$, so any merger to monopoly raises price by the full monopoly-vs-competition gap. This is the worst case for consumers and only synergies large enough to push $p^M(c^M)$ below $c$ would protect them. Regulators almost always block such mergers.
  3. Static benchmark in differentiated Bertrand or Cournot: duopoly profits and prices already lie above marginal cost. The price jump from merger is smaller. Use a numerical example (e.g., the 2023 Q2 setup) to compute pre- and post-merger price changes and the implied welfare numbers.
  4. The Williamson trade-off formally: cost savings rectangle vs DWL triangle (cite Concepts/Williamson Trade-off). Draw the diagram and state the result: even modest synergies often suffice when starting from a high price (small triangle) and a large output (big rectangle).
  5. Consumer-surplus vs total-surplus standards: UK CMA and EU DG-COMP use a consumer-welfare standard, which is harder for merging firms to satisfy. Total surplus is easier because it counts producer gains.
  6. Three or more firms case: in Cournot oligopoly with $n$ firms and symmetric cost $c$, equilibrium price is $p_n = \frac{a + n c}{n + 1}$. A 2-to-1 reduction of firms (e.g., $n = 4 \to 3$) raises price by a fraction roughly $\frac{1}{(n+1)(n)}$ times the gap to monopoly. The effect shrinks rapidly in $n$. Remaining competitors discipline the merged firm.
  7. Dynamic considerations: entry, repeated-game collusion, and innovation. With easy entry, even a 2-to-1 merger may be approved because the threat of entry pins price near $p^D$. With high entry barriers, collusion among the post-merger smaller set of firms becomes easier (fewer firms means easier monitoring), strengthening the regulator's case to block.
  8. Conclusion: the duopoly-to-monopoly case is the hardest for the merging firms because no residual competition remains. As $n$ rises, the welfare cost shrinks and the synergies case becomes easier. Frame the conclusion as a function of $n$, of the standard of welfare, and of entry conditions.
  • Appeared in: 2022 Q7.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Williamson Trade-off, Concepts/Merger Analysis, Concepts/Cournot Duopoly, Concepts/Bertrand Duopoly, Concepts/Differentiated Bertrand, Concepts/Entry Deterrence.
Sub-question Type B.E.2: Collusion sustainability essay, with multiplicity of equilibria
  • What it asks: Use repeated-game theory to identify the factors that determine whether collusion is sustainable, and discuss whether the multiplicity of equilibria identified by the folk theorem is a problem for the theory's predictive power.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis: collusion is sustainable when each firm's short-term gain from cheating is dominated by the long-term value of cooperation, weighted by the discount factor. The folk theorem confirms that any individually rational payoff vector is sustainable for $\delta$ large enough, which is a virtue (rationalises observed collusion) and a vice (no sharp prediction). The multiplicity is partly tamed by focal points, antitrust enforcement, and equilibrium selection.
  2. The grim-trigger benchmark: derive the critical $\delta^$ for the symmetric duopoly using either Bertrand or Cournot stage game (cite Concepts/Grim Trigger Strategy and Concepts/Critical Discount Factor). Present $\delta^ = \frac{u^D - u^C}{u^D - u^N}$.
  3. Factors raising $\delta^*$ (making collusion harder): low patience (high real interest rate), low monitoring quality (long detection lag), large number of firms (each firm's punishment loss is smaller while deviation gain is the full monopoly payoff), demand shocks (Green-Porter style price wars when monitoring is imperfect), asymmetric firms (free-riding incentives differ).
  4. Factors lowering $\delta^*$ (making collusion easier): stable market, transparent prices, frequent interaction, capacity constraints (cap the deviation profit), homogeneous products with public prices.
  5. The folk theorem and multiplicity: for $\delta \to 1$, the set of SPE payoffs equals the set of feasible individually rational payoffs (cite Concepts/Folk Theorem). This means cooperation, partial cooperation, and even alternation between firms can all be sustained.
  6. Is multiplicity a problem? Arguments for "yes": no point prediction, hard to falsify, hard for the regulator to identify which collusive outcome to deter. Arguments for "no": (a) the folk theorem matches the empirical reality of cartels picking focal prices (often the joint-profit-maximising one), (b) standard refinements like renegotiation-proofness or stationary symmetric equilibria cut the equilibrium set, (c) the theorem's value is rationalising collusion, not predicting its exact form.
  7. Application to competition policy: the antitrust authority's task is to deter the cooperative SPE, not the punishment SPE. Leniency programmes raise the deviation payoff $u^D$ (by reducing the punishment's expected severity to a deviator who confesses), which raises $\delta^*$ and destabilises collusion. This is a concrete policy use of the theory despite the multiplicity.
  8. Conclusion: multiplicity is a real cost in terms of sharp prediction but not a fatal one. The theory still pins down the comparative statics that drive collusion, and policy interventions can be evaluated by how they shift $\delta^*$ even without selecting a unique equilibrium.
  • Appeared in: 2023 Q7.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Infinitely Repeated Games, Concepts/Grim Trigger Strategy, Concepts/Critical Discount Factor, Concepts/Folk Theorem, Concepts/Subgame Perfect Equilibrium.
Sub-question Type B.E.3: Merger vs collusion, regulator's preference
  • What it asks: When do firms prefer to merge rather than collude (or vice versa), and which does the regulator prefer?
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis: a merger and successful collusion can both achieve the joint-profit-maximising outcome, but they differ in commitment, stability, side-payments, and legal risk. Firms prefer merger when collusion is hard to sustain (low $\delta$, many firms, demand instability) or when synergies are large. The regulator generally prefers collusion to be unstable and is hostile to mergers that lack synergies; the regulator's choice between blocking collusion and blocking a merger turns on which is easier to detect and on whether synergies exist.
  2. What collusion can achieve: joint profit maximisation as the cooperative payoff in an infinitely repeated game, sustained by grim-trigger or similar punishments above the critical $\delta^*$.
  3. What merger can achieve: legal joint ownership, single-decision-maker, full internalisation of pricing externalities, possible cost synergies. No incentive-compatibility constraint on the joint pricing decision because the merged firm is a single entity.
  4. Why firms prefer merger: (a) commitment (no need to satisfy $\delta \ge \delta^*$), (b) realisation of cost synergies that collusion cannot deliver, (c) escape from the legal risk of explicit collusion (cartel fines and prison terms in many jurisdictions), (d) immune to defection.
  5. Why firms might prefer collusion: (a) reversibility (can exit the cartel if conditions change), (b) no merger regulatory approval needed, (c) avoids costly integration of management, (d) flexibility in profit shares.
  6. Regulator's view: collusion has no efficiency gain (firms remain separate so productive efficiency is unchanged) and harms consumers. The regulator unambiguously prefers to break it. Merger has the Williamson trade-off (cite Concepts/Williamson Trade-off): potentially harmful via market power, potentially beneficial via synergies. The regulator's preference depends on synergies.
  7. Implication for policy mix: leniency programmes destabilise collusion; merger control screens out mergers that lack synergies but allows pro-efficiency ones. The regulator's preference is implicit in the policy mix: kill collusion always, allow mergers conditionally.
  8. Conclusion: firms' preference depends on the sustainability of collusion ($\delta$, number of firms, market stability) and on the size of merger synergies. The regulator's preference is asymmetric: collusion is condemned because it has no offsetting gain; mergers are weighed case by case.
  • Appeared in: 2024 Q7.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Merger Analysis, Concepts/Williamson Trade-off, Concepts/Infinitely Repeated Games, Concepts/Grim Trigger Strategy, Concepts/Critical Discount Factor.
Sub-question Type B.E.4: Entry, price, and whether mergers should be banned
  • What it asks: Under what conditions does entry reduce price, and should mergers be banned outright?
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis: entry reduces price when residual demand for the entrant is positive at prices above marginal cost and the post-entry equilibrium is more competitive than the pre-entry one. Mergers should not be banned outright because they can deliver Williamson-style synergies, but they should be subject to case-by-case scrutiny.
  2. Entry and price in Cournot oligopoly: with $n$ symmetric firms, $p_n = \frac{a + n c}{n + 1}$, monotonically falling in $n$ and converging to $c$ as $n \to \infty$. Entry strictly reduces price as long as the entrant's marginal cost is not too far above incumbents'.
  3. Entry and price in Bertrand: even one entrant drives price to $c$ in homogeneous Bertrand. In differentiated Bertrand, entry expands the set of varieties and shifts each incumbent's residual demand, reducing prices but by less than in Cournot.
  4. When entry does not reduce price: (a) entry deterrence by limit pricing or capacity preemption (cite Concepts/Entry Deterrence), (b) entry into a separate niche that doesn't compete with incumbents, (c) collusive accommodation where the entrant joins the cartel, (d) cost-disadvantaged entry that exits quickly.
  5. Connecting to merger policy: a merger reduces $n$, so by symmetry with the entry argument it raises price (the reverse of entry). The case for blocking is the case for not undoing entry.
  6. Why not ban all mergers?: (a) Williamson synergies (cite Concepts/Williamson Trade-off) can dominate the DWL triangle, (b) some mergers are pro-competitive when they create a stronger rival to a dominant incumbent (the maverick rationale), (c) mergers can enable economies of scale and R&D investment that no firm alone could finance, (d) blocking all mergers chills the market for corporate control and protects underperforming managers.
  7. The case-by-case standard: regulators use HHI thresholds, market-definition tests, efficiency defences, and post-merger price-prediction models. This balances the synergies and the market-power costs.
  8. Conclusion: entry typically reduces price, with exceptions tied to deterrence, accommodation, and niche entry. A blanket merger ban discards the synergies and the discipline of the market for corporate control. The right policy is conditional approval with active enforcement.
  • Appeared in: 2025 Q7.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Cournot Duopoly, Concepts/Bertrand Duopoly, Concepts/Entry Deterrence, Concepts/Williamson Trade-off, Concepts/Merger Analysis.

Sub-question Type B.L.1: Cournot duopoly with optional cost-reducing investment (2-stage game with threshold)
  • What it asks: A two-stage game where firm 1 first chooses whether to undertake a cost-reducing investment at fixed cost $K$, then both firms play Cournot. Find the SPE, the threshold $K$ above which firm 1 does not invest, and discuss the credibility of off-path play.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Stage 2 Cournot with symmetric costs (no investment): both firms have cost $c$. Apply A.6 to get $q^ = \frac{a-c}{3b}$, $\pi^ = \frac{(a-c)^2}{9 b}$. Call this $\pi^N$.
  2. Stage 2 Cournot with asymmetric costs (firm 1 has lower cost $c' < c$, firm 2 still has $c$): redo the FOCs. $q_1 = \frac{a - 2 c' + c}{3 b}$, $q_2 = \frac{a - 2 c + c'}{3 b}$. Profits $\pi_1^I = b \cdot q_1^2$ and $\pi_2^I = b \cdot q_2^2$. Firm 1's profit rises (intuition: the cost-cutting firm captures more of the market and the equilibrium price drops by less than the cost reduction).
  3. Stage 1 SPE: firm 1 invests iff $\pi_1^I - K \ge \pi_1^N$. Solve for the threshold $K^* = \pi_1^I - \pi_1^N$.
  4. Stage 1 strategies: firm 1's strategy is "invest if $K < K^*$, do not invest otherwise". Firm 2's strategy is "play the Cournot quantity appropriate to firm 1's cost level". This is an SPE because both stage-2 continuations are NEs of the Cournot subgame at the relevant cost levels.
  5. NE that fails subgame perfection: a profile where firm 2 threatens to flood the market with output if firm 1 invests (in order to deter the investment) can be a NE of the strategic form if firm 1 believes the threat. It fails SPE because firm 2's threat is not best-response in the post-investment Cournot subgame.
  6. Credibility of off-path play: this is the standard SPE-vs-NE distinction. Off-path moves must themselves be NE in the continuation game. Bluff threats are not SPE-credible.

Sub-question Type B.L.2: Parametrised 2x3 game with affine normalisation and repeated play
  • What it asks: A 2x3 strategic-form game whose payoffs depend on parameters $x$, $y$, $t$, $w$. Normalise the payoffs via an affine transformation, eliminate dominated rows/columns, find all NE (pure and mixed) at specific parameter values, and analyse cooperation in repeated play.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Affine normalisation: the NE set is invariant under transformations of the form $v_i = \alpha_i + \beta_i u_i$ with $\beta_i > 0$ applied independently to each player's payoff. Choose $\alpha_i, \beta_i$ to set, for example, each player's minimum payoff to $0$ and maximum to $1$. State explicitly why this is valid (cite Concepts/Affine Utility Normalisation): best responses depend only on the ordering and ratios of payoff differences within each player.
  2. Iterated dominance: check whether one column (say R) is dominated by a mixture of the other two columns. If so, eliminate R and reduce to a 2x2.
  3. Pure-strategy NE at the boundary $x = 0$: apply the A.1 best-response method to the reduced 2x2.
  4. Mixed-strategy NE at $x = 0$: apply A.2 indifference principle. State the expected payoffs.
  5. Mixed NE at $x = 10$ (only): redo the indifference principle with the new payoffs. Note explicitly that there is no pure NE in this case, hence "mixed only".
  6. Sustaining cooperation in repeated play: identify the cooperative outcome (typically (T, M) or (B, L), whichever is Pareto-superior to the static NE), use the static-NE as the punishment, and derive the critical $\delta^*$ for grim-trigger sustenance via A.8.

How to recognise the format on the day

Part A Game Theory / IO questions are short, formula-driven, and tightly scaffolded. Cues:

  1. A small bimatrix in the question stem: expect A.1 (pure NE), A.2 (mixed NE), A.3 (NE definition). Allow about 12 to 15 minutes.
  2. The words "Bertrand" or "price competition" with identical products: expect A.4 (price equals MC). Allow 8 to 10 minutes plus a short comment on why the result is fragile.
  3. The words "differentiated" or a demand of the form $q_i = \alpha - \beta p_i + \gamma p_j$: expect A.5 plus comparative statics and possibly a merger comparison. Allow 15 minutes.
  4. The word "Cournot": expect A.6, often as Stage 2 of a multi-stage problem. Allow 10 minutes.
  5. An extensive-form tree or "sequential" wording: expect A.7 backward induction. Allow 10 to 12 minutes.
  6. Words "repeated game", "infinitely", "discount factor", or "$\delta$": expect A.8 grim trigger and the critical $\delta^*$. Allow 15 minutes.
  7. The word "merger" with a duopoly setup: expect A.9 Williamson trade-off with a welfare diagram. Allow 12 minutes.

Part B Game Theory / IO essays cluster around four prompts: (i) duopoly merger analysis, (ii) collusion sustainability, (iii) merger vs collusion, (iv) entry and merger bans. The recipe is the same: open with a one-sentence thesis, build the theoretical apparatus (Cournot, Bertrand, repeated games, Williamson), then use it to answer the regulatory question. Always finish with a position. Examiners reward a clear stance backed by the formal model over hedging.

4 Best Response Functions Tier 2

1. Intuition

Each player's best response function maps the opponent's strategy to the player's optimal reply. Nash equilibria are fixed points of the joint best-response map: each player is best responding to the other.

Drawing best-response functions in a two-player game lets you read off pure-strategy NE as intersections. In Cournot, best responses slope downward (strategic substitutes); in differentiated Bertrand they slope upward (strategic complements).

4 Nash Equilibrium Tier 2

Part A: short compulsory problems
Sub-question Type A.1: Define certainty equivalent and risk premium, use to argue acceptance or refusal
  • What it asks: Give precise definitions of the Concepts/Certainty Equivalent $w^c$ and Concepts/Risk Premium $\pi$ for a lottery $L$ paying $w_i$ with probability $p_i$, then deploy them to say whether a risk-averse agent accepts a project, sells a lottery ticket, or buys it. Often phrased as "explain how the certainty equivalent determines whether Janet plays."
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. State the definitions cleanly. Expected utility of the lottery is $E[u(W)] = \sum_i p_i u(w_i)$. The certainty equivalent $w^c$ is the sure wealth that gives the same utility, $u(w^c) = E[u(W)]$. The risk premium is $\pi = E[W] - w^c$.
  2. Note the equivalent statement: an Concepts/Expected Utility maximiser is willing to accept the lottery in exchange for sure wealth $w_0$ if and only if $w_0 \leq w^c$, equivalently $u(w_0) \leq E[u(W)]$.
  3. For risk-averse $u$ (concave), Jensen's inequality gives $E[u(W)] < u(E[W])$, so $w^c < E[W]$ and $\pi > 0$. State this as the operational test.
  4. Apply to the specific scenario: compute $E[u(W)]$, invert $u$ to find $w^c$, compare to the asking price or the outside option.
  • Appeared in: 2019 Q3(a), 2020 Q3(a), 2016 Q2(a).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Certainty Equivalent, Concepts/Risk Premium, Concepts/Expected Utility, Concepts/Risk Aversion.
Sub-question Type A.2: Compute the certainty equivalent and risk premium for a specific utility and lottery
  • What it asks: Given an explicit utility (typically $u(w) = \log w$, $u(w) = \sqrt{w}$, $u(w) = 2\sqrt{w}$ or $u(w) = -e^{-aw}$) and a lottery, compute $w^c$ and $\pi$ numerically.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Write out $E[u(W)] = \sum_i p_i u(w_i)$ explicitly. Keep symbolic where possible to avoid arithmetic slips.
  2. Invert: $w^c = u^{-1}(E[u(W)])$. For $u(w) = \log w$, $w^c = \exp(E[\log W])$, the geometric mean of outcomes when probabilities are equal. For $u(w) = a\sqrt{w}$, $w^c = (E[\sqrt{W}])^2$. For $u(w) = -e^{-aw}$, $w^c = -\frac{1}{a}\log(-E[u(W)])$.
  3. Compute $E[W] = \sum_i p_i w_i$ and the risk premium $\pi = E[W] - w^c$.
  4. Sanity check the sign: for concave $u$ you need $\pi > 0$. If the question asks for the "minimum price Janet would sell at" with current wealth $w_0$, this is $w^c$ relative to $w_0$, not $w^c$ alone, so add or subtract starting wealth where the lottery is over wealth changes versus over final wealth.
  • Appeared in: 2017 Q3(b) (Janet $u = 2\sqrt{w}$, lottery $\pm 40$ from $w = 10000$), 2018 Q3(a) (Janet log utility, project with two outcomes), 2019 Q3(b)(c) (lottery 12 or 0, square root utility, selling and buying prices), 2025 Q10(b) (CE of lottery B under specific $v(t)$).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Certainty Equivalent, Concepts/Risk Premium, Concepts/CRRA Utility, Concepts/CARA Utility.
Sub-question Type A.3: Show ARA is decreasing and RRA is constant for CRRA-style utility
  • What it asks: Given $u(w) = 2\sqrt{w}$, $u(w) = \log w$ or a related CRRA form, show that the Arrow-Pratt coefficient of absolute risk aversion $A(w) = -u''(w)/u'(w)$ is decreasing in $w$ and that the coefficient of relative risk aversion $R(w) = w \cdot A(w)$ is constant.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Compute $u'(w)$ and $u''(w)$. For $u(w) = 2\sqrt{w}$: $u'(w) = w^{-1/2}$, $u''(w) = -\frac{1}{2}w^{-3/2}$.
  2. Form $A(w) = -u''(w)/u'(w)$. For $u = 2\sqrt{w}$, $A(w) = \frac{1}{2w}$. Differentiate: $A'(w) = -\frac{1}{2w^2} < 0$, so Concepts/DARA Utility holds.
  3. Form $R(w) = w \cdot A(w)$. For $u = 2\sqrt{w}$, $R(w) = \frac{1}{2}$, a constant, so this is Concepts/CRRA Utility with coefficient $1/2$.
  4. State the economic content: under DARA, agents hold more risky assets in absolute terms as they get richer. Under CRRA, the share of wealth in risky assets is wealth-invariant.
  • Appeared in: 2017 Q3(a), 2022 Q8 (essay context, drawing on this computation), 2025 Q10(c)(d).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Arrow-Pratt ARA, Concepts/Arrow-Pratt RRA, Concepts/DARA Utility, Concepts/CRRA Utility.
Sub-question Type A.4: Use the Arrow-Pratt approximation to compare risk premia across agents or lottery sizes
  • What it asks: For a small zero-mean risk $\tilde{\epsilon}$ around wealth $w$, the risk premium satisfies $\pi \approx \frac{1}{2} A(w) \sigma^2$ where $\sigma^2 = \text{Var}(\tilde{\epsilon})$. Use this to compare two agents, or to predict how $\pi$ scales with the size of the lottery.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. State the approximation: for small $\sigma^2$, $\pi(w, \tilde{\epsilon}) \approx \frac{1}{2} A(w) \cdot \text{Var}(\tilde{\epsilon})$.
  2. For each agent, evaluate $A(w)$ at the relevant wealth. The ratio of risk premia is $\pi_S / \pi_J \approx A_S(w) / A_J(w)$.
  3. For scaling in lottery size: if the lottery is scaled by $\lambda$, variance scales by $\lambda^2$, so $\pi$ scales by $\lambda^2$ approximately. This is why doubling the stakes more than doubles the premium for the same agent.
  4. Sanity check: the approximation is local. For large lotteries, compute the exact premium and compare. The error grows in $\sigma^2$ and in the curvature of $A$.
  • Appeared in: 2017 Q3(c) (compare Sam's risk premium to Janet's for a smaller lottery).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Arrow-Pratt ARA, Concepts/Risk Premium, Concepts/Expected Utility.
Sub-question Type A.5: Infer risk attitude from observed willingness to swap or sell
  • What it asks: Given that an agent is indifferent between, or strictly prefers, one lottery over another (or over a sure outcome), what does this tell you about the curvature of $u$? Often the question pairs this with a specific functional form like $u = \log w$ and asks for a numerical bound on a price.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Translate the observed choice into an inequality on expected utility. "Janet is willing to swap lottery $X$ for sure wealth $w_0$" gives $u(w_0) \geq E[u(X)]$.
  2. Compare $w_0$ to $E[X]$. If $w_0 < E[X]$, Janet is risk-averse (she accepts less than the expected value to avoid risk). If $w_0 > E[X]$, she is risk-loving. If equal, risk-neutral.
  3. For numerical bounds: substitute the assumed $u$, set the indifference equation $u(w_0) = E[u(X)]$, and solve for $w_0$. This is the largest sum she would pay or the smallest she would accept.
  4. State the qualitative conclusion (sign of $u''$) before quoting numbers, since the question usually wants the inference, not just the answer.
  • Appeared in: 2016 Q2(b)(c) (Janet, lottery swap, log utility), 2020 Q3(b) (Sam's argument that Janet must be risk averse), 2024 Q4(b) (preferring $L$ to $L'$ implies $p > q$ and bounds $u$).

Sub-question Type A.6: Optimal risk-sharing in a syndicate of $n$ investors and the optimal size of the group
  • What it asks: A risky project with payoff $X$ is shared equally between $n$ investors, each takes $X/n$ and pays a share of the cost. For what $n$ does each investor accept? At what $n$ is per-investor expected utility maximised, and why does utility eventually fall as $n$ grows?
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Write each investor's payoff: net wealth is $w_0 + X/n - c/n$ where $c$ is the cost of joining (or zero if costless). Their EU is $E[u(w_0 + X/n - c/n)]$.
  2. Acceptance condition: $E[u(w_0 + X/n - c/n)] \geq u(w_0)$. For log utility this gives a closed-form bound on $n$.
  3. As $n \to \infty$, the per-person risk shrinks like $1/n^2$ in variance terms, so the risk premium falls. By the Arrow-Pratt approximation, $\pi_n \approx \frac{1}{2}A(w_0) \sigma^2/n^2$.
  4. Optimal group size when there is a cost or non-investor outside option. Maximise $E[u(w_0 + (X-c)/n)]$ over $n$, then check the FOC. Beyond the optimum, each new member dilutes the expected upside more than the variance reduction is worth, especially if $E[X] - c > 0$ but the per-capita expected gain $(E[X] - c)/n$ falls in $n$. This is the "sharing eventually hurts" intuition.
  5. State the trade-off cleanly: more members means less risk per head but also less expected gain per head.
  • Appeared in: 2014 Q4(b)(c), 2018 Q3(b)(c).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Risk Pooling, Concepts/Risk Aversion, Concepts/Expected Utility, Concepts/Arrow-Pratt ARA.
Sub-question Type A.7: Two-state insurance problem in a state-space diagram
  • What it asks: An agent faces wealth $w_g$ in the good state (prob $1-\pi$) and $w_b = w_g - L$ in the bad state (prob $\pi$). They buy coverage $q$ at premium price $\gamma$. Find optimal coverage, draw the Concepts/State-Space Insurance Diagram, compare fair ($\gamma = \pi$) with unfair ($\gamma > \pi$) premia, and the role of monopoly versus competitive insurer.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Wealth in each state after buying $q$ units of coverage at unit price $\gamma$: $w_g(q) = w_0 - \gamma q$ and $w_b(q) = w_0 - L + (1-\gamma)q$. Plot $w_b$ against $w_g$. The budget line has slope $-\gamma/(1-\gamma)$. The 45-degree line is the "full insurance" locus.
  2. Indifference curves of EU $\pi u(w_b) + (1-\pi)u(w_g)$ have slope $-\frac{(1-\pi)u'(w_g)}{\pi u'(w_b)}$. At the 45-degree line this slope is $-(1-\pi)/\pi$, the fair-odds slope.
  3. Fair premium $\gamma = \pi$: budget slope equals fair-odds slope, tangency lies on the 45 line, so optimal $q^* = L$ (full insurance).
  4. Unfair premium $\gamma > \pi$: budget is steeper than fair-odds, tangency to the right of the 45 line, so $w_g > w_b$ at optimum, hence $q^* < L$ (underinsurance). Solve the FOC $\frac{(1-\pi)u'(w_g)}{\pi u'(w_b)} = \frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}$.
  5. Monopoly versus competitive: competitive insurers earn zero profit, so $\gamma = \pi$ if risk-neutral and frictionless. Monopolist extracts the agent's surplus by offering a contract that holds the agent at $u(w_0)$ on the EU indifference curve through the endowment, the contract lies on the agent's reservation indifference curve at the highest profit point.
  6. Risk pooling between two agents: if incomes are perfectly negatively correlated ($\rho = -1$), aggregate variance is zero, full insurance is possible without cost. As $\rho \to 1$, no gains from pooling. The pooling gain falls in $\rho$.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q4(a)(b)(c)(d), 2023 Q4(a)(b)(c)(d), 2018 Q10(a)(b)(c)(d) (long-answer extension).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/State-Space Insurance Diagram, Concepts/Fair Premium, Concepts/Risk Pooling, Concepts/Risk Aversion, Concepts/Expected Utility.
Sub-question Type A.8: Compound lotteries, the reduction property, and the independence axiom
  • What it asks: Given two stage-lotteries that play out into final prizes with different probability trees, check whether a particular preference ranking is implied by Concepts/Expected Utility alone. Distinguishes between rankings that follow from reduction of compound lotteries (any EU maximiser must hold) and rankings that only follow from extra structure (concavity, monotonicity).
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Use Concepts/Reduction of Compound Lotteries: a two-stage lottery yielding $L_1$ with probability $\alpha$ and $L_2$ with probability $1-\alpha$ is equivalent to the simple lottery with prize probabilities $\alpha \Pr_{L_1}(\cdot) + (1-\alpha) \Pr_{L_2}(\cdot)$.
  2. Reduce both compound lotteries to simple form. If one simple form first-order stochastically dominates the other, every monotone $u$ ranks them the same way.
  3. Check whether the prizes can be ordered so that the ranking holds for all $u$ (FOSD), for all concave $u$ (SOSD), or only for specific $u$. Cite the Concepts/Independence Axiom when the question hinges on substituting a common-prize branch.
  4. For statements of the form "every risk-averse agent prefers $X$ to $Y$ when $y_1 < x_1 < x_2 < y_2$": check whether this is single-crossing of CDFs, which gives Concepts/Second-Order Stochastic Dominance of $X$ over $Y$ when $E[X] \geq E[Y]$. Without the mean condition, no general ranking.

Sub-question Type A.9: First-order stochastic dominance check between two lotteries
  • What it asks: Given two CDFs $F$ and $G$, check whether $F$ first-order stochastically dominates $G$, equivalently $F(x) \leq G(x)$ for all $x$ with strict inequality somewhere. If yes, every monotone $u$ ranks $F$ strictly above $G$.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Compute or sketch both CDFs over the support.
  2. Check $F(x) \leq G(x)$ pointwise. If true with strict inequality somewhere, $F$ FOSD-dominates $G$.
  3. If the CDFs cross, FOSD fails. Try Concepts/Second-Order Stochastic Dominance by integrating: $\int_{-\infty}^x F(t) dt \leq \int_{-\infty}^x G(t) dt$ for all $x$ with equal means gives SOSD, which is the all-risk-averse ranking.
  4. State the implication: under FOSD, $E_F[u(X)] \geq E_G[u(X)]$ for every weakly increasing $u$, with strict inequality for strictly increasing $u$ and non-degenerate $F-G$.

Sub-question Type A.10: Affine normalisation of vNM utility and invariance of risk preferences
  • What it asks: Show that if $v(w) = \alpha + \beta u(w)$ with $\beta > 0$, then $v$ represents the same preferences over lotteries as $u$. Optionally justify a particular normalisation, like setting $u$ to map two reference outcomes to 0 and 1.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Pick two lotteries $L$ and $L'$. Show $E[u(L)] \geq E[u(L')] \iff E[v(L)] \geq E[v(L')]$ using linearity of expectation and $\beta > 0$.
  2. Note the Arrow-Pratt coefficients are invariant: $-v''/v' = -(\beta u'')/(\beta u') = -u''/u'$, so Concepts/Arrow-Pratt ARA and RRA are pinned down by preferences, the cardinal scale is not.
  3. Defend the normalisation. vNM utility is unique up to positive affine transformation, so fixing two points (e.g., $u(\text{worst}) = 0$, $u(\text{best}) = 1$) is without loss of generality and helpful for calibration.
  4. Be precise about what is not invariant: differences of utility levels are meaningful only relative to the chosen scale, but ratios of differences (and the implied probabilities at indifference) are invariant.

Sub-question Type B.E.2: Explain EU theory and why a risk-averse agent still invests in a risky asset

  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis: Risk aversion does not mean refusing all risk. Concavity of $u$ implies a strict positive risk premium for fair gambles, but for any actuarially favourable gamble (positive risk premium offered by the market), a risk-averse agent strictly prefers some positive exposure. The first-order condition at zero exposure has a strictly positive value, so the corner is never optimal.

EU framework: State the four Concepts/von Neumann Morgenstern Axioms (completeness, transitivity, Concepts/Continuity Axiom, Concepts/Independence Axiom) and the vNM theorem: under these axioms there exists $u: X \to \mathbb{R}$ unique up to positive affine transformation such that $L \succ L' \iff E_L[u(x)] > E_{L'}[u(x)]$.

Under Concepts/CARA Utility, $\alpha^$ is wealth-independent; under Concepts/CRRA Utility, the share $\alpha^*/w_0$ is wealth-independent. Both are observed empirical regularities in different domains.
  8. Caveat: If $\mu \leq r_f$, the agent shorts the risky asset (or holds nothing if shorting is banned). The result is sharp: $\alpha^* > 0 \iff \mu > r_f$ given strict concavity.

Sub-question Type B.E.3: Sordid club framing of continuity and independence axioms
  • What it asks: A "Sordid Club" offers a lottery with a small probability of a very bad outcome and a large probability of a small good outcome. Use this to illustrate the Concepts/Continuity Axiom and the Concepts/Independence Axiom. Prove that EU satisfies them. Identify which EU maximisers would play.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis: Continuity rules out lexicographic aversion to catastrophic outcomes: for any three ranked prizes $x \succ y \succ z$, there is some probability mix of $x$ and $z$ indifferent to $y$. Independence rules out reversals from adding common branches. Many people resist these axioms in stylised settings like the Sordid Club, but EU satisfies them by construction.
  2. State the axioms: For prizes $x \succ y \succ z$, continuity says $\exists \alpha \in (0,1)$ with $y \sim \alpha x + (1-\alpha) z$. Independence says $L \succ L' \iff \alpha L + (1-\alpha) L'' \succ \alpha L' + (1-\alpha) L''$ for any $L''$ and any $\alpha \in (0,1]$.
  3. EU satisfies continuity: With $u(x) > u(y) > u(z)$, set $\alpha = (u(y) - u(z))/(u(x) - u(z))$, which is in $(0,1)$. Then $\alpha u(x) + (1-\alpha) u(z) = u(y)$, so the mix is EU-indifferent to $y$. Done.
  4. EU satisfies independence: $L \succ L' \iff E_L[u] > E_{L'}[u]$. By linearity, $E_{\alpha L + (1-\alpha)L''}[u] = \alpha E_L[u] + (1-\alpha) E_{L''}[u]$, similarly for $L'$. So $L \succ L' \iff \alpha E_L[u] + (1-\alpha) E_{L''}[u] > \alpha E_{L'}[u] + (1-\alpha) E_{L''}[u]$, which is $\alpha L + (1-\alpha) L'' \succ \alpha L' + (1-\alpha) L''$. Done.
  5. Sordid Club application: Suppose the lottery offers $+\$1$ with prob $1-\epsilon$ and a catastrophic loss $-C$ with prob $\epsilon$. Continuity says some $\epsilon$ makes the agent indifferent between playing and not playing. People who refuse to play for any $\epsilon > 0$ violate continuity, which by the vNM theorem rules out a finite-valued utility. Under EU, the agent plays iff $(1-\epsilon) u(w_0 + 1) + \epsilon u(w_0 - C) \geq u(w_0)$. For risk-averse $u$ with continuous $u(w_0 - C)$ finite, an $\epsilon$ small enough makes this hold.
  6. Who plays: EU maximisers with $u$ such that $u(w_0 - C)$ is not $-\infty$. Log utility on wealth that can hit zero or negative regions raises problems and may rationalise refusing for any $\epsilon$, but only because $u \to -\infty$, which itself is a borderline case for continuity. Risk-loving and risk-neutral agents always play if $E$ of payoff is positive. Heavily risk-averse agents need very small $\epsilon$.
  7. Caveats: Real-world refusal of small-probability catastrophic risks (e.g., insurance puzzles, terrorism aversion) may reflect probability distortion (prospect theory), violations of independence (Allais), or genuinely unbounded $u$. Cite the Allais paradox briefly.

Sub-question Type B.L.1: Alex's flood insurance with unfair premium under log utility (2018 Q10)
  • What it asks: Alex owns a house of value $h$ and financial assets $f$. The house floods with probability $\pi$, destroying value $h$. Insurance is available at unit premium $p > \pi$. Alex has log utility on total wealth and chooses coverage $q$.
  • (a) Draw wealth in the two states, mark no insurance, full insurance and partial insurance points.
  • (b) Show Alex is risk-averse, write down expected utility, explain why she underinsures.
  • (c) Solve for optimal $q$; show the tangency condition implies $q = 0$ at some threshold premium.
  • (d) Comparative statics: how does optimal $q$ change in house value $h$ and in financial assets $f$.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. State-space setup: Let $w_g$ = wealth in good (no-flood) state, $w_b$ = wealth in bad (flood) state. Without insurance: $w_g = f + h$, $w_b = f$. With $q$ units of coverage: $w_g(q) = f + h - pq$, $w_b(q) = f + (1-p)q$ (coverage pays $q$ in the bad state minus premium $pq$ in both states; net good-state payoff is $-pq$, net bad-state payoff is $q - pq = (1-p)q$).
  2. Diagram: Plot $w_b$ on the vertical axis against $w_g$ on the horizontal. The endowment $(f+h, f)$ sits below the 45-degree line. The budget line through the endowment has slope $-(1-p)/p$ (from $\Delta w_b / \Delta w_g = -(1-p)/p$). Full insurance is the intersection with the 45-degree line, where $q = h$. Partial insurance is any interior point on the budget line above the endowment and below the 45-line.
  3. Risk aversion and EU: $u(w) = \log w$ has $u'' < 0$, hence risk-averse. EU is $V(q) = (1-\pi) \log(f + h - pq) + \pi \log(f + (1-p)q)$.
  4. Why she underinsures at unfair premium: At full insurance ($q = h$), wealth is $f + h - ph = f + h(1-p)$ in both states. The marginal utility of moving away from full insurance is $\frac{\partial V}{\partial q}|_{q=h}$. Computing: the marginal utility loss in the good state is $-(1-\pi) \cdot p / w$ and the marginal gain in the bad state is $\pi(1-p)/w$ at $w = f + h(1-p)$. Sum is $[\pi(1-p) - (1-\pi)p]/w = (\pi - p)/w < 0$ since $p > \pi$. So increasing $q$ above $h$ reduces utility, but the same logic shows that at slightly below full insurance, the marginal gain in the bad state exceeds the marginal loss in the good state up to the tangency point. The agent stops short of full insurance because each unit of cover transfers wealth from a likely good state to an unlikely bad state at an unfavourable rate.
  5. Optimal $q$: FOC: $\frac{\pi(1-p)}{f + (1-p)q} = \frac{(1-\pi)p}{f + h - pq}$. Cross-multiplying: $\pi(1-p)(f + h - pq) = (1-\pi)p(f + (1-p)q)$. Solve for $q$:

$$q^ = \frac{\pi(1-p)(f + h) - (1-\pi)pf}{(1-p)p[\pi + (1-\pi)]} = \frac{\pi(1-p)(f + h) - (1-\pi)pf}{p(1-p)}.$$ Simplify: $q^ = \frac{\pi(1-p)(f+h) - (1-\pi)pf}{p(1-p)}$.

  1. Boundary $q^ = 0$: Set $q^ = 0$: $\pi(1-p)(f+h) = (1-\pi)pf$, giving $p = \frac{\pi(f+h)}{\pi(f+h) + (1-\pi)f} \cdot \frac{1}{(\text{simplify})}$. More cleanly, $q^* > 0$ iff $\frac{\pi(1-p)}{(1-\pi)p} \cdot \frac{f+h}{f} > 1$, i.e., $\frac{f+h}{f} > \frac{(1-\pi)p}{\pi(1-p)}$. So coverage is positive iff the house is large enough relative to financial wealth, given the markup $p/\pi$.
  2. Comparative statics: $\partial q^ / \partial h > 0$ (bigger house, more coverage, intuitively). $\partial q^ / \partial f$: differentiate the formula. Higher $f$ raises both terms but the negative term grows faster relative to the positive term, so $\partial q^* / \partial f < 0$ under DARA (richer agents take less insurance, since each pound of wealth is less valuable at the margin in the bad state). For log utility, this is a clean result: insurance falls in non-insured wealth.
  3. Diagram annotation: Mark Alex's optimum strictly between the endowment and the full-insurance point. Mark the slope of the indifference curve at the optimum equal to $-p/(1-p)$. The 45-degree line lies above the optimum, showing underinsurance.

How to recognise the format on the day

A Decisions under Risk Part A problem opens with a named character (Janet, Sam, Arthur, Alex, Cornelia, Spin) facing a lottery, a project, or a flood. The first part almost always asks for a definition (CE, risk premium, FOSD) or a derivation of $A(w)$ and $R(w)$ from a stated utility. The second part computes a numerical CE or risk premium. The third part is either a comparative statics exercise (how does $\pi$ change in stakes, wealth or number of partners) or a state-space insurance setup with fair versus unfair premium. A Part B essay on risk is signalled by a real-world framing (financial advisor, doctor, Sordid Club, risky asset investment). The structure is always: state the EU framework, define the key concept (Arrow-Pratt, FOSD, independence axiom), apply it to the framing, then give a caveat or extension. A Part B long-answer on risk is either a flood-insurance problem (state-space, log utility, comparative statics) or a multi-part lottery analysis (CDFs, FOSD, ARA, independence). If a question asks you to "rank" two lotteries for all risk-averse agents, your first move is FOSD; if FOSD fails, try SOSD; if SOSD fails, say it depends on $u$.

6 Arrow-Pratt ARA Tier 1

1. Intuition

$A(w) = -u''(w)/u'(w)$. Measures the curvature of utility in dollars: how much risk premium the agent demands for a small gamble of given variance. Higher A means more risk-averse.

ARA's comparative statics distinguish CARA (constant), DARA (decreasing in wealth, the empirically reasonable case), and IARA (increasing, exotic) utilities. DARA implies wealthy people accept more dollar risk.

2. Formal mathematics

For a twice differentiable Bernoulli utility $u$ with $u' > 0$, the Arrow-Pratt coefficient of absolute risk aversion at wealth $w$ is

$$ A(w) \;=\; -\frac{u''(w)}{u'(w)}. $$

It is invariant to positive affine transformations of $u$, unlike $u''$ alone, so it is a genuine measure of curvature in utility units.

Risk premium approximation. Consider a small lottery $\tilde \varepsilon$ with $E[\tilde\varepsilon] = 0$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\tilde\varepsilon) = \sigma^2$. The risk premium $\pi(w, \tilde\varepsilon)$ solves

$$ u(w - \pi) \;=\; E[u(w + \tilde\varepsilon)]. $$
  1. Taylor expand the left side: $u(w - \pi) \approx u(w) - \pi u'(w)$ for small $\pi$.
  2. Taylor expand the right side to second order: $E[u(w+\tilde\varepsilon)] \approx u(w) + u'(w) E[\tilde\varepsilon] + \tfrac{1}{2} u''(w) E[\tilde\varepsilon^2] = u(w) + \tfrac{1}{2} u''(w) \sigma^2$.
  3. Equate the two expansions and cancel $u(w)$: $-\pi u'(w) = \tfrac{1}{2} u''(w) \sigma^2$.
  4. Solve for $\pi$, $$ \pi \;\approx\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\frac{u''(w)}{u'(w)}\sigma^2 \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2} A(w)\, \sigma^2. $$

So $A(w)$ is, up to a factor of two, the marginal risk premium per unit variance for small risks. The corresponding measure for proportional risks is the relative coefficient $R(w) = w A(w) = -w u''(w)/u'(w)$, with premium $\pi/w \approx \tfrac{1}{2} R(w) \sigma_r^2$ where $\sigma_r$ is the standard deviation of the proportional risk $\tilde\varepsilon/w$.

Pratt's theorem. The following are equivalent: (i) $A_1(w) \geq A_2(w)$ for all $w$, (ii) $u_1 = \phi \circ u_2$ for some increasing concave $\phi$, (iii) $\pi_1(w, \tilde\varepsilon) \geq \pi_2(w, \tilde\varepsilon)$ for all $w$ and all $\tilde\varepsilon$.

References: Pratt (1964 Econometrica); Mas-Colell, Whinston, Green Chapter 6.C; Gravelle and Rees Chapter 17; Micro2025.pdf Topic 6.

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • Insurance pricing. The maximum premium a household will pay above the fair price is approximately $\tfrac{1}{2} A(w)\sigma^2$, so estimates of $A$ from survey lotteries feed directly into demand for cover.
  • Portfolio share in risky assets. Under mean-variance, the optimal risky share is roughly $\mu / (A(w)\sigma^2)$, linking measured $A$ to observed equity holdings.
  • Health and safety regulation. Value of statistical life calculations use $A(w)$ implicitly when translating between income and mortality risk tradeoffs.
  • Essay: why the ratio, not $u''$. Affine reparameterisations scale $u''$ but cancel in the ratio, so $A$ is the right curvature measure for behaviour.
  • Essay: Pratt's theorem. Comparative risk aversion is fully ordinal, $u_1$ is more risk averse than $u_2$ iff one is a concave transform of the other.
  • Limitation. The approximation $\pi \approx \tfrac{1}{2} A \sigma^2$ is local; for large risks third moments and higher start to matter, motivating prudence ($-u'''/u''$) and temperance.

6 CARA Utility Tier 1

1. Intuition

Constant Absolute Risk Aversion: $u(w) = -e^{-a w}/a$ for $a > 0$. ARA is constant at $a$. Wealth does not affect the risk premium for a given dollar gamble, which is unrealistic but tractable.

CARA shines under normally distributed wealth: $CE = E[w] - \frac{1}{2} a \sigma^2$. This is the basis of mean-variance analysis and Holmstrom-Milgrom linear contracts.

2. Formal mathematics

Constant absolute risk aversion (CARA) is the family of Bernoulli utilities with $A(w) = a$ independent of wealth. The ODE $-u''/u' = a$ integrates to

$$ u(w) \;=\; -\frac{1}{a} e^{-a w}, \qquad a > 0. $$

Up to positive affine transforms this is the unique CARA utility; $a = 0$ gives risk neutrality as a limiting case.

  1. Check $u'(w) = e^{-aw} > 0$ and $u''(w) = -a e^{-aw} < 0$, so $u$ is strictly increasing and concave.
  2. Compute $A(w) = -u''(w)/u'(w) = a$, constant in $w$.
  3. Relative risk aversion is $R(w) = w A(w) = a w$, increasing linearly in wealth.

Mean-variance form under normality. Let $\tilde w \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. Using the moment generating function of the normal,

$$ E[-\tfrac{1}{a} e^{-a \tilde w}] \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{a} \exp\!\left(-a\mu + \tfrac{1}{2} a^2 \sigma^2\right). $$

Since $-\tfrac{1}{a} e^{-a x}$ is monotone in $x$, maximising expected utility is equivalent to maximising

$$ \mu - \tfrac{a}{2}\sigma^2, $$

the canonical mean-variance objective. This is the principal reason CARA plus normal returns is the workhorse of textbook portfolio choice.

Exact risk premium. For $\tilde\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, the certainty equivalent is $\mathrm{CE} = w - \tfrac{a}{2}\sigma^2$, so $\pi = \tfrac{a}{2}\sigma^2$. The risk premium is independent of initial wealth, capturing the "constant absolute" property exactly, not just locally.

Empirical issue. Wealth invariance is the main weakness: doubling wealth leaves the dollar premium for a given absolute lottery unchanged, which contradicts the observation that richer households accept larger absolute gambles. This motivates moving to CRRA or DARA.

References: Mas-Colell, Whinston, Green Chapter 6.C; Varian Chapter 12.4; Gravelle and Rees Chapter 17.

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • Mean-variance portfolio choice. CARA preferences with normal returns deliver the textbook risky share $\alpha^* = (\mu - r)/(a\sigma^2)$, used in introductory finance and the original Merton problem when wealth effects are suppressed.
  • Principal-agent models. Holmstrom and Milgrom (1987) use CARA plus normal noise so that incentive contracts collapse to a tractable certainty equivalent with risk cost $\tfrac{a}{2}\sigma^2$.
  • Insurance under additive risk. Optimal coinsurance with CARA does not depend on initial wealth, a simplifying benchmark even if empirically too strong.
  • Essay: why CARA is analytically convenient. Linearity of certainty equivalents in mean and variance removes wealth as a state variable, which makes dynamic problems with normal shocks recursive and closed-form.
  • Essay: empirical implausibility. Constant absolute risk aversion implies that a millionaire and a student would reject the same fair gamble of plus or minus 100. Survey and field evidence reject this; richer agents accept larger absolute bets.
  • Limitation. CARA implies relative risk aversion $R(w) = aw$ increasing without bound, which clashes with consumption-based asset pricing where roughly constant $R$ is preferred.
  • See also [[Concepts/CRRA Utility]] and [[Concepts/DARA Utility]].
6 CRRA Utility Tier 1

1. Intuition

Constant Relative Risk Aversion: $u(w) = w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ for $\gamma \neq 1$, with log utility as the limiting case at $\gamma = 1$. RRA is constant at gamma; ARA decreases with wealth.

The workhorse utility function in finance (asset pricing, portfolio choice) and growth theory (Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans). Captures the stylised fact that the rich and the poor seem to demand similar fractions of their wealth for proportional gambles.

2. Formal mathematics

Constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) is the family with $R(w) = w A(w) = \gamma$ constant. Solving $-w u''/u' = \gamma$ with $u' > 0$ yields the power utility

$$ u(w) \;=\; \begin{cases} \dfrac{w^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} & \gamma > 0,\ \gamma \neq 1, \\[4pt] \ln w & \gamma = 1. \end{cases} $$

$\gamma = 1$ is the limiting log case (apply L'Hopital to the bracket above).

  1. Differentiate, $u'(w) = w^{-\gamma}$ and $u''(w) = -\gamma w^{-\gamma - 1}$.
  2. Then $A(w) = -u''/u' = \gamma / w$, decreasing in wealth (so CRRA is also DARA whenever $\gamma > 0$).
  3. Relative risk aversion is $R(w) = w A(w) = \gamma$, constant.

Proportional risk premium. For a small multiplicative lottery $\tilde w = w(1 + \tilde\varepsilon)$ with $E[\tilde\varepsilon] = 0$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\tilde\varepsilon) = \sigma_r^2$, the Arrow-Pratt approximation gives

$$ \frac{\pi}{w} \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{2} R(w)\, \sigma_r^2 \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\, \sigma_r^2. $$

The risk premium scales proportionally with wealth: a doubling of $w$ doubles the dollar premium for the same proportional risk. This is the empirical regularity CARA fails to capture.

Lognormal closed form. If $\ln \tilde w \sim \mathcal{N}(m, s^2)$, then $E[u(\tilde w)] = E[\tilde w^{1-\gamma}]/(1-\gamma)$ and using the lognormal moment formula,

$$ \ln \mathrm{CE} \;=\; m + \tfrac{1-\gamma}{2}\, s^2, $$

so $\ln(\bar w / \mathrm{CE}) \approx \tfrac{\gamma}{2} s^2$. Log returns and CRRA together give exact mean-log-variance objectives, the foundation of consumption-based asset pricing.

Stationarity. CRRA is the only utility for which optimal consumption and portfolio shares are independent of wealth, which is why balanced growth in macro typically requires $\gamma$ constant.

References: Mas-Colell, Whinston, Green Chapter 6.C; Varian Chapter 12; Mehra and Prescott (1985) for the equity premium puzzle; Micro2025.pdf Topic 6.

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • Consumption-based asset pricing. The stochastic discount factor under CRRA is $M_{t+1} = \beta (c_{t+1}/c_t)^{-\gamma}$, giving the Euler equation that underpins Hansen-Singleton and Mehra-Prescott.
  • Equity premium puzzle. Matching the historical 6 percent equity premium with reasonable consumption volatility requires $\gamma$ around 30, far above survey estimates near 2 to 4; Mehra and Prescott (1985) is the canonical statement.
  • Balanced growth in macro. Log utility ($\gamma = 1$) is the only specification giving a constant savings rate in the Ramsey model, which is why it appears so often in growth theory.
  • Essay: wealth-invariant portfolio share. CRRA delivers a risky asset share that is independent of wealth, consistent with the rough constancy of equity portfolio weights across the wealth distribution.
  • Essay: tension with HARA evidence. Some field studies find $R$ falling mildly with wealth, motivating HARA forms that nest CRRA and CARA.
  • Limitation. CRRA conflates risk aversion with the elasticity of intertemporal substitution ($\mathrm{EIS} = 1/\gamma$), a coupling broken by Epstein-Zin preferences.

6 DARA Utility Tier 1

1. Intuition

Decreasing Absolute Risk Aversion: A'(w) < 0. The agent demands a smaller dollar risk premium as wealth grows. Standard assumption because it matches the empirical fact that wealthier people invest a larger absolute amount in risky assets.

Log utility, CRRA with gamma < infinity, and many other reasonable forms exhibit DARA. The classic 2017 Q3 Part A question exploits this property in u(w) = 2 sqrt(w).

2. Formal mathematics

A utility $u$ exhibits decreasing absolute risk aversion (DARA) if its Arrow-Pratt coefficient is strictly decreasing in wealth,

$$ A(w) \;=\; -\frac{u''(w)}{u'(w)} \quad \text{with} \quad A'(w) < 0. $$

Equivalently, the risk premium $\pi(w, \tilde\varepsilon)$ for a fixed absolute lottery $\tilde\varepsilon$ falls as $w$ rises: richer agents are willing to accept larger dollar gambles.

Characterisation via $u'''$. Differentiate $A$,

$$ A'(w) \;=\; -\frac{u'''(w) u'(w) - [u''(w)]^2}{[u'(w)]^2}. $$

Hence $A'(w) < 0$ is equivalent to

$$ u'''(w) \;>\; \frac{[u''(w)]^2}{u'(w)} \;>\; 0. $$
  1. DARA therefore requires prudence, $u''' > 0$. Kimball (1990) calls $P(w) = -u'''/u''$ the coefficient of absolute prudence; DARA is the statement $P(w) > A(w)$.
  2. Examples: log utility has $A(w) = 1/w$, decreasing. Power utility $w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ with $\gamma > 0$ has $A(w) = \gamma/w$, also DARA. Exponential utility is not DARA: $A$ is constant.
  3. Quadratic utility $u(w) = w - bw^2$ is the canonical counterexample, having increasing ARA on its domain of monotonicity.

Implication for precautionary saving. Under DARA with $u''' > 0$, adding a mean-zero future income risk raises optimal saving relative to the certainty case (Leland 1968, Sandmo 1970, Kimball 1990). The strength of the response is governed by prudence $P(w)$ rather than by $A(w)$ directly.

Implication for portfolio choice. Under DARA, the dollar holding of a risky asset rises with wealth (Arrow 1965): risky investment is a normal good. CARA forces a constant dollar holding, which contradicts the empirical pattern.

References: Arrow (1965 Aspects of the Theory of Risk Bearing); Kimball (1990 Econometrica); Mas-Colell, Whinston, Green Chapter 6.C; Gravelle and Rees Chapter 17.

3. Graphical illustration

For sigma=1 lottery: log utility pi = 0.10, CARA(a=0.5) pi = 0.25

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • Precautionary saving. Empirical and quantitative life-cycle work uses DARA preferences to generate the observed buffer-stock saving response to income risk; without prudence the consumption Euler equation is certainty-equivalent.
  • Risky asset as a normal good. Arrow's theorem says dollar holdings of risky assets rise with wealth iff utility is DARA. SCF and HRS data are consistent with weak DARA in the cross section.
  • Development and microinsurance. DARA rationalises why poor households appear extremely risk averse to small absolute losses, while wealthier ones tolerate larger absolute exposures.
  • Essay: prudence vs risk aversion. $A$ governs the willingness to bear risk, $P = -u'''/u''$ governs the response of saving to risk. DARA needs $P > A$, so prudence has to dominate the curvature effect.
  • Essay: ranking common utilities. Quadratic is IARA (bad), CARA is constant, log and power with $\gamma > 0$ are DARA, HARA nests all three on its domain.
  • Limitation. DARA is a qualitative property; without restricting to CRRA or HARA, it does not pin down the level of risk aversion, so calibration still needs an extra moment.
  • See also [[Concepts/CARA Utility]], [[Concepts/CRRA Utility]] and [[Concepts/Arrow-Pratt ARA]].
6 Expected Utility Tier 1

1. Intuition

A decision-maker evaluates a lottery L by $E[u(L)] = \sum_i p_i u(x_i)$, where u is the von Neumann-Morgenstern (vNM) utility function and the sum is over outcomes. The decision-maker prefers L to L' if and only if expected utility of L exceeds that of L'.

Expected utility is not utility of the expected outcome: $E[u(L)] \neq u(E[L])$ except under risk neutrality. For a risk-averse agent (u concave), $E[u(L)] < u(E[L])$, which generates the risk premium.

2. Formal mathematics

A lottery $L$ over a finite outcome set $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ is a probability vector $(p_1, \dots, p_n)$ with $p_i \geq 0$ and $\sum_i p_i = 1$. A preference relation $\succsim$ on the set of lotteries $\mathcal{L}$ admits an expected utility (vNM) representation if there exists $u : X \to \mathbb{R}$ such that

$$ L \succsim L' \iff U(L) := \sum_{i=1}^n p_i\, u(x_i) \;\geq\; \sum_{i=1}^n p'_i\, u(x_i) = U(L'). $$

The Bernoulli utility $u$ is unique up to a positive affine transformation, $u \mapsto a u + b$ with $a > 0$.

Representation theorem (von Neumann-Morgenstern). If $\succsim$ on $\mathcal{L}$ satisfies completeness, transitivity, continuity and the independence axiom, then $\succsim$ admits an expected utility representation.

  1. Fix a best lottery $\bar L$ and a worst lottery $\underline L$ with $\bar L \succ \underline L$. By continuity, for every $L$ there is a unique $\alpha(L) \in [0,1]$ such that $L \sim \alpha(L)\bar L + (1-\alpha(L))\underline L$.
  2. Independence implies $\alpha$ is linear in mixtures: $\alpha(\beta L_1 + (1-\beta) L_2) = \beta \alpha(L_1) + (1-\beta)\alpha(L_2)$.
  3. Define $U(L) = \alpha(L)$. Linearity in probabilities gives $U(L) = \sum_i p_i U(\delta_{x_i})$, so set $u(x_i) = U(\delta_{x_i})$ and recover $U(L) = \sum_i p_i u(x_i)$.

Risk attitudes. An agent is risk averse iff $u$ is concave, i.e. for any non-degenerate lottery $\tilde w$ with mean $\bar w$,

$$ E[u(\tilde w)] \leq u(\bar w) \quad \text{(Jensen).} $$

The certainty equivalent $\mathrm{CE}$ solves $u(\mathrm{CE}) = E[u(\tilde w)]$, and the risk premium is $\pi = \bar w - \mathrm{CE} \geq 0$ for a risk averse agent. Convex $u$ gives risk loving, linear $u$ risk neutral.

References: Mas-Colell, Whinston, Green Chapter 6.1 to 6.3; Varian Chapter 12; Micro2025.pdf Topic 6.

  • Insurance demand. A risk averse agent with concave $u$ pays a premium up to $\pi$ above the actuarially fair price, which explains why insurance markets exist even with positive loading.
  • Portfolio choice. Merton's two-fund result and the CAPM rest on a vNM agent maximising $E[u(\tilde w)]$ over risky returns.
  • Health and labour economics. Willingness to pay for mortality reduction is derived from $E[u]$ over alive and dead states.
  • Essay: normative vs descriptive. vNM is often defended as a coherence requirement (Dutch book style), while Kahneman and Tversky show systematic violations such as the Allais and common ratio paradoxes.
  • Essay: cardinality without interpersonal comparability. $u$ is cardinal up to affine transform, so welfare comparisons across agents still require additional assumptions.

6 Independence Axiom Tier 2

1. Intuition

If L is preferred to L', then any mixture (alpha L + (1-alpha) M) is preferred to (alpha L' + (1-alpha) M). Adding the same 'side lottery' M with the same probability does not change the ranking between L and L'.

Independence is the substantive axiom of EU. The Allais paradox shows real subjects violate it: they care about the 'sure thing' aspect of one option in a way EU theory rules out.

2. Formal mathematics

The independence axiom states that for any three lotteries $L, L', L'' \in \mathcal{L}$ and any $\alpha \in (0,1]$,

$$ L \succsim L' \iff \alpha L + (1-\alpha) L'' \;\succsim\; \alpha L' + (1-\alpha) L''. $$

Mixing both lotteries with the same third lottery $L''$ on the same odds leaves the ranking unchanged. Together with completeness, transitivity and continuity, independence is necessary and sufficient for an expected utility representation.

Linearity in probabilities. Independence forces the utility functional $U$ to be linear in the probability vector. To see this, take $L, L'$ with $U(L) = U(L')$ and apply independence with $L''$ arbitrary,

$$ U(\alpha L + (1-\alpha) L'') = \alpha U(L) + (1-\alpha) U(L''). $$

Linearity is exactly what makes indifference curves in the probability simplex straight and parallel.

  1. Consider three outcomes $x_1 < x_2 < x_3$. A lottery is a point $(p_1, p_3)$ in the Marschak-Machina triangle, with $p_2 = 1 - p_1 - p_3$.
  2. Expected utility is $U = p_1 u_1 + (1-p_1-p_3)u_2 + p_3 u_3$, linear in $(p_1, p_3)$.
  3. Indifference curves $U = \mathrm{const}$ are therefore parallel straight lines with slope $-(u_2 - u_1)/(u_3 - u_2)$.

Allais paradox. Empirically, indifference curves "fan out" near the certainty edge, violating parallelism. Subjects prefer a sure 1m to a lottery with a small chance of 0, yet flip the preference once a common 0.89 probability of 0 is added to both. This is a direct violation of independence with $L''$ equal to "win 0 with probability 1".

References: Mas-Colell, Whinston, Green Chapter 6.B; Machina (1987 JEP); Micro2025.pdf Topic 6 lecture notes.

3. Graphical illustration

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • Allais common consequence paradox. Choices over (1m sure vs 0.10 chance of 5m, 0.89 of 1m, 0.01 of 0) and over (0.11 chance of 1m vs 0.10 chance of 5m) routinely reverse, a textbook independence violation.
  • Common ratio paradox. Scaling both lotteries by the same factor changes choices, again contradicting independence.
  • Rabin calibration. Concave EU with reasonable small stakes risk aversion implies absurd large stakes risk aversion, suggesting the failure is not just probabilities but the EU functional itself.
  • Essay: normative defence. Independence can be motivated by a "sure thing" argument: if the third branch is irrelevant when realised, it should be irrelevant ex ante. Debates pit this against the descriptive evidence.
  • Essay: weakening independence. Machina's local EU, rank-dependent utility (Quiggin) and cumulative prospect theory all relax independence while keeping monotonicity.
  • Limitation. Without independence, dynamic consistency typically breaks, raising the question of whether to model agents as sophisticated, naive or resolute.

Excluded sub-topics

The catalogue contains no clear Part B long-answer question whose primary content is pure adverse selection in the 2014 to 2025 window. The Information Economics long-answer slot in those years is held by moral hazard problems (2014 Q11, 2017 Q10, 2024 Q10), which belong in Question Types 6 - Moral Hazard. The 2024 Q9 essay sits at the boundary of adverse selection and moral hazard and is cross-listed here.

Also excluded: pure moral hazard sub-topics (hidden action, principal agent, optimal incentive contracts), pure risk-without-information topics (Arrow Pratt, certainty equivalent, risk premium without an information friction). These live in Question Types 4 - Decisions under Risk and Question Types 6 - Moral Hazard.

Part A: short compulsory problems
Sub-question Type A.1: Akerlof lemons with discrete quality types, full unravelling
  • What it asks: Given a market for used goods (cars, bicycles) with a discrete distribution of quality types and buyers who cannot observe quality, identify which types trade at which prices, find the equilibrium price, and judge efficiency.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. List the types, their seller reservation values $v_s^i$, and the buyer values $v_b^i$. Note that the buyer typically values quality at $v_b^i = v_s^i + k$ where $k$ is the gain from trade.
  2. Compute the full-information benchmark: every type with $v_b^i > v_s^i$ trades at a price in $[v_s^i, v_b^i]$. Note total surplus.
  3. Under asymmetric information, the buyer pays one pooled price $p$ for a unit of unknown type. Compute the buyer's expected value $E[v_b \mid \text{types selling at } p]$. The buyer is willing to pay up to $E[v_b]$.
  4. Apply the seller participation condition: type $i$ sells if $p \ge v_s^i$. The set of selling types is the set with $v_s^i \le p$.
  5. Solve the fixed point: a candidate $p$ is an equilibrium if $p = E[v_b \mid v_s^i \le p]$, or, if $p > E[v_b]$ no trade occurs, or if $p < v_s^{\min}$ nothing is sold.
  6. Check for Concepts/Full Unravelling: start with the pool that includes the highest type. If $E[v_b]$ over that pool is less than $v_s^{\text{top}}$, the top type withdraws. Iterate, dropping the next-highest type each round, until either an equilibrium pool remains or the market collapses to nothing.
  7. State efficiency: any equilibrium where a type with $v_b^i > v_s^i$ fails to trade is inefficient. The wedge is the asymmetric information friction, not preferences.
  8. If asked about a parameter (e.g. the gain $k$): find the threshold value of $k$ above which all types trade in one pool. At that threshold, $p = E[v_b]$ over all types and $p \ge v_s^{\text{top}}$.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2016 Q3 (used bicycles, three quality levels X, Y, Z, three consumer types)
  • 2018 Q4 (second-hand bicycles, three types, hidden quality)
  • 2025 Q4 (used cars, 2H + 1M + 2L, full trade when $k \ge 24$, and separate analysis for $k = 20$ and $k = 5$)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Akerlof Lemons Market, Concepts/Adverse Selection, Concepts/Full Unravelling, Concepts/Asymmetric Information, Concepts/Pooling Equilibrium.
Sub-question Type A.2: Threshold for full trade and the role of the gain-from-trade parameter
  • What it asks: Find the smallest value of the gain-from-trade parameter $k$ (i.e. the buyer's premium over the seller's reservation) such that all types trade at a single pooled price, and state the equilibrium price.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Write $v_b^i = v_s^i + k$ for each type $i$.
  2. The pooled price under full trade is $p = E[v_b] = \bar v_s + k$ where $\bar v_s$ is the average seller reservation across types.
  3. The binding participation constraint is the highest type's: $p \ge v_s^{\text{top}}$. Substitute: $\bar v_s + k \ge v_s^{\text{top}}$, so $k \ge v_s^{\text{top}} - \bar v_s$.
  4. Plug in numbers. For 2025 Q4 with $v_s = (60, 36, 12)$ in shares $(2, 1, 2)/5$, $\bar v_s = (2 \cdot 60 + 36 + 2 \cdot 12)/5 = 36$, so $k \ge 60 - 36 = 24$. At $k = 24$, $p = 60$, and the top types are exactly indifferent.
  5. State efficiency: at $k$ above threshold every gainful trade occurs, so the outcome is first-best efficient, asymmetric information happens to be non-binding.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2025 Q4(a)(b)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Akerlof Lemons Market, Concepts/Full Unravelling, Concepts/Pareto Efficiency.
Sub-question Type A.3: Partial trade and market unravelling at intermediate parameter values
  • What it asks: When the gain-from-trade parameter is below the full-trade threshold, identify which types still trade, find the new pooled price, and check whether the market collapses entirely.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Start by assuming all types trade. Check the top type's participation: $\bar v_s + k \ge v_s^{\text{top}}$. If it fails the top type drops out.
  2. Recompute $\bar v_s$ over the remaining pool. Add $k$. Check the new top type's participation.
  3. Iterate. Each round can only remove the top remaining type.
  4. If a pool is reached where every remaining seller is willing to trade at the pooled $E[v_b]$, that is an equilibrium. The price is $E[v_b \mid \text{pool}]$.
  5. If the iteration reaches the L-only pool and the L type still trades, the equilibrium is L-only at $p = v_s^L + k$. If even the L type fails to participate, the market collapses entirely.
  6. Note inefficiency: any types with $v_b^i > v_s^i$ who do not trade represent lost gains from trade. For 2025 Q4 at $k = 20$, H drops out, the M+L pool has $\bar v_s = (36 + 24)/3 = 20$, oh wait recompute with shares: M + L has 1 + 2 sellers, $\bar v_s = (36 + 2 \cdot 12)/3 = 20$, so $p = 20 + 20 = 40$, M needs $p \ge 36$ tick, L needs $p \ge 12$ tick. Check H: $p = 40 < 60$, H out, consistent. Efficiency: H types had $v_b^H = 80 > v_s^H = 60$, the H trade is lost.
  7. For $k = 5$, full pool: $\bar v_s + k = 41 < 60$, H out. M + L pool: $\bar v_s + k = 25 < 36$, M out. L only: $p = 12 + 5 = 17 \ge 12$, L trades. Equilibrium is L only at $p = 17$. Both H and M gains from trade are lost.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2025 Q4(c) ($k = 20$ and $k = 5$)
  • 2016 Q3(b) (asymmetric info equilibrium starting from X owners)
  • 2018 Q4(b) (market equilibrium with hidden quality)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Full Unravelling, Concepts/Adverse Selection, Concepts/Asymmetric Information.
Sub-question Type A.4: Reimbursement, warranty, or money-back guarantee restoring efficiency
  • What it asks: A contract feature lets the buyer reclaim part of the price if the good is bad. Show whether this restores the efficient outcome, and find any other equilibria.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Let the reimbursement (refund) be $R$, paid by the seller to the buyer if the good turns out to be the lowest type.
  2. Write the buyer's expected payment as the price $p$ minus the expected refund. If only the bottom type triggers a refund of $R$, the buyer pays $p - \pi_L R$ in expectation, where $\pi_L$ is the conditional probability of getting a low type given the pool.
  3. Write the seller's net receipt: high or medium types receive $p$, low type receives $p - R$.
  4. Try the candidate efficient equilibrium: all types pool at one $p$. For the L type to still want to trade we need $p - R \ge v_s^L$. For the H type we still need $p \ge v_s^H$.
  5. The break-even price under the buyer's willingness to pay is $E[v_b] = \bar v_b$, and the seller's revenue averaged across types is $p - \pi_L R$, which must equal $\bar v_b$ in a competitive market.
  6. Verify Concepts/Incentive Compatibility: a low-type seller does not want to imitate a high-type seller because the refund is triggered by realised quality, not by the seller's claim. The refund acts as a quality bond.
  7. Show that with sufficient $R$, the L type still finds it weakly worthwhile to sell, while the H type is willing to participate at the pooled price. Conclude the Concepts/Reimbursement Contracts equilibrium is efficient.
  8. Identify other equilibria: there are usually pooling equilibria where only some types trade, or no-trade equilibria sustained by pessimistic off-path beliefs.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2018 Q4(c) (reimbursement of 50 restoring efficiency)
  • 2018 Q4(d) (other inefficient equilibria)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Reimbursement Contracts, Concepts/Akerlof Lemons Market, Concepts/Pareto Efficiency, Concepts/Incentive Compatibility.
Sub-question Type A.5: Spence signalling, why H wants to signal and the credibility / single-crossing condition
  • What it asks: In a Spence labour market with high and low productivity workers, explain when high-productivity workers want to incur an education signal, and state the conditions under which the signal is credible.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Set up two types $H$ and $L$ with productivities $\theta_H > \theta_L$, and a costly signal $e$ (years of education) with cost $c_H(e) < c_L(e)$ per unit and $c_H'(e) < c_L'(e)$. This is the Concepts/Single Crossing Property.
  2. Under full information each type earns its productivity: $w_H = \theta_H$, $w_L = \theta_L$.
  3. Under asymmetric information with no signal, competitive firms pay the pooled wage $\bar w = \lambda \theta_H + (1 - \lambda) \theta_L$ where $\lambda$ is the H share. The L type is paid above productivity, the H type below.
  4. H wants to signal because under pooling the H type subsidises the L type and earns $\bar w < \theta_H$. By acquiring a signal that L cannot profitably mimic, H separates and is paid $\theta_H$.
  5. The signal is credible in a Concepts/Separating vs Pooling Equilibrium separating equilibrium if (i) H prefers signalling to mimicking L: $\theta_H - c_H(e^) \ge \theta_L$, and (ii) L prefers no signal to mimicking H: $\theta_L \ge \theta_H - c_L(e^)$. Combine: $\theta_H - \theta_L \le c_L(e^)$ and $\theta_H - \theta_L \ge c_H(e^)$.
  6. The smallest signal level satisfying both is $e^$ such that $c_L(e^) = \theta_H - \theta_L$ (L just indifferent between signalling and not). This is the least-cost separating signal selected by the Concepts/Intuitive Criterion.
  7. Comment on efficiency: H earns $\theta_H - c_H(e^*)$, L earns $\theta_L$, the signal cost is pure waste because education here is unproductive, so separating is socially worse than pooling on welfare grounds, even though the individual H is better off.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2017 Q4(a) (why H workers want to signal, credibility conditions)
  • 2020 Q4(a) (why H types signal, credibility)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Spence Signalling, Concepts/Single Crossing Property, Concepts/Separating vs Pooling Equilibrium, Concepts/Intuitive Criterion.
Sub-question Type A.6: Spence signalling, pooling wage with no signal available
  • What it asks: If the signal is unavailable (or forbidden by law), what is the competitive wage and how do the two types fare?
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Without a signal, no information transmits. Competitive firms break even on the average productivity: $w_{\text{pool}} = \lambda \theta_H + (1 - \lambda) \theta_L$.
  2. Both types earn the same wage. L is overpaid, H is underpaid.
  3. Check that H does not refuse to participate. If H has an outside option (home production) below $w_{\text{pool}}$, H stays in the labour market. If H's outside option exceeds $w_{\text{pool}}$, H drops out and the market unravels exactly as in Concepts/Akerlof Lemons Market.
  4. State that the no-signal pooling outcome can be socially superior to costly separation because no real resources are burned, even though distribution is regressive in productivity.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2017 Q4(b) (pooling wage when no signal available)
  • 2020 Q4(b) (pooling equilibrium no signal)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Pooling Equilibrium, Concepts/Spence Signalling.
Sub-question Type A.7: Spence signalling, checking a candidate separating outcome and finding the equilibrium
  • What it asks: Given specific cost numbers for the H and L types of acquiring the signal, check whether the candidate (H signals, L does not) is incentive compatible, find the equilibrium wage schedule, and comment on efficiency.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Write down the candidate: H takes signal $e$ at cost $c_H$, paid $w_H = \theta_H$. L takes no signal, paid $w_L = \theta_L$.
  2. Check H's Concepts/Incentive Compatibility: $\theta_H - c_H \ge \theta_L$, i.e. $c_H \le \theta_H - \theta_L$.
  3. Check L's Concepts/Incentive Compatibility: $\theta_L \ge \theta_H - c_L$, i.e. $c_L \ge \theta_H - \theta_L$.
  4. If both hold (e.g. 2017 Q4 with $c_H = 45 < \theta_H - \theta_L$ and $c_L = 55 > \theta_H - \theta_L$ for some plausible numbers), the candidate is an equilibrium. State so.
  5. Apply the Concepts/Intuitive Criterion to refine if multiple separating equilibria exist: the surviving equilibrium uses the lowest $e^*$ that still deters L. Higher signal levels are dominated for H.
  6. Comment on efficiency: total output under separating is $\lambda \theta_H + (1 - \lambda) \theta_L$ minus the signal cost $\lambda c_H$. Total output under pooling is also $\lambda \theta_H + (1 - \lambda) \theta_L$ with no cost. Pooling is socially better if the signal is unproductive. Separating is privately better for H, worse for L.
  7. If asked about a "semi-separating" or alternative equilibrium: try mixing by one type and find a wage schedule that makes the mixing type indifferent.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2017 Q4(c) (separating outcome at costs 45 vs 55)
  • 2020 Q4(c) (check separating, find alternative equilibrium, efficiency comment)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Spence Signalling, Concepts/Separating vs Pooling Equilibrium, Concepts/Intuitive Criterion, Concepts/Incentive Compatibility.
Sub-question Type A.8: Rothschild Stiglitz separating equilibrium in competitive insurance (state-space diagram)
  • What it asks: In a competitive insurance market with high and low risk types, derive the separating equilibrium where H gets full insurance and L gets partial insurance at a fair premium for L's risk type, and verify both incentive compatibility and zero-profit conditions.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Set up the Concepts/State-Space Insurance Diagram. The horizontal axis is wealth in the no-loss state $W_1$, the vertical axis is wealth in the loss state $W_2$. The 45-degree line is full insurance.
  2. Mark the endowment $E = (W, W - L)$, where $W$ is initial wealth and $L$ is the loss.
  3. Draw the Concepts/Fair Odds Line through $E$ for each risk type. The H line has slope $-(1 - \pi_H)/\pi_H$, the L line has slope $-(1 - \pi_L)/\pi_L$ with $\pi_H > \pi_L$. The L line is steeper. Both lines pass through $E$.
  4. Each type's indifference curves are smooth and convex, with slope at the 45-degree line equal to the type's fair-odds line. Curves of different types cross only once: this is Concepts/Single Crossing Property in insurance form. H's curves are flatter at any point than L's curves passing through the same point.
  5. The first contract: $\alpha_H$ is full insurance on H's fair-odds line. This is the point where H's fair-odds line meets the 45-degree line, giving wealth $W - \pi_H L$ in both states.
  6. The second contract $\alpha_L$ is the point on L's fair-odds line that just makes H indifferent between $\alpha_H$ and $\alpha_L$. Solve $u_H(\alpha_L) = u_H(\alpha_H)$ on L's fair-odds line. Geometrically, slide down L's fair-odds line from full insurance until you hit H's indifference curve through $\alpha_H$.
  7. Verify Concepts/Incentive Compatibility: H weakly prefers $\alpha_H$ (binding by construction). L strictly prefers $\alpha_L$ to $\alpha_H$, because L's indifference curves are steeper, so L's curve through $\alpha_L$ lies above H's curve through $\alpha_L$ on the L-fair-odds line.
  8. Verify zero profit: each contract sits on the offering type's fair-odds line, so the insurer breaks even type by type.
  9. Verify no profitable deviation: any contract that attracts only L can be offered, but every such contract lies below $\alpha_L$ on L's fair-odds line and thus loses L's business. Any pooling contract that attracts both types must lie below the pooled fair-odds line and is cream-skimmed away by an entrant.
  10. For the existence question (2014 Q2 part e): a pooling contract on the pooled fair-odds line, weighted by $\lambda$, can attract both types if H prefers it to $\alpha_H$. The separating equilibrium exists when the share of L types $\lambda$ is high enough that the pooled fair-odds line is too unattractive for H to prefer pooling. If $\lambda$ is low (many H types, pooled rate close to H's rate), the pooling deviation succeeds and no separating equilibrium exists, giving the non-existence problem.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2014 Q2(a) (state-space diagram and explanation)
  • 2014 Q2(b) (H premium and compensation)
  • 2014 Q2(c) (H expected utility)
  • 2014 Q2(d) (two conditions on (P, C) for L contract, verify $C \approx 2$)
  • 2014 Q2(e) (existence as function of $\lambda$)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Rothschild-Stiglitz Screening, Concepts/State-Space Insurance Diagram, Concepts/Fair Odds Line, Concepts/Single Crossing Property, Concepts/Incentive Compatibility, Concepts/Separating vs Pooling Equilibrium, Concepts/Adverse Selection.
Sub-question Type A.9: Insurance under different regulatory regimes (observable vs unobservable type, common price rule)
  • What it asks: Compare equilibrium premiums under three regimes: (i) types observable so each gets a type-specific fair premium, (ii) types unobservable but firms can offer menu of contracts, (iii) regulation forces a common price across types.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Regime (i) observable types: each type $j$ pays the Concepts/Fair Odds Line premium $p_j = \pi_j$ per unit cover. Full insurance is offered to each type, since competition pushes contracts to the 45-degree line on each type's fair-odds line.
  2. Compute $p_L = \pi_L$ and $p_H = \pi_H$ and note that the H premium exceeds the L premium.
  3. Compare full insurance versus partial insurance: at fair premium, risk-averse agents always demand full insurance, so partial insurance is dominated.
  4. Regime (ii) common price (regulatory): the regulator forces a single price $p$ across both types. Firms break even if and only if $p = \lambda \pi_L + (1 - \lambda) \pi_H$, the pooled probability. Demand at price $p$: the H type wants more than fair coverage, the L type wants less.
  5. If the contract is full insurance at the pooled price: H is happy (pays below fair), L is overcharged.
  6. The break-even price for partial insurance under a common-price rule equals the pooled $\pi$ as long as both types still participate.
  7. Compare: under regime (i) the L type pays $\pi_L$ and the H type pays $\pi_H$, total surplus is at first best. Under regime (ii) the L type cross-subsidises the H type, the L type may underinsure or drop out. If L drops out, only H remains and the price rises to $\pi_H$ (unravelling).
  8. Conclude: the common-price rule creates Concepts/Adverse Selection within the regulated market, and may be welfare-reducing if it triggers L exit. It can still be defended on equity or solidarity grounds.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2022 Q4(a) (observable types: full vs partial insurance, find $p_L, p_H$)
  • 2022 Q4(b) (common price: full vs partial insurance break-even)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Fair Odds Line, Concepts/Rothschild-Stiglitz Screening, Concepts/Adverse Selection, Concepts/State-Space Insurance Diagram.
Sub-question Type A.10: Screening with limited coverage at fair premium, comparing welfare to full coverage
  • What it asks: A monopolist or competitive insurer offers a menu where the high type buys full insurance at a high premium and the low type is offered limited coverage at a fair-for-low premium. Compute coverages, premiums, expected utility, and compare welfare.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Set up Cornelia with log utility $u(w) = \ln w$, wealth $W$, loss $L$, loss probability $\pi$. Optimal at fair premium $p = \pi$ is full coverage with consumption $W - \pi L$ in both states.
  2. At a non-fair price $p > \pi$, the optimum is partial coverage. The first-order condition is $\frac{u'(W_1)}{u'(W_2)} = \frac{p}{1 - p} \cdot \frac{1 - \pi}{\pi}$ where $W_1 = W - p q$ and $W_2 = W - L + q - p q$ and $q$ is coverage. With log utility, $W_2 / W_1 = (1 - p) \pi / (p (1 - \pi))$, then solve for $q$.
  3. At fair price $p = \pi$, the FOC collapses to $W_1 = W_2$, giving $q = L$ (full coverage). This is the Concepts/Individual Rationality benchmark.
  4. Under screening with limited coverage at a fair price, the insurer offers coverage $q^*$ less than $L$ at price $\pi$. This is incentive compatible against the H type if H prefers full insurance at a higher price to limited cover at the L fair price.
  5. Compute Cornelia's expected utility under each contract: full cover at price $1/2$, full cover at fair $1/3$, limited cover $q^*$ at fair $1/3$.
  6. Welfare comparison: a limited-coverage contract at a fair premium can dominate a full-coverage contract at an unfair premium for the L type. Conditional on being asked to compare to "the original" (typically the unfair-premium full insurance), the screening contract is superior for L.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2023 Q4(a) (optimal coverage at $p = 1/2$)
  • 2023 Q4(b) (coverage at fair price $p = 1/3$)
  • 2023 Q4(c) (competitive screening with limited coverage at fair premium)
  • 2023 Q4(d) (welfare comparison)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Rothschild-Stiglitz Screening, Concepts/Fair Odds Line, Concepts/Individual Rationality, Concepts/Incentive Compatibility, Concepts/State-Space Insurance Diagram.
Part B: essay-style questions
Sub-question Type B.E.1: Are adverse selection and moral hazard equally relevant under asymmetric information and equally irrelevant under symmetric information?
  • What it asks: Discuss whether the two information frictions are symmetric in importance, when each one binds, and what symmetric information implies for each.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis: agree that both vanish under symmetric information by definition, but reject the equal-relevance claim. The two frictions differ in timing (before vs after contracting), in the variable hidden (type vs action), in the contractual remedies, and in welfare consequences. The relative importance depends on the application.
  2. Define crisply: Concepts/Adverse Selection is hidden information before contracting, the uninformed party cannot tell types apart at offer time. Concepts/Moral Hazard is hidden action after contracting, the contract changes the informed party's incentives to take an unobserved action. Cite Concepts/Akerlof Lemons Market for AS and the principal agent model for MH.
  3. Symmetric-information benchmark: under full observability, AS contracts are type-specific (Walrasian first-best), MH contracts pay for the observed action. Both frictions are absent and both deliver first-best. The claim's second half is correct.
  4. Why AS bites: signal worse types out (lemons), full unravelling can destroy markets. Use the 2016 / 2025 used-goods examples. Remedies: signalling (Spence), screening (Rothschild Stiglitz), warranties / reimbursement, mandatory disclosure.
  5. Why MH bites: principal must pay a risk-averse agent based on noisy output, generating an Concepts/Agency Cost (the wedge between observed and unobserved effort surplus). Remedies: monitoring, performance pay, output-based contracts.
  6. Asymmetry of relevance: AS dominates markets where heterogeneity in types matters and effort is fixed (health insurance, used cars). MH dominates where effort is variable and types are similar (managerial pay, sharecropping). Many real markets have both, e.g. insurance with risk types and care effort.
  7. Welfare framings differ: AS welfare loss comes from missing trades and signalling waste. MH welfare loss is the agency cost paid in risk to the risk-averse agent. The two are not commensurable, so saying they are "equally relevant" is meaningless without context.
  8. Symmetric info caveat: under symmetric information, AS truly vanishes (types are priced). MH technically also vanishes if the action is contractible. There is one subtle case: even with symmetric information, contracts may be incomplete (third-party non-verifiability), in which case MH-like distortions can remain. This nuances the claim's second half.
  9. Conclusion: the claim is half right. Symmetric info kills both. Under asymmetric info, the two are distinct frictions with distinct remedies and distinct welfare costs, and their relative importance is empirical, not theoretical.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2024 Q9
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Adverse Selection, Concepts/Moral Hazard, Concepts/Asymmetric Information, Concepts/Akerlof Lemons Market, Concepts/Spence Signalling, Concepts/Rothschild-Stiglitz Screening, Concepts/Agency Cost.
Sub-question Type B.E.2 (anticipated): Evaluate Akerlof's contribution to information economics
  • What it asks: A likely future prompt, not in the 2014 to 2025 set, asking how Akerlof's 1970 lemons paper changed the agenda.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis: Akerlof showed that asymmetric information alone, with no other friction, can destroy a market that has positive gains from trade. This put information at the centre of microeconomics and triggered the signalling-screening literature.
  2. The model: used cars, sellers know quality, buyers do not. Pool price equals expected quality. High-quality sellers exit. Iterate to the Concepts/Full Unravelling result.
  3. Welfare consequence: the unique equilibrium is inefficient. This breaks the first welfare theorem assumption that all goods are tradable at a single price reflecting their value.
  4. Mechanism design successors: signalling (Spence 1973) lets the informed side reveal type at a cost. Screening (Rothschild Stiglitz 1976) lets the uninformed side design menus.
  5. Empirical relevance: insurance, credit, labour, healthcare. Briefly cite one stylised application.
  6. Limits: full unravelling rarely seen in clean form because warranties, repeat purchase, certification, and regulation exist. The point is the threat, not the literal collapse.
  7. Conclusion: Akerlof's contribution is the framework, not the precise market collapse story. Information economics is now load-bearing in IO, labour, and contract theory.
  • Appeared in: Not in 2014 to 2025 catalogue, flagged as likely future essay.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Akerlof Lemons Market, Concepts/Full Unravelling, Concepts/Spence Signalling, Concepts/Rothschild-Stiglitz Screening, Concepts/Adverse Selection.
Sub-question Type B.E.3 (anticipated): Signalling versus screening, which is the better cure for adverse selection?
  • What it asks: A likely future prompt comparing the two mechanism-design responses.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis: signalling and screening solve adverse selection from opposite sides, the informed side acts first in signalling, the uninformed side acts first in screening. They are dual and complementary, not substitutes.
  2. Signalling (Spence): informed types pay a costly signal whose marginal cost differs by type (Concepts/Single Crossing Property). Separation requires the signal cost to lie between the productivity differential's bounds.
  3. Screening (Rothschild Stiglitz): uninformed firms offer a menu, types self-select. The H type gets the efficient bundle, the L type gets a distorted bundle just enough to deter H mimicry.
  4. Welfare comparison: signalling can be wasteful if the signal has no productive value (education-as-signal critique). Screening is wasteful if the L type's bundle is distorted away from L's first best.
  5. Existence: screening can fail to exist (Rothschild Stiglitz non-existence under low $\lambda$). Signalling equilibria are usually multiple and require a refinement like the Concepts/Intuitive Criterion.
  6. Empirical: education signalling vs job-trial screening, life-insurance medicals vs employer-paid blood tests.
  7. Conclusion: neither is universally better. Use signalling when the informed side can credibly burn resources, use screening when the uninformed side can credibly commit to a menu.
  • Appeared in: Not in 2014 to 2025 catalogue, flagged as likely future essay.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Spence Signalling, Concepts/Rothschild-Stiglitz Screening, Concepts/Single Crossing Property, Concepts/Intuitive Criterion.

7 Adverse Selection Tier 1

1. Intuition

When one side of a market has private information about its type before contracting, the uninformed side faces an adverse selection of types willing to trade at a given price. The classic example is Akerlof's used-car market.

Adverse selection can shut down trade entirely (full unravelling) or reduce its scope. Solutions include signalling (informed party reveals type) and screening (uninformed party offers a menu inducing self-selection).

2. Formal mathematics

Akerlof's lemons model. Sellers know quality $q \in [0, \bar q]$ with density $f(q)$; buyers see only the distribution. Buyers' willingness to pay is $\beta \cdot \mathbb{E}[q \mid \text{traded}]$ with $\beta > 1$, sellers' reservation is $q$ itself.

  1. At candidate price $p$, only types with $q \leq p$ choose to sell.
  2. Conditional expected quality is $\bar q(p) = \mathbb{E}[q \mid q \leq p]$.
  3. Market-clearing price satisfies $p = \beta \cdot \bar q(p)$.
  4. If $\beta \cdot \bar q(p) < p$ for every $p > 0$, the only equilibrium has zero trade (full unravelling). With a uniform $[0,1]$ distribution and $\beta < 2$, this is the unique outcome.

The market breaks because each price selects only worse-than-expected sellers, dragging the price down to the floor. See Lecture 7, Gravelle and Rees Ch 19.

3. Graphical illustration

Demand line for $\beta = 1.5$ sits below the supply line for all $q > 0$, so the lemons market unravels to zero trade.

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • Health insurance markets (US, pre-ACA). High-risk types disproportionately buy; community-rated premia rise; healthy types drop out. The ACA's individual mandate was a direct response.
  • Used cars (Akerlof 1970). Original motivating example. Mitigations: dealer warranties, lemon laws, manufacturer-certified pre-owned schemes.
  • Job markets for senior hires. Outside options are private; high types may signal via track record, degrees, or interview tests.
  • Essay move (2024 Q9). Contrast AS with moral hazard: AS is about hidden type at the moment of contracting; MH is about hidden action after contracting. Both vanish under symmetric information; under asymmetric information, only one is relevant per context.
  • Essay move. Mitigations: signalling (Spence), screening (Rothschild-Stiglitz), mandatory pooling (ACA, social insurance), warranties (reimbursement contracts).
  • See also [[Concepts/Akerlof Lemons Market]], [[Concepts/Spence Signalling]], [[Concepts/Rothschild-Stiglitz Screening]], [[Concepts/Moral Hazard]].

Part A: short compulsory problems
Sub-question Type A.1: First-best contract when effort is observable
  • What it asks: Risk-neutral principal hires risk-averse agent with utility $v(w) = \sqrt{w}$ (or log) and reservation utility $\bar u$. Effort $e$ is observable and contractible. Find the wage that implements a given effort level.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Observation point: when effort is observable the principal writes a forcing contract, "pay $w^*$ if you choose $e$, pay zero (or a punishment) otherwise". The IC constraint is slack.
  2. Risk-sharing principle: a risk-neutral principal and risk-averse agent share risk efficiently when the agent bears no risk. So pay a constant wage in every state.
  3. Bind IR: the principal pays the lowest constant wage that meets participation. With $v(w) = \sqrt{w}$ and effort cost $c(e)$:

    4. $$\sqrt{w^} - c(e) = \bar u \implies w^ = (\bar u + c(e))^2.$$

  4. Compute principal's expected profit: $\Pi^{FB}(e) = \pi_L(e) x_L + \pi_H(e) x_H - w^*(e)$.
  5. Pick optimal effort: principal picks the $e$ that maximises $\Pi^{FB}(e)$ across the allowed effort levels.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q3(a), 2019 Q4(a), 2023 Q3(a), 2024 Q10(a) (low-effort baseline).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Principal-Agent Problem, Concepts/First-Best Contract, Concepts/Individual Rationality, Concepts/Risk vs Insurance Trade-off.
Part B: essay-style questions
Sub-question Type B.E.1: Evaluate statements on the principal-agent problem (2022 Q9)
  • What it asks: A two-profit, two-effort principal-agent setup with risk-averse agent. Three statements are given, often along the lines of "(i) the agent always bears risk, (ii) the agency cost rises in the agent's risk aversion, (iii) if the principal is also risk-averse, the contract becomes first-best." The candidate must evaluate each.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  1. Intro thesis: set up the canonical model in one paragraph. Two outcomes $x_L < x_H$, two efforts $e_L < e_H$ with $\pi_H(e_H) > \pi_H(e_L)$. Risk-neutral principal, risk-averse agent with $v(w) = \sqrt{w}$, effort cost $c(\cdot)$, reservation utility $\bar u$. State that the IC and IR constraints both bind when the principal wants to implement $e_H$.
  2. Statement (i) "the agent must bear wage risk to provide incentives": true when implementing $e_H$ under unobservable effort, since the IC constraint requires $w_H > w_L$. False when implementing $e_L$ since constant wage works. Also false in the first-best.
  3. Statement (ii) "agency cost rises in risk aversion": true. With a more concave utility (say $v(w) = w^{1/\rho}$ with higher $\rho$), the agent demands a larger risk premium for the same wage spread, so the principal pays more in expectation. Formally, the Jensen gap between $E[w]$ and $w^{FB}$ widens.
  4. Statement (iii) "if principal is risk-averse too, contract becomes first-best": false in general. The principal becoming risk-averse changes the trade-off but does not remove the IC constraint, which is the source of the second-best inefficiency. What changes is risk-sharing: optimal contracts shift some risk back to the principal.
  5. Conclusion: tie together. The friction is hidden action plus risk aversion together. Either alone is harmless: observable effort gives first-best regardless of risk aversion, and a risk-neutral agent gives first-best via "sell the firm to the agent".
  • Appeared in: 2022 Q9.

Part B: long-answer problems
Sub-question Type B.L.1: Two-state principal-agent with two effort levels (canonical)
  • What it asks: Risk-neutral principal hires risk-averse agent with $v(w) = \sqrt{w}$. Two outcomes $x_L, x_H$ with probabilities depending on effort. Two effort levels $e_L < e_H$ with costs $c_L < c_H$. Reservation utility $\bar u$. Find first-best and second-best contracts, the principal's profit under each, and the agency cost.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  1. Set up notation: wages $w_L, w_H$ in low and high outcome, utility $v(w) = \sqrt{w}$, effort cost $c(e)$, probabilities $p_L(e), p_H(e) = 1 - p_L(e)$.
  2. First-best (observable effort): principal pays a constant wage $w^$ such that $\sqrt{w^} = \bar u + c(e)$, so $w^ = (\bar u + c(e))^2$. The principal picks the effort that maximises $\pi_L(e) x_L + \pi_H(e) x_H - w^(e)$. The agent bears no risk, full risk-sharing is achieved.
  3. Second-best (unobservable effort): principal solves

$$\min_{w_L, w_H} \pi_L(e_H) w_L + \pi_H(e_H) w_H$$ subject to IR: $\pi_L(e_H) \sqrt{w_L} + \pi_H(e_H) \sqrt{w_H} - c(e_H) \geq \bar u$, and IC: $\pi_L(e_H) \sqrt{w_L} + \pi_H(e_H) \sqrt{w_H} - c(e_H) \geq \pi_L(e_L) \sqrt{w_L} + \pi_H(e_L) \sqrt{w_H} - c(e_L)$.

  1. Lagrangian: $L = \pi_L^H w_L + \pi_H^H w_H - \lambda [\pi_L^H \sqrt{w_L} + \pi_H^H \sqrt{w_H} - c_H - \bar u] - \mu [(\pi_L^H - \pi_L^L) \sqrt{w_L} + (\pi_H^H - \pi_H^L) \sqrt{w_H} - (c_H - c_L)]$, where I write $\pi_s^e = \pi_s(e)$ for brevity.
  2. FOCs: $\partial L / \partial w_L = \pi_L^H - \lambda \pi_L^H \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{w_L}} - \mu (\pi_L^H - \pi_L^L) \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{w_L}} = 0$, and analogously for $w_H$. Rearrange:

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{w_s}} = \frac{1}{\lambda + \mu \cdot (1 - \pi_s^L / \pi_s^H)} \cdot \frac{2}{1}$$ after dividing by $\pi_s^H$. The key takeaway: $\sqrt{w_s}$ is a decreasing function of the likelihood ratio $\pi_s^L / \pi_s^H$ (Mirrlees-Holmstrom sufficient statistic).

  1. Linear system shortcut: rather than chase Lagrange multipliers, use that both constraints bind. Let $u_s = \sqrt{w_s}$. The two constraints are linear in $(u_L, u_H)$:

    2. $$\pi_L^H u_L + \pi_H^H u_H = \bar u + c_H, \qquad (\pi_H^H - \pi_H^L)(u_H - u_L) = c_H - c_L.$$

  2. Closed form for the spread:

$$u_H - u_L = \frac{c_H - c_L}{\pi_H^H - \pi_H^L} \equiv \Delta.$$ This is the "incentive spread". It rises in the cost gap $c_H - c_L$ and falls in the informativeness of the outcome about effort $\pi_H^H - \pi_H^L$.

  1. Solve for individual wages: from IR, $u_L = \bar u + c_H - \pi_H^H \cdot \Delta$ and $u_H = u_L + \Delta$. Then $w_s = u_s^2$.
  2. Expected wage bill under second-best: $E[w^{SB}] = \pi_L^H u_L^2 + \pi_H^H u_H^2$. By Jensen, since $u_L \neq u_H$, $E[w^{SB}] > (E[u])^2 = (\bar u + c_H)^2 = w^{FB}(e_H)$.
  3. Agency cost: $AC = E[w^{SB}] - w^{FB}(e_H) > 0$. The strict inequality comes from the agent's concave utility times the wage spread $\Delta$.
  4. Pick optimal effort under second-best: compare net profit of implementing $e_H$ (= $\pi_L^H x_L + \pi_H^H x_H - E[w^{SB}]$) against $e_L$ (= $\pi_L^L x_L + \pi_H^L x_H - (\bar u + c_L)^2$). The principal picks whichever is larger.
  5. Possible reversal: if the agency cost is large enough, the principal switches from $e_H$ under first-best to $e_L$ under second-best. This is "effort distortion" on top of the risk-sharing distortion.

8 Agency Cost Tier 1

1. Intuition

The difference between the principal's expected profit under the first-best (observable effort) and the second-best (unobservable effort) contracts. Measures the welfare loss from the information asymmetry, expressed in pounds.

Agency cost rises with the agent's risk aversion, with the variance of output given effort, and with the gap between marginal product of effort across states. It falls to zero when effort is verifiable or when the agent is risk-neutral.

2. Formal mathematics

The welfare loss from unobservable effort. Let $\pi^{FB}$ be the principal's expected profit when effort is observable (first-best) and $\pi^{SB}$ when it is not (second-best). Agency cost is

$$AC = \pi^{FB} - \pi^{SB} \geq 0.$$
  1. Two components. (i) Risk premium: $w_L^{**} \neq w_H^{**}$ imposes risk; the principal must raise expected wage to keep IR binding. (ii) Effort distortion: if $AC$ is large, the principal may prefer to implement low effort, foregoing the surplus from high effort.
  2. Two-state $\sqrt{w}$ algebra: $AC = (p_H - p_L) \cdot c \cdot \big[ \text{wage spread term} \big]$. With binding IR and IC, the wage spread scales as $c / (p_H - p_L)$.
  3. $AC$ is increasing in agent risk aversion (curvature of $u$), in the gap $c(e_H) - c(e_L)$, and decreasing in the signal informativeness $p_H - p_L$.
  4. $AC$ falls to zero when (a) effort is observable, (b) the agent is risk neutral (sell the firm), or (c) the signal perfectly reveals effort.

3. Graphical illustration

The pink bar is the welfare loss from unobservable effort: foregone surplus plus risk premium.

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • Executive pay-for-performance literature. Estimated agency cost of unobserved CEO effort is in the order of 1 to 3 percent of firm value in S&P 500 companies.
  • Microcredit and joint-liability lending. Group-lending arrangements push the agency cost back onto borrowers via peer monitoring (cheaper signal of effort).
  • Government contracting. Cost-plus contracts have higher agency cost than fixed-price contracts; the optimal mix depends on cost uncertainty (Laffont-Tirole).
  • Essay move. Higher signal informativeness shrinks $AC$. Real-time productivity tracking, performance ranking against peers, and post-trade reporting are all attempts to raise the signal.

8 Incentive Compatibility Tier 1

1. Intuition

The agent must prefer the principal's desired action (e.g., high effort) over any deviation. IC is the constraint that turns the principal's effort-implementation problem into a constrained optimisation: a wage schedule must reward the desired action enough to overcome the cost difference.

2. Formal mathematics

The agent must weakly prefer the principal's desired effort to any other effort. In a two-effort model, this is the IC constraint:

$$\sum_i [p_i(e_H) - p_i(e_L)] u(w_i) \geq c(e_H) - c(e_L).$$
  1. The left-hand side is the difference in expected utility from the wage schedule between high and low effort. The right-hand side is the extra effort cost.
  2. For two states with high and low outcomes, IC becomes $(p_H - p_L)[u(w_H) - u(w_L)] \geq c(e_H) - c(e_L)$.
  3. IC creates a wedge: $w_H$ must exceed $w_L$ by enough to compensate the agent for the higher effort cost. The size of the wedge depends on $(p_H - p_L)$, the signal-to-effort ratio.
  4. Informativeness principle (Holmstrom 1979): any signal correlated with effort, beyond profit, should enter the optimal wage schedule. Conversely, signals uncorrelated with effort should not.

3. Graphical illustration

IC requires the wage schedule to lie below the dashed line. The wedge $c/(p_H - p_L)$ is the minimum incentive intensity.

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • Executive stock options. The strike price and vesting schedule are calibrated so that CEO effort changes option value enough to offset the effort cost.
  • Sales commissions. Per-unit commission rate plus base salary is a classic linear contract; commission rate captures incentive intensity.
  • Relative performance evaluation. Including peer firms' performance in the bonus formula filters out common shocks (a Holmstrom informativeness application).
  • Clawback clauses. Recover paid bonuses if performance is later restated; effectively sharpens IC by removing the upside reward without sustained good performance.
  • Essay move. IC is binding only when effort is unobservable; with observable effort, the principal sets a flat wage and forces $e_H$ by contract.

8 Individual Rationality Tier 1

1. Intuition

The agent must receive at least their reservation utility ($\bar u$) in expectation, otherwise they will not accept the contract. IR pins down the level of expected utility the agent gets; in the second-best optimum it binds (the principal extracts all surplus above the outside option).

2. Formal mathematics

The participation constraint. The agent will sign the contract only if expected utility under the chosen effort meets or exceeds the outside option $\bar u$:

$$\sum_i p_i(e^*) u(w_i) - c(e^*) \geq \bar u.$$
  1. $\bar u$ is the agent's reservation utility, given by their best outside option (next-best job, unemployment benefit, etc.).
  2. At the principal's optimum, IR binds: any slack means the principal could reduce wages by a small amount and still attract the agent, raising profit. So IR holds with equality.
  3. Binding IR pins down the level of expected utility offered. The principal extracts all surplus above $\bar u$.
  4. Comparative statics: a tighter labour market raises $\bar u$, shifts IR outward, and reduces principal profit. Government wage floors or unemployment benefits operate the same way.

3. Graphical illustration

IR pins down the wage at which the agent is exactly indifferent between accepting and walking away.

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • Reservation wages. Empirical labour-economics estimates put reservation wages around 80 to 90 percent of last earned wage in the first months of unemployment, rising as benefits run down.
  • Health insurance markets. If the outside option is uninsured catastrophe risk, IR is satisfied at relatively unfavourable contract terms; mandatory pooling tightens IR for low-risk types.
  • Workfare programmes. Conditional benefits raise $\bar u$ for low-skill workers, pushing employers to raise wages.
  • Essay move. Surplus extraction is the principal's reward for designing the contract. If competition forces the principal to compete for the agent (multiple principals), the IR slack accrues to the agent as rent.
  • Limitation. Real outside options depend on search frictions, information about job availability, and bargaining timing; the static IR model is a useful abstraction not an exact prediction.

8 Principal-Agent Problem Tier 1

1. Intuition

A principal (employer, owner, government) hires an agent (worker, manager, contractor) whose effort is unobservable but affects output. The principal designs a wage schedule maximising expected profit subject to the agent's participation (IR) and incentive (IC) constraints.

The first-best solution (with observable effort) pays a flat wage and forces the efficient effort. With unobservable effort, the second-best balances incentives against risk imposition on the agent, generating agency cost.

2. Formal mathematics

Canonical two-state two-effort setup. Agent has $u(w) = \sqrt{w}$ and effort costs $c(e_L) = 0$, $c(e_H) = c$. Probabilities of the high outcome are $p_H$ under high effort and $p_L < p_H$ under low effort.

The principal implementing high effort solves:

$$\min_{w_L, w_H} p_H w_H + (1 - p_H) w_L \quad \text{s.t.}\; \text{IR}, \text{IC}.$$
  1. IR: $p_H \sqrt{w_H} + (1 - p_H) \sqrt{w_L} - c \geq \bar u$.
  2. IC: $(p_H - p_L)(\sqrt{w_H} - \sqrt{w_L}) \geq c$.
  3. Both bind at the optimum. Let $u_H = \sqrt{w_H}, u_L = \sqrt{w_L}$. Then $u_H - u_L = c / (p_H - p_L)$ and $p_H u_H + (1 - p_H) u_L = \bar u + c$.
  4. Solve linearly: $u_L = \bar u + c - p_H \cdot c / (p_H - p_L)$ and $u_H = u_L + c / (p_H - p_L)$.
  5. Wages: $w_L^{**} = u_L^2, w_H^{**} = u_H^2$.

The principal compares the expected profit under high effort (with these wages) against the first-best low-effort profit (flat wage $\bar u^2$) to decide which effort to implement.

3. Graphical illustration

First-best gives a flat wage. Second-best spreads wages around the 45-degree line; the gap is the risk imposed on the agent.

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • CEO pay. Stock options align CEO incentives with shareholder returns but expose CEOs to market risk they cannot diversify (firm-specific human capital plus equity grant).
  • Sharecropping. Landlord-tenant arrangements split harvest 50-50 to balance risk-sharing with effort incentives.
  • Franchise contracts. Franchisor charges franchisee a fixed fee plus royalty rate; the royalty captures incentive intensity.
  • Essay move. Agency cost rises with risk aversion ($r$) and output noise ($\sigma^2$). Low-noise output measures shrink the second-best wage spread.
  • Essay move. When the principal can monitor effort cheaply (modern productivity-tracking tools), the second-best collapses toward first-best.

8 Moral Hazard Tier 2

1. Intuition

After a contract is signed, the agent takes an action (effort) that the principal cannot observe but that affects the joint payoff. The agent's incentive to exert effort depends on the wage schedule, generating a trade-off between insurance and incentives.

The canonical example is insurance: insured drivers may drive less carefully because the insurer bears the cost of accidents. Solutions include partial coverage, deductibles, and performance-contingent contracts.

2. Formal mathematics

Risk-averse agent, risk-neutral principal, two effort levels $e_L, e_H$ with $c(e_H) > c(e_L)$, two outcomes (high profit $\pi_H$, low profit $\pi_L$) with $\Pr(\pi_H \mid e_H) = p_H > p_L = \Pr(\pi_H \mid e_L)$.

The principal designs wages $(w_L, w_H)$ paid contingent on observed profit. The agent solves:

$$\max_{e} \; \Pr(\pi_H \mid e) u(w_H) + \Pr(\pi_L \mid e) u(w_L) - c(e).$$
  1. IR: $p_H u(w_H) + (1 - p_H) u(w_L) - c(e_H) \geq \bar u$.
  2. IC: $[p_H - p_L] [u(w_H) - u(w_L)] \geq c(e_H) - c(e_L)$.
  3. Observable effort: principal sets a flat wage $w^* = u^{-1}(\bar u + c(e_H))$, agent bears no risk, first-best is implemented.
  4. Unobservable effort: both IR and IC bind. Solving with $u(w) = \sqrt{w}$ yields a two-equation linear system in $\sqrt{w_L}, \sqrt{w_H}$ and the second-best wages satisfy $w_H > w_L$.

See Bolton and Dewatripont Ch 4, Laffont and Martimort Ch 4.

3. Graphical illustration

Second-best contract sits where IR and IC both bind. Distance from the 45-degree line measures the risk imposed on the agent.

4. Real-life examples and essay points

  • Executive compensation. Stock and option grants tie pay to firm performance to motivate effort. Critics: signal noise (CEO performance hard to attribute), risk imposed on managers.
  • Insurance deductibles. Co-pays and deductibles keep insured agents partly exposed to losses so they take care. Pure first-dollar coverage maximises moral hazard.
  • Bank bailouts (too-big-to-fail). Implicit bailout guarantees reduce bank-shareholder skin-in-the-game and incentivise excess risk-taking. Dodd-Frank and Basel III impose capital and resolution requirements to push risk-bearing back.
  • Essay move. Risk aversion is what creates agency cost. With a risk-neutral agent, selling the firm to the agent implements first-best.
  • Essay move (Peltzman effect). When safety improves (seatbelts, airbags, anti-lock brakes) agents drive faster; net safety gain is smaller than the engineering effect alone.
  • See also [[Concepts/Principal-Agent Problem]], [[Concepts/Incentive Compatibility]], [[Concepts/Agency Cost]], [[Concepts/Linear Contracts]].
8 Risk vs Insurance Trade-off Tier 2

1. Intuition

Steeper wage schedules give the agent stronger incentives to exert effort but expose them to more output risk, raising the wage premium required to satisfy IR. Flatter wages insure the agent against output risk but weaken incentives. The optimal contract balances these.

The trade-off vanishes if the agent is risk neutral (sell the firm to the agent and get the first-best) or if output perfectly reveals effort (set wage on output).

8 Second-Best Contract Tier 2

8 Linear Contracts Tier 3

1. Intuition

Wage $w = t + s x$ where x is observable output, t is a base wage, and s is the slope of incentive intensity. Optimal in Holmstrom-Milgrom's CARA-normal model: $s^* = 1/(1 + r \sigma^2 c)$.

Higher agent risk aversion r, higher output noise sigma^2, or higher effort cost convexity c all reduce optimal s. The trade-off between insurance (flat wage) and incentives (steep wage) is sharpest in this model.
