Question Types: General Equilibrium and Welfare

All Core Microeconomics finals questions on general equilibrium, welfare economics and social choice from 2014 to 2025, organised by Part A short problem, Part B essay, and Part B long-answer problem. Maps to Topics 1 and 2 in the 2025 reading list.
Exam rubric: Part A is ~4 compulsory short problems worth 40 percent total. Part B has ~6 long questions of which candidates pick 2, worth 60 percent. One Part B question is mainly technical; others are essay-style or structured.

Excluded sub-topics

None identified, but verify against current Micro2025 syllabus. The 2014 to 2020 sample is biased towards Q1 (Part A) and Q10/Q11 (one Part B). Older syllabi may have included formal Arrow's impossibility proofs or core convergence in larger replica economies, which do not appear in the recent set.

Part A: short compulsory problems

The typical Part A GE question is an exchange or production economy where you compute the competitive equilibrium, check Pareto efficiency, and discuss policy. Almost every year features one. The questions split between (i) production economies with firms and households (2014, 2017, 2018, 2019) and (ii) pure exchange economies in an Edgeworth box (2015, 2020, 2022, 2024, 2025).

Sub-question Type A.1: Compute competitive equilibrium in a pure exchange economy

  • What it asks: Given endowments $\omega_A, \omega_B$ and utilities (usually Cobb-Douglas or quasi-linear), find the price ratio $p_x / p_y$ and the equilibrium allocation $(x_A^, y_A^, x_B^, y_B^)$.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Normalise: set $p_y = 1$ and write $p = p_x$. With two goods Walras' Law makes one market redundant, so only one price needs solving.
  • Write each agent's wealth: $m_A = p \omega_{Ax} + \omega_{Ay}$. Same for B.
  • Get Marshallian demands. For Cobb-Douglas $u = x^\alpha y^{1-\alpha}$ the demands are $x^ = \alpha m / p_x$ and $y^ = (1-\alpha) m / p_y$. Memorise this, it appears every year.
  • Impose market clearing for good x: $x_A^(p) + x_B^(p) = \omega_{Ax} + \omega_{Bx}$.
  • Solve for p, then plug back to get allocations. Check the y-market clears as a sanity check on Walras.
  • For quasi-linear $u = v(x) + y$ (2025 Q1), demand for x is determined by $v'(x) = p$ alone (income effect on x is zero), so $x^ = (v')^{-1}(p)$. The numeraire good y absorbs all income variation. Equilibrium x comes from $\sum x_i^(p) = \sum \omega_{ix}$.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q1, 2020 Q1, 2022 Q1, 2024 Q1, 2025 Q1.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium, Concepts/Walras Law, Concepts/Cobb-Douglas Demand, Concepts/Quasi-linear Utility, Concepts/Excess Demand.

Sub-question Type A.2: Compute competitive equilibrium in a production economy

  • What it asks: Firms use labour $L$ to produce output $Y$ via a technology like $Y = 2AL^{1/2}$ or Cobb-Douglas $Y = A K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$; households choose consumption and labour supply with utility over $c$ and leisure. Find the equilibrium real wage $w/p$, employment, and output.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Firm side. Profit max gives $p \cdot MPL = w$, so $MPL = w/p$. For $Y = 2AL^{1/2}$, $MPL = A L^{-1/2}$, giving labour demand $L^d = (A p / w)^2$ per firm.
  • Aggregate firm demand and supply. With $N$ firms, total $L^d = N (Ap/w)^2$ and total $Y^s = N \cdot 2A L^{1/2} = 2 N A^2 p / w$.
  • Household side. With log utility over $c$ and leisure $\ell$, $u = \alpha \log c + (1-\alpha) \log \ell$ and time endowment $T = 1$. Budget is $p c = w (1 - \ell) + \pi$ where $\pi$ is profit income. Cobb-Douglas gives $c^ = \alpha (w + \pi) / p$, leisure $\ell^ = (1-\alpha)(w + \pi)/w$.
  • Aggregate household demand. With $M$ households, total $c^d = M c^$, total labour supply $L^s = M (1 - \ell^)$.
  • Impose market clearing. Either labour ($L^d = L^s$) or goods ($Y^s = c^d$); Walras gives the other for free. Solve for $w/p$.
  • Compare with planner's problem. By the first welfare theorem the competitive allocation maximises a representative household's utility subject to feasibility, so it should equal the direct planner's choice in 2017 Q1.
  • Appeared in: 2014 Q1, 2017 Q1, 2018 Q1, 2019 Q1.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium, Concepts/First Welfare Theorem, Concepts/Cobb-Douglas Demand, Concepts/PPF and MRT.

Sub-question Type A.3: Verify Walras' Law and use it to skip a market

  • What it asks: Show $\sum_g p_g z_g(p) = 0$ for all $p$, where $z_g$ is aggregate excess demand for good $g$. Then conclude that if $K-1$ markets clear, the $K$th does too.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Start from individual budget constraints: $\sum_g p_g x_{ig}(p) = \sum_g p_g \omega_{ig}$ for each agent $i$. This is an identity from budget exhaustion (Walras' Law on the individual).
  • Subtract endowment from both sides: $\sum_g p_g (x_{ig} - \omega_{ig}) = 0$, so $\sum_g p_g z_{ig} = 0$ for each $i$.
  • Sum over agents: $\sum_i \sum_g p_g z_{ig} = \sum_g p_g z_g(p) = 0$. Done.
  • Application: in 2014 Q1 the question gives you goods and labour markets and asks you to verify the labour market clears using Walras given the goods market clears. Just compute aggregate value of excess demand in the cleared market and use Walras to back out the other.
  • Appeared in: 2014 Q1(e), 2022 Q1(a), 2025 Q1(a).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Walras Law, Concepts/Excess Demand.

Sub-question Type A.4: Verify Pareto efficiency of an allocation

  • What it asks: Given an allocation, prove it is (or is not) Pareto efficient. Either invoke MRS equalisation or construct a Pareto-improving reallocation.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Recall the interior PE condition for a two-good two-agent exchange economy: $MRS_A = MRS_B$ together with feasibility. With Cobb-Douglas $u_A = x_A^\alpha y_A^{1-\alpha}$, $MRS_A = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} \cdot \frac{y_A}{x_A}$.
  • Plug in the candidate allocation and check equality. For 2024 Q1 with symmetric $\alpha = 1/2$ at $(12, 12; 12, 12)$, both MRSs equal 1, so PE holds.
  • If MRSs are unequal, propose a small trade: the agent with the higher MRS for $x$ relative to $y$ buys $x$ from the other. Show both utilities rise.
  • For corner cases (one agent has zero of a good), check Kuhn-Tucker style: PE only requires that no Pareto-improving feasible deviation exists. Leontief preferences (2022 Q1) create kinks where MRS is a set, not a number, and the PE locus thickens; check feasibility along the kink.
  • For multi-good economies (2019 Q10), the condition generalises to $MRS_{xy}^A = MRS_{xy}^B$ for every pair of goods $(x, y)$. Equivalently, at the allocation, find prices $p$ such that each agent's bundle solves their own utility max at those prices (this is the second welfare theorem route).
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q1(b), 2019 Q1(b), 2019 Q10(c), 2020 Q1(a), 2022 Q1(b), 2024 Q1(a), 2025 Q1(b).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Pareto Efficiency, Concepts/Edgeworth Box, Concepts/Second Welfare Theorem.

Sub-question Type A.5: First welfare theorem (statement and use)

  • What it asks: State the first welfare theorem and link a computed competitive equilibrium to Pareto efficiency.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • State the theorem: in an economy with locally non-satiated preferences and complete markets without externalities, every competitive equilibrium allocation is Pareto efficient.
  • Prove sketch: suppose not, then there is a feasible $(x_i')$ Pareto-dominating equilibrium $(x_i^)$. By LNS, any bundle preferred to $x_i^$ must cost at least as much as $x_i^*$ at equilibrium prices, and any strictly preferred bundle must cost strictly more. Summing over agents the dominating allocation costs more than the endowment in value, contradicting feasibility.
  • Use in the question: after computing CE in part (c), state that PE follows immediately by FWT, so we do not need to verify MRS equality separately.
  • Watch the assumptions: 2017 Q1(d) asks about the effect of a wage-income tax, which breaks the link because the tax wedge introduces a distortion that violates the LNS-CE setup.
  • Appeared in: 2017 Q1(d), 2019 Q1, 2024 Q1, plus implicit in 2022 Q5 and 2023 Q5 essays.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/First Welfare Theorem, Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium, Concepts/Pareto Efficiency.

Sub-question Type A.6: Second welfare theorem and lump-sum redistribution

  • What it asks: Given a Pareto efficient target allocation $K$, find lump-sum transfers of endowments that decentralise $K$ as a competitive equilibrium, and find the supporting price.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • State the theorem: under convexity of preferences and production sets, every Pareto efficient allocation is a competitive equilibrium for some redistribution of initial endowments.
  • Find the supporting price: at $K$, compute $MRS_A(K) = MRS_B(K)$ (true because $K$ is PE). This common value is the equilibrium price ratio $p_x / p_y$.
  • Construct the transfer: find new endowments $\tilde \omega_A, \tilde \omega_B$ such that (i) $\tilde \omega_A + \tilde \omega_B = \omega_A + \omega_B$ (aggregate preserved), (ii) at price $p_x / p_y$ from step 2, agent A's Cobb-Douglas demand from wealth $p \tilde\omega_{Ax} + \tilde\omega_{Ay}$ equals $K_A$, same for B.
  • In practice for 2015 Q1(e): solve $\alpha (p \tilde\omega_{Ax} + \tilde\omega_{Ay}) / p = K_{Ax}$ for the wealth, then split this wealth between $\tilde\omega_{Ax}$ and $\tilde\omega_{Ay}$ along the budget line.
  • Easy case: transfers in one good only. 2020 Q1(b) asks for a Pareto improving transfer of $x$ only, exploiting that at the endowment $MRS_A > MRS_B$, so moving $x$ from B to A while letting them retrade lifts both.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q1(e), 2020 Q1(b), 2024 Q1(b), 2025 Q1(c).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Second Welfare Theorem, Concepts/Edgeworth Box, Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium.

Sub-question Type A.7: Social planner choices (utilitarian, Rawlsian)

  • What it asks: Pick the allocation that maximises a given social welfare function over the set of feasible allocations or over the set of allocations reachable by a specific instrument (e.g. transfers of one good).
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Utilitarian planner maximises $W = u_A + u_B$ subject to feasibility. With Cobb-Douglas $u_i = \log x_i + \log y_i$ and a transfer of good $y$ only at fixed equilibrium prices (2024 Q1(c)), the planner picks the transfer to equate weighted marginal utilities of income across agents.
  • Algorithm for utilitarian transfer: write each agent's indirect utility $v_i(p, m_i)$, then maximise $v_A(p, m_A) + v_B(p, m_B)$ subject to $m_A + m_B$ constant. FOC equates $\partial v_A / \partial m = \partial v_B / \partial m$. For Cobb-Douglas $u = \log x + \log y$ this gives equal wealth across agents, so the recipient is the poorer one.
  • Rawlsian planner maximises $\min(u_A, u_B)$. Algorithm: equalise utilities subject to feasibility. For 2025 Q1(c) with quasi-linear $u = v(x) + y$, transfers of $y$ shift utility one-for-one. Solve $v_A(x_A^) + y_A = v_B(x_B^) + y_B$ together with $y_A + y_B = \omega_{Ay} + \omega_{By}$.
  • For 2020 Q1(d) with log utility: utilitarian maximises $\log x_A + \log y_A + \log x_B + \log y_B$ subject to feasibility. FOC give $x_A = x_B$ and $y_A = y_B$ (equal split). Rawlsian also equalises utilities given symmetric utilities, so it coincides here when the planner can choose freely. Distinguish carefully which instruments are available.
  • Note: utilitarian and Rawlsian can coincide when utility functions are symmetric and the planner has full instrument freedom. They differ when utilities differ across agents or when only some transfers are allowed.
  • Appeared in: 2020 Q1(d), 2024 Q1(c), 2025 Q1(c).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Utilitarian Planner, Concepts/Rawlsian Planner, Concepts/Social Welfare Functions.

Sub-question Type A.8: Edgeworth box construction and identification of regions

  • What it asks: Draw the Edgeworth box, mark endowment $\omega$, the contract curve, the lens of Pareto-improving allocations, and identify whether labelled points are PE, Pareto-superior to $\omega$, or Pareto-inferior.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Set up the box. Width equals $\omega_{Ax} + \omega_{Bx}$, height equals $\omega_{Ay} + \omega_{By}$. Origin $O_A$ at bottom-left for A, origin $O_B$ at top-right for B (so B's quantities increase down and left).
  • Mark the endowment $\omega$. Draw the indifference curves of A and B through $\omega$. The lens between them is the set of allocations that Pareto-dominate $\omega$.
  • Contract curve: locus of $MRS_A = MRS_B$ from $O_A$ to $O_B$. For symmetric Cobb-Douglas with identical $\alpha$, this is the diagonal. For asymmetric $\alpha_A \neq \alpha_B$ it bows.
  • Classify labelled points: a point is PE iff on the contract curve. Pareto-superior to $\omega$ iff in the closure of the lens. Pareto-inferior iff $\omega$ is in the lens of the point.
  • 2015 Q1(c) twist: point $G$ is on the contract curve (PE) but not in the lens of $A$. State that PE does not imply unanimous improvement, then propose a social welfare function such as utilitarian or Rawlsian to break the tie.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q1(a)(b)(c), 2020 Q1(a), 2022 Q1(b).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Edgeworth Box, Concepts/Pareto Efficiency, Concepts/Pareto Criterion.

Sub-question Type A.9: MRS equals MRT, the production efficiency condition

  • What it asks: For a one-consumer one-firm production economy, derive and verify the condition $MRS = MRT$ at the competitive equilibrium, and draw the PPF.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Compute MRT from the PPF. For $Y = 2 L^{1/2}$ with time endowment $T = 1$, leisure $\ell = 1 - L$ and so $Y^2 / 4 + \ell = 1$, giving a PPF in $(\ell, Y)$ space. $|dY/d\ell| = 2/Y$ along the PPF.
  • Compute MRS for the consumer between leisure and output. For $u = \alpha \log Y + (1-\alpha) \log \ell$, $MRS = \frac{\alpha \ell}{(1-\alpha) Y}$.
  • Set MRS = MRT: $\frac{\alpha \ell}{(1-\alpha) Y} = 2/Y$, solve for $\ell$ and then $Y, L$.
  • Compare to CE. In CE, the firm's FOC gives $MPL = w/p$, the consumer's FOC gives $MRS = w/p$, and the firm's technology gives $MRT = 1/MPL$. So the consumer's FOC times the firm's FOC gives $MRS = MRT$, confirming FWT.
  • Draw the PPF with the equilibrium tangency point. Mark the budget line through the endowment (here, $(1, 0)$ since the household owns time and the firm) with slope equal to the negative price ratio.
  • Appeared in: 2017 Q1, 2019 Q1(a)(d).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/PPF and MRT, Concepts/First Welfare Theorem, Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium.

Sub-question Type A.10: Comparative statics on competitive equilibrium

  • What it asks: How does the equilibrium price or allocation move when an exogenous variable changes (number of firms $N$, productivity $A$, number of households $M$, endowment $\omega$, transfer $T$)?
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Write the equilibrium equation implicitly, e.g. $L^d(w/p; N, A) = L^s(w/p; M)$ for the production economy.
  • Totally differentiate: $\frac{\partial L^d}{\partial (w/p)} d(w/p) + \frac{\partial L^d}{\partial A} dA = \frac{\partial L^s}{\partial (w/p)} d(w/p)$, solve for $d(w/p)/dA$.
  • For 2014 Q1(d) with $N$ firms and $M$ households, the equilibrium $w/p$ depends on $N/M$ (ratio matters under constant returns at the aggregate). Doubling both leaves $w/p$ unchanged.
  • For 2018 Q1(d) a rise in productivity $A$ in Cobb-Douglas tech raises factor shares of revenue proportionally but the price effect distributes differently across capitalists and workers. Decompose: $d \pi / dA$ for capitalists has a direct production effect and an indirect price effect.
  • For 2024 Q1(b) a transfer of good $y$: under Cobb-Douglas with both agents consuming both goods in interior, transfers of $y$ shift wealth but the relative demand for $x$ versus $y$ within each agent is unchanged in the income ratio, so the relative price stays put. Show this by computing aggregate $x$-demand as a function of $p$ after the transfer and checking it does not move.
  • Appeared in: 2014 Q1(d), 2018 Q1(d), 2024 Q1(b).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium, Concepts/Cobb-Douglas Demand.

Sub-question Type A.11: Identify CE versus non-CE via excess demand

  • What it asks: At a candidate price $p$, compute aggregate excess demand $z(p)$. The price is a CE iff $z(p) = 0$. Identify why a non-CE price fails (excess demand or excess supply).
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Compute individual demands at $p$ from Marshallian formulas.
  • Sum to aggregate demand $D(p)$, subtract aggregate endowment to get excess demand $z(p) = D(p) - \Omega$.
  • Check sign. If $z(p) > 0$ for good $x$, there is excess demand and the auctioneer should raise $p_x$. If $z(p) < 0$, lower it.
  • For 2015 Q1(d): at $p_0$, $z = 0$; at $p_1$, $z \neq 0$ in at least one market. Identify the excess in words, e.g. "at $p_1$ A wants to sell more $x$ than B wishes to buy at this price".
  • For 2022 Q1(a) with Leontief-Cobb-Douglas: verify the monotonicity claim $\partial z / \partial p < 0$ in one good. This is a property of gross substitutes, which ensures uniqueness of CE.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q1(d), 2022 Q1(a)(b).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Excess Demand, Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium.

Sub-question Type A.12: Pareto criterion definition and critique

  • What it asks: Define the Pareto criterion as a partial ordering on allocations; discuss its strengths (minimal, uncontroversial) and weaknesses (incompleteness, silence on distribution).
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Define: allocation $A$ Pareto-dominates $B$ iff $u_i(A) \geq u_i(B)$ for all $i$ and strict for some $i$. $A$ is Pareto efficient iff nothing dominates it.
  • Strengths: requires no interpersonal utility comparisons, respects unanimity, is the weakest defensible welfare criterion.
  • Weaknesses: incomplete (cannot rank most allocation pairs), silent on distribution (any point on the contract curve is PE, including extremely unequal ones), insensitive to inequality.
  • Propose an extension like utilitarian $W = \sum u_i$, Rawlsian $W = \min u_i$, or Kaldor-Hicks (potential compensation). State the corresponding axiomatic cost: utilitarian needs cardinal utility, Rawlsian privileges the worst-off, Kaldor-Hicks ignores actual compensation.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q1(a)(c), 2020 Q1(c), plus essays 2022 Q5 and 2024 Q5.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Pareto Criterion, Concepts/Social Welfare Functions, Concepts/Kaldor-Hicks Compensation.

Part B: essay-style questions

Sub-question Type B.E.1: Two fundamental welfare theorems and policy implications

  • What it asks: State and prove (or sketch) FWT and SWT. Discuss what each one means for the design of microeconomic policy: should the state redistribute endowments and let markets clear, or intervene in markets directly?
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  • Intro thesis (~150 words): the FWT establishes the efficiency case for markets; the SWT establishes that any desired efficient distribution can be reached through lump-sum transfers, in principle separating efficiency from distribution. The practical bite is much smaller than the formal result suggests, because lump-sum transfers do not exist for endowments that are partly skill or effort, and because the assumptions fail in many real markets.
  • Define carefully: a competitive equilibrium $(p^, x^)$, Pareto efficiency, the assumptions (LNS preferences, complete markets, no externalities, convex preferences and production for SWT).
  • Prove FWT: contradiction argument from LNS, as in A.5 step 2.
  • Sketch SWT: separating hyperplane theorem on the set of preferred bundles and the aggregate production set, both convex. The separating hyperplane is the price.
  • Policy use: FWT supports laissez-faire under its assumptions. SWT supports the "tax endowments then let markets work" view. Together they suggest distribution is a political problem solvable by lump-sum transfers; the second-best Mirrleesian view comes in when transfers must depend on observed income, generating distortions.
  • Limitations: externalities (Topic 3), public goods, asymmetric information (Topics 7 and 8), monopoly power, incomplete markets, non-convexities from fixed costs or indivisibilities, behavioural failures. Each kills FWT, SWT, or both.
  • Conclusion: SWT is more politically interesting, FWT more theoretically central. Together they frame why microeconomists separate efficiency analysis from distributional analysis.
  • Appeared in: 2023 Q5 (option A).
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/First Welfare Theorem, Concepts/Second Welfare Theorem, Concepts/Pareto Efficiency, Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium.

Sub-question Type B.E.2: Models of trade and distributional implications

  • What it asks: Compare two or three models of international trade (Ricardian, Heckscher-Ohlin, monopolistic competition / new trade) and what each predicts about who gains and who loses.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  • Intro thesis (~150 words): each model isolates a different gain from trade. Ricardian: comparative advantage from technology. HO: factor endowment differences. New trade: scale economies and variety. The distributional consequences differ sharply across models, which is why the political economy of trade does not fit one box.
  • Ricardian setup: two goods, one factor (labour), constant returns. Country with comparative advantage in good $x$ exports $x$. All workers gain in real terms if specialisation is complete and trade is voluntary. Diagram: world PPF as the upper envelope of country PPFs.
  • HO setup: two goods, two factors, identical technology across countries. Stolper-Samuelson: the factor used intensively in the exported good gains in real terms, the other loses. So skilled labour in skill-abundant countries gains; unskilled labour loses. Direct distributional conflict within country.
  • New trade: monopolistic competition with love of variety, fixed costs. Gains come from larger market, lower price-cost margin, and variety. Distributional effects more diffuse but matter at firm level (productive firms expand, unproductive exit, workers reallocate).
  • Synthesis: trade raises aggregate income, but the distributional question depends on which margin dominates empirically. Autor-Dorn-Hanson on the China shock shows large concentrated losses in import-competing regions that the standard models predict only weakly.
  • Conclusion: the compensation logic from Kaldor-Hicks (essay B.E.4) connects directly here, since the question of whether the gains can compensate the losers, and whether they actually do, is central.
  • Appeared in: 2023 Q5 (option B), 2025 Q5.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Ricardian Trade Model, Concepts/PPF and MRT, Concepts/Kaldor-Hicks Compensation.

Sub-question Type B.E.3: Social choice rule design

  • What it asks: A senior tutor must aggregate tutors' rankings of students into a single ranking. Design or evaluate aggregation rules in light of Arrow-style impossibility results.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  • Intro thesis (~150 words): no rule can satisfy unrestricted domain, Pareto, IIA, and non-dictatorship simultaneously. Practical rules trade off these properties; the question becomes which trade-off the senior tutor finds least bad.
  • State Arrow's theorem: with at least three alternatives and at least two voters, no aggregation rule satisfies (i) unrestricted domain, (ii) Pareto, (iii) IIA, (iv) non-dictatorship.
  • Survey candidate rules: simple majority (fails transitivity on three or more alternatives, Condorcet cycles), Borda count (fails IIA, can be manipulated by adding alternatives), plurality (ignores intensity), approval voting (fails ordinal completeness), score voting (uses cardinal info, fails IIA).
  • Apply to tutors: how many alternatives? how many voters? if only a small number of clearly best students, plurality with runoff is reasonable. If many students of similar quality, Borda or score voting captures intensity better but is more manipulable.
  • Strategy-proofness: Gibbard-Satterthwaite says any non-dictatorial rule with three or more alternatives is manipulable. Senior tutor should expect tutors to vote strategically; pick a rule whose manipulability is empirically modest.
  • Conclusion: recommend a specific rule, justify on the senior tutor's likely preferences over (Pareto, IIA, simplicity, strategy-proofness, transparency). A common pragmatic answer is Borda count for ranking with a sanity check by simple majority on the top candidate.
  • Appeared in: 2022 Q5.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Social Welfare Functions, Concepts/Pareto Criterion.

Sub-question Type B.E.4: Kaldor-Hicks compensation principle as a policy guide

  • What it asks: Should microeconomic policy follow the Kaldor-Hicks criterion (a change is desirable if gainers could in principle compensate losers, even if they do not), and if not, what should guide policy?
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  • Intro thesis (~150 words): KH coincides with cost-benefit analysis and is a workhorse of applied welfare economics; but its silence on whether compensation actually happens makes it ethically and politically unstable. Policy should use KH as a screening tool for efficiency and a separate distributional weight for politics.
  • Define KH: change from $A$ to $B$ is a KH improvement iff the winners in $B$ could compensate the losers and still be better off than at $A$. Equivalent to $\sum \Delta CV \geq 0$ when CVs are well-defined.
  • Strengths: completes the Pareto ordering, supports CBA, transparent in money terms, separates efficiency from distribution analytically.
  • Weaknesses: (i) Scitovsky paradox: KH can be cyclic, $A$ over $B$ and $B$ over $A$. (ii) Hypothetical compensation does not help actual losers. (iii) Implicit weighting: a dollar to a millionaire counts the same as a dollar to a poor person. (iv) Path-dependent measure (CV vs EV).
  • Alternatives: actual compensation (rare and politically costly), weighted CBA (distributional weights, used by HMT Green Book), Sen's capability approach, Rawlsian maximin, utilitarian SWF, or even procedural rules ignoring outcome welfare.
  • Conclusion: KH is a useful first cut; serious policy needs explicit distributional weights or transfer instruments alongside it. The free-trade case shows the limitation, because the China shock losers were not in practice compensated.
  • Appeared in: 2024 Q5.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Kaldor-Hicks Compensation, Concepts/Pareto Criterion, Concepts/Social Welfare Functions.

Sub-question Type B.E.5: Free trade under unanimous consent

  • What it asks: Under what conditions does free trade make everyone better off in a country that requires consensus? Is such adoption likely?
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  • Intro thesis (~150 words): free trade raises aggregate real income (Ricardian and HO both predict this) but creates losers as well as gainers within country. Unanimous consent requires either no losers or credible compensation. The first is rare except in special cases; the second is hard to commit to.
  • No-loser cases: small open economy with one factor (pure Ricardian), so the wage rises uniformly. Or autarky already at world prices, so trade has no effect. Or trade in goods that no domestic factor produced (variety gains only).
  • General case requires compensation: SWT and FWT together imply that any Pareto-efficient distribution can be reached by lump-sum transfers, so if the post-trade aggregate is larger than autarky and transfers are feasible, unanimous improvement is reachable.
  • Practical adoption likely?: lump-sum transfers are not available; redistribution comes through taxes that distort behaviour. Losers also discount future transfer promises if the political process is uncertain. Hence consensus is hard to achieve in practice, even when the KH gains are large.
  • Sketch a model: Ricardian world with two goods, two countries, symmetric L. Show that with quasi-linear preferences over an outside numeraire good plus a tax-financed lump-sum transfer, free trade Pareto dominates autarky. Then show that with realistic distortionary taxes the result can fail.
  • Conclusion: free trade with redistribution is theoretically a unanimous improvement; without redistribution it usually creates losers. The empirical record (China shock) suggests we should be sceptical that the redistribution actually arrives.
  • Appeared in: 2025 Q5.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Ricardian Trade Model, Concepts/Second Welfare Theorem, Concepts/Kaldor-Hicks Compensation, Concepts/Pareto Criterion.

Part B: long-answer problems

Sub-question Type B.L.1: Two-sector Ricardian trade with PPF and gains from trade

  • What it asks: Two countries (Home and Foreign) produce two goods using a single factor (labour) under linear technology. Derive PPFs, find autarky and trade equilibria, compute gains from trade, and do comparative statics on the foreign endowment.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Derive PPF and MRT. In each country with labour $L$ and unit labour requirements $a_x, a_y$, the PPF is the line $a_x X + a_y Y = L$. $MRT = a_x / a_y$, constant.
  • Derive MRS for Cobb-Douglas preferences $u = X^\alpha Y^{1-\alpha}$. $MRS = \frac{\alpha Y}{(1-\alpha) X}$.
  • Autarky CE: with one representative consumer-producer per country, set $MRS = MRT$ to find autarky allocation $(X^a, Y^a)$ and price $p^a = a_x / a_y$.
  • World price under trade: with symmetric labour endowments and symmetric productivity, $p^w = 1$ by symmetry. With asymmetry, $p^w$ lies between the two autarky prices.
  • Pattern of trade: each country exports the good in which it has comparative advantage (lower autarky price relative to world price).
  • Gains from trade diagram: draw the country's PPF, the autarky indifference curve tangent at $(X^a, Y^a)$, and the trade budget line through the production point at world price $p^w$. The trade indifference curve lies strictly outside the autarky one, with the area between representing gains from trade.
  • Comparative statics: if Foreign $L^*$ rises, Foreign supply of its export good rises, pushing the world relative price away from autarky-Foreign towards Home. Home's terms of trade improve, Home real income rises. Use the trade triangle to verify.
  • Appeared in: 2015 Q11.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Ricardian Trade Model, Concepts/PPF and MRT, Concepts/Cobb-Douglas Demand, Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium.

Sub-question Type B.L.2: Multi-good exchange economy with N traders

  • What it asks: Three goods, three types of traders with Cobb-Douglas preferences over the goods, asymmetric endowments. Compute individual demands, aggregate excess demands, CE prices, check Walras, then check PE of a candidate allocation.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Individual demands. For Cobb-Douglas $u_i = \sum_g \alpha_{ig} \log x_{ig}$ with $\sum_g \alpha_{ig} = 1$, the demand is $x_{ig}^* = \alpha_{ig} m_i / p_g$ where $m_i = \sum_g p_g \omega_{ig}$.
  • Will all types trade? Compare each agent's endowment-MRS to the others'. If $MRS_{xy}^i \neq MRS_{xy}^j$ at the endowment, there is a Pareto-improving trade between $i$ and $j$. Confirm gains from trade exist.
  • Excess demand for each good: $z_g(p) = \sum_i (x_{ig}^*(p) - \omega_{ig}) = \sum_i (\alpha_{ig} m_i / p_g - \omega_{ig})$.
  • Normalise $p_3 = 1$. Two market-clearing equations $z_1(p_1, p_2) = 0$ and $z_2(p_1, p_2) = 0$ give two equations in two unknowns. Solve. With Cobb-Douglas, prices come out cleanly: $p_g = \sum_i \alpha_{ig} m_i / \sum_i \omega_{ig}$ which becomes a fixed-point in $m_i$.
  • Walras check: verify $\sum_g p_g z_g(p) = 0$ identically. If you solved two markets to zero, the third must clear automatically.
  • PE check of a new allocation $K$. Compute $MRS_{xy}^i$ for each agent at $K_i$. PE iff all three MRSs equal across agents for every pair of goods. If yes, find the supporting prices (the common MRS values). If no, identify a Pareto-improving trade between two specific agents.
  • Appeared in: 2019 Q10.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Competitive Equilibrium, Concepts/Cobb-Douglas Demand, Concepts/Walras Law, Concepts/Excess Demand, Concepts/Pareto Efficiency, Concepts/Second Welfare Theorem.

How to recognise the format on the day

A Part A short problem in GE/Welfare almost always opens with a small economy described in 4 to 6 lines, then asks sub-parts (a) to (e) with verbs like "compute", "show", "find", "verify", "draw". Each sub-part is worth a few marks, the total is around 10 marks, and a competent answer is two to three pages of working. Expect numeric answers and clearly stated economic conditions (MRS = MRT, price ratio, market clearing).

A Part B essay uses verbs like "discuss", "evaluate", "assess", "should". One short paragraph sets up a claim or policy question; the rest of the prompt is a single open invitation. No diagrams are demanded but one or two well-chosen diagrams strengthen the answer. Expect 4 to 6 pages of writing with a few formal results inserted as scaffolding rather than as the main content.

A Part B long-answer problem looks like a stretched Part A: a longer setup with more agents, more goods, more parameters, and sub-parts (a) to (f) or (g) where later parts build on earlier ones. The verbs are still "compute", "derive", "find", but the algebra is heavier and the diagram requirements often explicit ("sketch the PPF", "draw the trade triangle"). Expect 5 to 8 pages and worry less about narrative, more about correctness and clear labelling.
