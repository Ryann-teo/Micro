Question Types: Game Theory and Industrial Organisation

All Core Microeconomics finals questions on game theory, oligopoly, and competition policy from 2014 to 2025, organised by Part A short problem, Part B essay, and Part B long-answer problem. Maps to Topics 4 and 5 in the 2025 reading list.
Applied / Competition Policy questions appear only in Part B per the rubric.

Excluded sub-topics

  • Evolutionary stability (ESS) is not in the Core syllabus.
  • Auction theory beyond a passing mention sits in the optional Micro paper, not Core.
  • Bayesian games / games of incomplete information are not examined in Part A or Part B for Core. Asymmetric information shows up only via the Adverse Selection and Moral Hazard topics, which have their own Question Types file.
  • Tacit collusion in finitely repeated games beyond the standard backward-induction unravelling is not directly examined, except as part of the standard "why finite horizon breaks cooperation" argument.

Part A: short compulsory problems

Sub-question Type A.1: Find all pure-strategy Nash equilibria of a 2x2 game (and identify dominance relations)

  • What it asks: Given a 2x2 (or occasionally 2x3) bimatrix, list every pure-strategy NE. Often paired with a request to check for dominant strategies, iteratively eliminate dominated strategies, or comment on weak vs strict dominance.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Write the bimatrix in standard form, Row payoff first then Column payoff.
  • Check dominance first. For each player, ask: is there a strategy whose payoff is strictly higher than another strategy's payoff for every choice by the opponent? If yes, eliminate the strictly dominated strategy. Repeat. Iterated strict dominance preserves the NE set.
  • Best-response method for any leftover game. For each Column strategy, underline Row's highest payoff. For each Row strategy, underline Column's highest payoff. A cell with both payoffs underlined is a pure-strategy NE.
  • State each pure NE as a strategy profile and quote both players' equilibrium payoffs.
  • If asked about weak dominance: note that weak dominance can eliminate Nash equilibria, so iterated weak dominance is order-dependent and is a less safe solution concept.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2014 Q3(a) (Arms race 2x2, two pure NE plus one mixed)
  • 2018 Q2(a) (parametrised 2x2 at $t=2$, pure NE via dominance)
  • 2018 Q2(c) (at $t=3$, weak-dominance discussion)
  • 2019 Q2(a)(b) (dominance vs NE across two 2x2 coordination games)
  • 2020 Q2(a) (Coaching arms race 2x2, pure and mixed NE)
  • 2022 Q2(a) (Symmetric 2x2 with $a, b, c, d$, NE under different parameter rankings)
  • 2023 Q10(b) (eliminate dominated strategy R from a 2x3)
  • 2024 Q3(a) (2x2 parametrised by $x \in (0,1)$)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Nash Equilibrium, Concepts/Dominance, Concepts/Best Response Functions.

Sub-question Type A.2: Find the mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium of a 2x2 game

  • What it asks: Compute the mixed NE when no pure NE exists, or when the question asks for "all" NE. Often paired with computing expected equilibrium payoffs.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Let Row play Top with probability $p$, Bottom with probability $1-p$. Let Column play Left with probability $q$, Right with probability $1-q$.
  • Indifference principle: in any totally mixed NE, each player must be indifferent between the strategies in their support. So I set $u_{\text{Row}}(\text{Top} \mid q) = u_{\text{Row}}(\text{Bottom} \mid q)$ and solve for $q$. Then $u_{\text{Col}}(\text{Left} \mid p) = u_{\text{Col}}(\text{Right} \mid p)$ and solve for $p$.
  • Key intuition to write down: player 1's mixing probability is determined by making player 2 indifferent, not by making player 1 indifferent. This is the most common mistake.
  • Substitute the equilibrium probabilities back into either pure-strategy payoff to get each player's expected equilibrium payoff. Both expressions must give the same number (a sanity check).
  • If the question asks "find all NE", list pure NEs from A.1 first and then add the mixed NE.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2014 Q3(a) (Arms race, mixed NE in addition to two pure NE)
  • 2018 Q2(b) ($t=4$, mixed NE)
  • 2020 Q2(a) (Coaching arms race mixed NE)
  • 2022 Q2(a) (mixed NE in the $a>c, b
  • 2023 Q10(c)(d) (mixed NE at $x=0$ and at $x=10$)
  • 2024 Q3(a) (mixed NE as a function of $x$)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Mixed Strategy Equilibrium, Concepts/Nash Equilibrium, Concepts/Best Response Functions.

Sub-question Type A.3: Define Nash equilibrium formally and discuss its predictive content

  • What it asks: A bookwork prompt embedded in a longer problem. Sometimes paired with: when does NE coincide with dominance solvability, when does dominance solvability fail to pick a NE, why is NE useful for prediction.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • State the formal definition: a strategy profile $(\sigma_1^, \ldots, \sigma_n^)$ is a Nash equilibrium if for every player $i$ and every alternative strategy $\sigma_i$, $u_i(\sigma_i^, \sigma_{-i}^) \ge u_i(\sigma_i, \sigma_{-i}^*)$.
  • Restate in words: no player can strictly gain by unilaterally deviating, holding others' strategies fixed.
  • Note that strictly dominant-strategy equilibrium implies NE, but not the converse. Iterated strict dominance preserves NE; weak dominance does not.
  • For "useful for prediction" discussion: NE is necessary for any self-enforcing rational play, but coordination games show NE need not be unique, so it is not always sufficient for sharp prediction. Refinements (SPE, trembling-hand, risk-dominance) help in specific contexts.
  • If asked when dominance suffices: dominance-solvable games (like Prisoner's Dilemma) yield a unique prediction without invoking NE refinements.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2018 Q2 (preamble asks for the NE definition)
  • 2019 Q2 (dominance vs NE relationships and predictive usefulness)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Nash Equilibrium, Concepts/Dominance.

Sub-question Type A.4: Solve homogeneous-product Bertrand duopoly (price equals marginal cost)

  • What it asks: Two firms set prices simultaneously for an identical good, marginal cost $c$. Show the unique pure-strategy NE has $p_1 = p_2 = c$. Often a stepping stone to a discussion of why this "Bertrand paradox" is unrealistic and what extensions restore positive margins.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • State the demand-allocation rule: all consumers buy from the lower-priced firm, demand is split equally if $p_1 = p_2$.
  • Argue $p_i < c$ is dominated (negative profit if any positive sales).
  • Argue $p_i > c$ with $p_j \ge p_i$ is not best response: firm $j$ can undercut by $\epsilon$ and capture the entire market at a still-positive margin.
  • The only profile with no profitable deviation is $p_1 = p_2 = c$, both earning zero profit.
  • State the policy implication: two firms suffice to mimic a perfectly competitive price.
  • Note the assumptions that drive the result: identical product, unlimited capacity, perfect information, no search costs, one-shot interaction. Relaxing any one restores positive margins.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2016 Q1(a) (describe Bertrand duopoly with $p = \text{MC}$ result)
  • 2017 Q2 (identical-product Bertrand as opening setup for Williamson trade-off)
  • 2025 Q3(a)(b) (check pA in {4, 5, 5.5, 6} as best response to $p_B = 5$, derive unique pure NE)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Bertrand Duopoly, Concepts/Best Response Functions, Concepts/Nash Equilibrium.

Sub-question Type A.5: Solve differentiated Bertrand for the symmetric equilibrium

  • What it asks: Linear demand of the form $q_i = \alpha - \beta p_i + \gamma p_j$, marginal cost $c$. Derive best-response functions and find the symmetric NE $(p^, p^)$. Often paired with: comparative statics in $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $c$, and a merger-to-monopoly comparison.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Write firm $i$'s profit $\pi_i = (p_i - c)(\alpha - \beta p_i + \gamma p_j)$.
  • FOC with respect to $p_i$: $\alpha - 2\beta p_i + \gamma p_j + \beta c = 0$, giving the best response $p_i(p_j) = \frac{\alpha + \beta c + \gamma p_j}{2\beta}$.
  • Impose symmetry $p_i = p_j = p^$ and solve: $p^ = \frac{\alpha + \beta c}{2\beta - \gamma}$.
  • Check that $\gamma < 2\beta$ for an interior solution and for stability of the best-response system.
  • Comparative statics: $p^$ rises in $\alpha$ (demand size), in $c$ (cost passthrough), and in $\gamma$ (less substitutability via cross-price effect). $p^$ falls in $\beta$ (own-price elasticity dominates).
  • Merger to monopoly: after merger the firm internalises the cross-price effect. Set common $p$ to maximise total profit $2(p-c)(\alpha - (\beta - \gamma) p)$. Monopoly price $p^M = \frac{\alpha + (\beta - \gamma) c}{2(\beta - \gamma)}$. Compare with $p^$: $p^M > p^$ unless costs fall.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2015 Q2(b)(c)(d) (symmetric Bertrand-Nash, comparative statics, merger to monopoly)
  • 2016 Q1(b) (differentiated products as the modification giving positive profits)
  • 2023 Q2(b)(c) (best responses, symmetric NE $p_a = p_b = 8$, post-merger monopoly with cost reduction)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Differentiated Bertrand, Concepts/Best Response Functions, Concepts/Merger Analysis.

Sub-question Type A.6: Cournot duopoly best-response and symmetric equilibrium

  • What it asks: Linear inverse demand $p = a - b(q_1 + q_2)$, constant marginal cost $c$. Derive each firm's best-response and the symmetric NE quantities, price, and profits.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Profit $\pi_i = (a - b(q_i + q_j))q_i - c q_i$.
  • FOC: $a - 2 b q_i - b q_j - c = 0$, best response $q_i(q_j) = \frac{a - c}{2b} - \frac{q_j}{2}$.
  • Symmetric NE $q_1 = q_2 = q^$: $q^ = \frac{a - c}{3 b}$.
  • Total output $Q^ = \frac{2(a-c)}{3b}$, equilibrium price $p^ = \frac{a + 2 c}{3}$, per-firm profit $\pi^* = \frac{(a-c)^2}{9 b}$.
  • Compare with monopoly ($q^M = \frac{a-c}{2b}$, $p^M = \frac{a+c}{2}$, $\pi^M = \frac{(a-c)^2}{4 b}$) and perfect competition ($p = c$). Cournot price sits strictly between the two.
  • If costs are asymmetric ($c_1 \ne c_2$), redo the FOCs separately. The lower-cost firm produces more.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2016 Q10(a) (symmetric Cournot NE with shared cost)
  • 2020 Q10(a) (Cournot NE, price, profits)
  • 2025 Q7 essay (Cournot is the canonical model for showing entry reduces price)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Cournot Duopoly, Concepts/Best Response Functions, Concepts/Nash Equilibrium.

Sub-question Type A.7: Characterise the SPE of a finite sequential game by backward induction

  • What it asks: Either an extensive-form tree or a simultaneous game played sequentially. Find the SPE strategy profile and the equilibrium path.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Identify the proper subgames, starting from the smallest (latest-move) subgames at the leaves.
  • In each smallest subgame, solve for the optimal action by the player who moves. Record this as that player's SPE behaviour at the corresponding information set.
  • Replace the subgame with the resulting payoff vector and move one stage up. Repeat.
  • After folding the tree all the way back to the root, the equilibrium path is the sequence of optimal actions traced from the root.
  • The SPE specifies actions at every node, including off-path nodes. This is the difference from a NE of the reduced strategic form, where some off-path threats may not be credible.
  • Compare with the NE of the simultaneous-move version: a sequential game's SPE is a NE of the strategic form, but not every NE of the strategic form is an SPE (some rely on incredible threats).
  • Appeared in:
  • 2022 Q2(b) (sequential SPNE under $b > d > c > a$)
  • 2024 Q3(b)(c) (does Row or Column prefer to move first; can both be worse off moving first under a unique NE)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Subgame Perfect Equilibrium, Concepts/Backward Induction, Concepts/Nash Equilibrium.

Sub-question Type A.8: Define SPE of a repeated game and sustain cooperation via grim trigger

  • What it asks: For an infinitely repeated stage game, find the critical discount factor $\delta^*$ such that the grim-trigger strategy supports the cooperative outcome as an SPE. Often phrased as "show that (Coop, Coop) is sustainable for $\delta$ large enough".
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • State the grim-trigger strategy: play the cooperative action in every period unless any deviation has occurred in any previous period; if any deviation has occurred, play the static-NE (punishment) action forever.
  • Cooperation payoff stream: $V^C = \frac{u^C}{1 - \delta}$ where $u^C$ is the per-period cooperative payoff.
  • Deviation payoff stream: deviate optimally today and earn $u^D$, then punished at static-NE payoff $u^N$ forever: $V^D = u^D + \frac{\delta u^N}{1 - \delta}$.
  • No-deviation condition: $V^C \ge V^D$. Rearrange to $\delta \ge \frac{u^D - u^C}{u^D - u^N} \equiv \delta^*$.
  • State $\delta^*$ explicitly and interpret: cooperation is sustainable when the per-period gain from deviation is small relative to the long-run loss of punishment.
  • SPE check: the punishment phase prescribes the static NE, which is a NE of the stage game, so playing it forever is itself an SPE of the continuation. This makes the grim-trigger strategy itself an SPE, not merely a NE.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2014 Q3(c)(d) (SPE of repeated arms race, sustaining (No, No) using MSE as punishment)
  • 2020 Q2(b)(c) (define SPE of repeated game; sustain (M, M) for large $\delta$)
  • 2025 Q3(c) (SPE strategies sustaining the monopoly-price collusive outcome in repeated Bertrand, find critical $\delta$)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Infinitely Repeated Games, Concepts/Grim Trigger Strategy, Concepts/Critical Discount Factor, Concepts/Subgame Perfect Equilibrium.

Sub-question Type A.9: Williamson trade-off, merger from duopoly to monopoly

  • What it asks: A merger between two firms creates market power (deadweight loss) but may also create cost synergies (productive efficiency gain). Decide whether the merger raises social welfare, often with a diagram.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Draw the demand curve. Mark pre-merger price (duopoly $p^D$) and quantity $Q^D$, and pre-merger marginal cost $c^D$.
  • Mark post-merger price $p^M$ (monopoly), quantity $Q^M$, and post-merger marginal cost $c^M$ (lower because of synergies).
  • Welfare-reducing area: the deadweight loss triangle from raising price from $p^D$ to $p^M$, evaluated at the pre-merger cost.
  • Welfare-enhancing area: the rectangle of cost savings $(c^D - c^M) \cdot Q^M$.
  • Williamson trade-off: the merger raises total surplus iff the cost-saving rectangle exceeds the DWL triangle. The rectangle is first-order in the cost reduction while the triangle is second-order in the price increase, so even modest synergies often dominate.
  • Consumer surplus check: consumers gain only if the price actually falls, which requires the cost reduction to be large enough that monopoly $p^M(c^M) < p^D$. The standard Williamson logic favours producers, so the regulator must care about total surplus rather than only consumer surplus for the merger to be approved.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2015 Q2(d) (monopoly price after merger)
  • 2017 Q2 (full Williamson trade-off prompt with diagram)
  • 2023 Q2(c) (post-merger monopoly with cost reduction, whether to allow)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Williamson Trade-off, Concepts/Merger Analysis, Concepts/Bertrand Duopoly.

Part B: essay-style questions

Sub-question Type B.E.1: Oligopoly theory plus merger essay (when should a duopoly merger be allowed?)

  • What it asks: Use oligopoly theory (Cournot, Bertrand, differentiated Bertrand, Williamson) to analyse whether a proposed two-to-one merger should be permitted, and explain how the conclusion changes if there are more than two initial firms.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  • Intro thesis: a duopoly merger generates market power that raises price and reduces consumer surplus, but it can also generate cost synergies. The regulator's decision depends on (i) the magnitude of synergies relative to deadweight loss, (ii) the standard of welfare (consumer vs total surplus), (iii) the post-merger market structure, and (iv) the threat of entry. With more than two initial firms, residual competition softens the price effect, making mergers more often welfare-improving.
  • Static benchmark in homogeneous Bertrand: duopoly already yields $p = c$, so any merger to monopoly raises price by the full monopoly-vs-competition gap. This is the worst case for consumers and only synergies large enough to push $p^M(c^M)$ below $c$ would protect them. Regulators almost always block such mergers.
  • Static benchmark in differentiated Bertrand or Cournot: duopoly profits and prices already lie above marginal cost. The price jump from merger is smaller. Use a numerical example (e.g., the 2023 Q2 setup) to compute pre- and post-merger price changes and the implied welfare numbers.
  • The Williamson trade-off formally: cost savings rectangle vs DWL triangle (cite Concepts/Williamson Trade-off). Draw the diagram and state the result: even modest synergies often suffice when starting from a high price (small triangle) and a large output (big rectangle).
  • Consumer-surplus vs total-surplus standards: UK CMA and EU DG-COMP use a consumer-welfare standard, which is harder for merging firms to satisfy. Total surplus is easier because it counts producer gains.
  • Three or more firms case: in Cournot oligopoly with $n$ firms and symmetric cost $c$, equilibrium price is $p_n = \frac{a + n c}{n + 1}$. A 2-to-1 reduction of firms (e.g., $n = 4 \to 3$) raises price by a fraction roughly $\frac{1}{(n+1)(n)}$ times the gap to monopoly. The effect shrinks rapidly in $n$. Remaining competitors discipline the merged firm.
  • Dynamic considerations: entry, repeated-game collusion, and innovation. With easy entry, even a 2-to-1 merger may be approved because the threat of entry pins price near $p^D$. With high entry barriers, collusion among the post-merger smaller set of firms becomes easier (fewer firms means easier monitoring), strengthening the regulator's case to block.
  • Conclusion: the duopoly-to-monopoly case is the hardest for the merging firms because no residual competition remains. As $n$ rises, the welfare cost shrinks and the synergies case becomes easier. Frame the conclusion as a function of $n$, of the standard of welfare, and of entry conditions.
  • Appeared in: 2022 Q7.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Williamson Trade-off, Concepts/Merger Analysis, Concepts/Cournot Duopoly, Concepts/Bertrand Duopoly, Concepts/Differentiated Bertrand, Concepts/Entry Deterrence.

Sub-question Type B.E.2: Collusion sustainability essay, with multiplicity of equilibria

  • What it asks: Use repeated-game theory to identify the factors that determine whether collusion is sustainable, and discuss whether the multiplicity of equilibria identified by the folk theorem is a problem for the theory's predictive power.
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  • Intro thesis: collusion is sustainable when each firm's short-term gain from cheating is dominated by the long-term value of cooperation, weighted by the discount factor. The folk theorem confirms that any individually rational payoff vector is sustainable for $\delta$ large enough, which is a virtue (rationalises observed collusion) and a vice (no sharp prediction). The multiplicity is partly tamed by focal points, antitrust enforcement, and equilibrium selection.
  • The grim-trigger benchmark: derive the critical $\delta^$ for the symmetric duopoly using either Bertrand or Cournot stage game (cite Concepts/Grim Trigger Strategy and Concepts/Critical Discount Factor). Present $\delta^ = \frac{u^D - u^C}{u^D - u^N}$.
  • Factors raising $\delta^*$ (making collusion harder): low patience (high real interest rate), low monitoring quality (long detection lag), large number of firms (each firm's punishment loss is smaller while deviation gain is the full monopoly payoff), demand shocks (Green-Porter style price wars when monitoring is imperfect), asymmetric firms (free-riding incentives differ).
  • Factors lowering $\delta^*$ (making collusion easier): stable market, transparent prices, frequent interaction, capacity constraints (cap the deviation profit), homogeneous products with public prices.
  • The folk theorem and multiplicity: for $\delta \to 1$, the set of SPE payoffs equals the set of feasible individually rational payoffs (cite Concepts/Folk Theorem). This means cooperation, partial cooperation, and even alternation between firms can all be sustained.
  • Is multiplicity a problem? Arguments for "yes": no point prediction, hard to falsify, hard for the regulator to identify which collusive outcome to deter. Arguments for "no": (a) the folk theorem matches the empirical reality of cartels picking focal prices (often the joint-profit-maximising one), (b) standard refinements like renegotiation-proofness or stationary symmetric equilibria cut the equilibrium set, (c) the theorem's value is rationalising collusion, not predicting its exact form.
  • Application to competition policy: the antitrust authority's task is to deter the cooperative SPE, not the punishment SPE. Leniency programmes raise the deviation payoff $u^D$ (by reducing the punishment's expected severity to a deviator who confesses), which raises $\delta^*$ and destabilises collusion. This is a concrete policy use of the theory despite the multiplicity.
  • Conclusion: multiplicity is a real cost in terms of sharp prediction but not a fatal one. The theory still pins down the comparative statics that drive collusion, and policy interventions can be evaluated by how they shift $\delta^*$ even without selecting a unique equilibrium.
  • Appeared in: 2023 Q7.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Infinitely Repeated Games, Concepts/Grim Trigger Strategy, Concepts/Critical Discount Factor, Concepts/Folk Theorem, Concepts/Subgame Perfect Equilibrium.

Sub-question Type B.E.3: Merger vs collusion, regulator's preference

  • What it asks: When do firms prefer to merge rather than collude (or vice versa), and which does the regulator prefer?
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  • Intro thesis: a merger and successful collusion can both achieve the joint-profit-maximising outcome, but they differ in commitment, stability, side-payments, and legal risk. Firms prefer merger when collusion is hard to sustain (low $\delta$, many firms, demand instability) or when synergies are large. The regulator generally prefers collusion to be unstable and is hostile to mergers that lack synergies; the regulator's choice between blocking collusion and blocking a merger turns on which is easier to detect and on whether synergies exist.
  • What collusion can achieve: joint profit maximisation as the cooperative payoff in an infinitely repeated game, sustained by grim-trigger or similar punishments above the critical $\delta^*$.
  • What merger can achieve: legal joint ownership, single-decision-maker, full internalisation of pricing externalities, possible cost synergies. No incentive-compatibility constraint on the joint pricing decision because the merged firm is a single entity.
  • Why firms prefer merger: (a) commitment (no need to satisfy $\delta \ge \delta^*$), (b) realisation of cost synergies that collusion cannot deliver, (c) escape from the legal risk of explicit collusion (cartel fines and prison terms in many jurisdictions), (d) immune to defection.
  • Why firms might prefer collusion: (a) reversibility (can exit the cartel if conditions change), (b) no merger regulatory approval needed, (c) avoids costly integration of management, (d) flexibility in profit shares.
  • Regulator's view: collusion has no efficiency gain (firms remain separate so productive efficiency is unchanged) and harms consumers. The regulator unambiguously prefers to break it. Merger has the Williamson trade-off (cite Concepts/Williamson Trade-off): potentially harmful via market power, potentially beneficial via synergies. The regulator's preference depends on synergies.
  • Implication for policy mix: leniency programmes destabilise collusion; merger control screens out mergers that lack synergies but allows pro-efficiency ones. The regulator's preference is implicit in the policy mix: kill collusion always, allow mergers conditionally.
  • Conclusion: firms' preference depends on the sustainability of collusion ($\delta$, number of firms, market stability) and on the size of merger synergies. The regulator's preference is asymmetric: collusion is condemned because it has no offsetting gain; mergers are weighed case by case.
  • Appeared in: 2024 Q7.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Merger Analysis, Concepts/Williamson Trade-off, Concepts/Infinitely Repeated Games, Concepts/Grim Trigger Strategy, Concepts/Critical Discount Factor.

Sub-question Type B.E.4: Entry, price, and whether mergers should be banned

  • What it asks: Under what conditions does entry reduce price, and should mergers be banned outright?
  • Step-by-step guide for a top essay:
  • Intro thesis: entry reduces price when residual demand for the entrant is positive at prices above marginal cost and the post-entry equilibrium is more competitive than the pre-entry one. Mergers should not be banned outright because they can deliver Williamson-style synergies, but they should be subject to case-by-case scrutiny.
  • Entry and price in Cournot oligopoly: with $n$ symmetric firms, $p_n = \frac{a + n c}{n + 1}$, monotonically falling in $n$ and converging to $c$ as $n \to \infty$. Entry strictly reduces price as long as the entrant's marginal cost is not too far above incumbents'.
  • Entry and price in Bertrand: even one entrant drives price to $c$ in homogeneous Bertrand. In differentiated Bertrand, entry expands the set of varieties and shifts each incumbent's residual demand, reducing prices but by less than in Cournot.
  • When entry does not reduce price: (a) entry deterrence by limit pricing or capacity preemption (cite Concepts/Entry Deterrence), (b) entry into a separate niche that doesn't compete with incumbents, (c) collusive accommodation where the entrant joins the cartel, (d) cost-disadvantaged entry that exits quickly.
  • Connecting to merger policy: a merger reduces $n$, so by symmetry with the entry argument it raises price (the reverse of entry). The case for blocking is the case for not undoing entry.
  • Why not ban all mergers?: (a) Williamson synergies (cite Concepts/Williamson Trade-off) can dominate the DWL triangle, (b) some mergers are pro-competitive when they create a stronger rival to a dominant incumbent (the maverick rationale), (c) mergers can enable economies of scale and R&D investment that no firm alone could finance, (d) blocking all mergers chills the market for corporate control and protects underperforming managers.
  • The case-by-case standard: regulators use HHI thresholds, market-definition tests, efficiency defences, and post-merger price-prediction models. This balances the synergies and the market-power costs.
  • Conclusion: entry typically reduces price, with exceptions tied to deterrence, accommodation, and niche entry. A blanket merger ban discards the synergies and the discipline of the market for corporate control. The right policy is conditional approval with active enforcement.
  • Appeared in: 2025 Q7.
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Cournot Duopoly, Concepts/Bertrand Duopoly, Concepts/Entry Deterrence, Concepts/Williamson Trade-off, Concepts/Merger Analysis.

Part B: long-answer problems

Sub-question Type B.L.1: Cournot duopoly with optional cost-reducing investment (2-stage game with threshold)

  • What it asks: A two-stage game where firm 1 first chooses whether to undertake a cost-reducing investment at fixed cost $K$, then both firms play Cournot. Find the SPE, the threshold $K$ above which firm 1 does not invest, and discuss the credibility of off-path play.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Stage 2 Cournot with symmetric costs (no investment): both firms have cost $c$. Apply A.6 to get $q^ = \frac{a-c}{3b}$, $\pi^ = \frac{(a-c)^2}{9 b}$. Call this $\pi^N$.
  • Stage 2 Cournot with asymmetric costs (firm 1 has lower cost $c' < c$, firm 2 still has $c$): redo the FOCs. $q_1 = \frac{a - 2 c' + c}{3 b}$, $q_2 = \frac{a - 2 c + c'}{3 b}$. Profits $\pi_1^I = b \cdot q_1^2$ and $\pi_2^I = b \cdot q_2^2$. Firm 1's profit rises (intuition: the cost-cutting firm captures more of the market and the equilibrium price drops by less than the cost reduction).
  • Stage 1 SPE: firm 1 invests iff $\pi_1^I - K \ge \pi_1^N$. Solve for the threshold $K^* = \pi_1^I - \pi_1^N$.
  • Stage 1 strategies: firm 1's strategy is "invest if $K < K^*$, do not invest otherwise". Firm 2's strategy is "play the Cournot quantity appropriate to firm 1's cost level". This is an SPE because both stage-2 continuations are NEs of the Cournot subgame at the relevant cost levels.
  • NE that fails subgame perfection: a profile where firm 2 threatens to flood the market with output if firm 1 invests (in order to deter the investment) can be a NE of the strategic form if firm 1 believes the threat. It fails SPE because firm 2's threat is not best-response in the post-investment Cournot subgame.
  • Credibility of off-path play: this is the standard SPE-vs-NE distinction. Off-path moves must themselves be NE in the continuation game. Bluff threats are not SPE-credible.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2016 Q10(a)(b)(c)(d)(e)(f) (symmetric Cournot baseline, asymmetric-cost Cournot, intuition for higher profit, SPNE threshold, NE with no investment at $K=200$, credibility discussion)
  • 2020 Q10(a)(b)(c)(d) (Cournot NE, NE if firm 1 invests, largest $K$ for SPE investment, credibility of firm 2's announcement at $K=150$)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Cournot Duopoly, Concepts/Subgame Perfect Equilibrium, Concepts/Backward Induction, Concepts/Best Response Functions, Concepts/Entry Deterrence.

Sub-question Type B.L.2: Parametrised 2x3 game with affine normalisation and repeated play

  • What it asks: A 2x3 strategic-form game whose payoffs depend on parameters $x$, $y$, $t$, $w$. Normalise the payoffs via an affine transformation, eliminate dominated rows/columns, find all NE (pure and mixed) at specific parameter values, and analyse cooperation in repeated play.
  • Step-by-step guide:
  • Affine normalisation: the NE set is invariant under transformations of the form $v_i = \alpha_i + \beta_i u_i$ with $\beta_i > 0$ applied independently to each player's payoff. Choose $\alpha_i, \beta_i$ to set, for example, each player's minimum payoff to $0$ and maximum to $1$. State explicitly why this is valid (cite Concepts/Affine Utility Normalisation): best responses depend only on the ordering and ratios of payoff differences within each player.
  • Iterated dominance: check whether one column (say R) is dominated by a mixture of the other two columns. If so, eliminate R and reduce to a 2x2.
  • Pure-strategy NE at the boundary $x = 0$: apply the A.1 best-response method to the reduced 2x2.
  • Mixed-strategy NE at $x = 0$: apply A.2 indifference principle. State the expected payoffs.
  • Mixed NE at $x = 10$ (only): redo the indifference principle with the new payoffs. Note explicitly that there is no pure NE in this case, hence "mixed only".
  • Sustaining cooperation in repeated play: identify the cooperative outcome (typically (T, M) or (B, L), whichever is Pareto-superior to the static NE), use the static-NE as the punishment, and derive the critical $\delta^*$ for grim-trigger sustenance via A.8.
  • SPE-credibility of the threat: confirm that the punishment is itself a stage-game NE so it can be played credibly forever.
  • Appeared in:
  • 2023 Q10(a)(b)(c)(d)(e) (affine normalisation, eliminate dominated R, NE at $x=0$, NE at $x=10$, sustaining cooperation with SPE threats in repeated play)
  • Relevant concepts: Concepts/Affine Utility Normalisation, Concepts/Dominance, Concepts/Nash Equilibrium, Concepts/Mixed Strategy Equilibrium, Concepts/Infinitely Repeated Games, Concepts/Grim Trigger Strategy, Concepts/Subgame Perfect Equilibrium.

How to recognise the format on the day

Part A Game Theory / IO questions are short, formula-driven, and tightly scaffolded. Cues:

  1. A small bimatrix in the question stem: expect A.1 (pure NE), A.2 (mixed NE), A.3 (NE definition). Allow about 12 to 15 minutes.
  2. The words "Bertrand" or "price competition" with identical products: expect A.4 (price equals MC). Allow 8 to 10 minutes plus a short comment on why the result is fragile.
  3. The words "differentiated" or a demand of the form $q_i = \alpha - \beta p_i + \gamma p_j$: expect A.5 plus comparative statics and possibly a merger comparison. Allow 15 minutes.
  4. The word "Cournot": expect A.6, often as Stage 2 of a multi-stage problem. Allow 10 minutes.
  5. An extensive-form tree or "sequential" wording: expect A.7 backward induction. Allow 10 to 12 minutes.
  6. Words "repeated game", "infinitely", "discount factor", or "$\delta$": expect A.8 grim trigger and the critical $\delta^*$. Allow 15 minutes.
  7. The word "merger" with a duopoly setup: expect A.9 Williamson trade-off with a welfare diagram. Allow 12 minutes.

    8. Part B Game Theory / IO essays cluster around four prompts: (i) duopoly merger analysis, (ii) collusion sustainability, (iii) merger vs collusion, (iv) entry and merger bans. The recipe is the same: open with a one-sentence thesis, build the theoretical apparatus (Cournot, Bertrand, repeated games, Williamson), then use it to answer the regulatory question. Always finish with a position. Examiners reward a clear stance backed by the formal model over hedging.

    Part B Game Theory / IO long-answer problems cluster around two formats: (i) a two-stage Cournot game with cost-reducing investment (2016 Q10, 2020 Q10), where the trick is to handle the asymmetric-cost Cournot stage carefully and then to discuss credibility of off-path threats; (ii) a 2x3 parametrised game with affine normalisation, iterated dominance, mixed NE, and repeated-game extension (2023 Q10), where the trick is to be patient with the algebra at each parameter value and to remember that affine normalisation leaves NE invariant.
